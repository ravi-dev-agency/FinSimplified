## 8.1 – What is an ETF?

An ETF is an index fund that you buy and sell on the stock exchange, like a share.

ETF stands for **Exchange Traded Fund**. The important word is the middle one.

A mutual fund is bought from the fund house. An ETF is bought from another investor, through your broker, on the exchange — the same place you would buy a share of any company.

### How it works

1. A fund house creates a fund that copies an index, say the Nifty 50
2. It buys all 50 companies in the same proportion as the index
3. It divides that portfolio into units and lists them on the exchange
4. You buy units through your broker, and they sit in your demat account
5. When the 50 companies rise, your units rise

You own a slice of all 50 companies. If you bought one unit at ₹250, you own ₹250 worth of the Nifty 50 — spread across every company in it, in the right proportion.

### A simple example

Nifty 50 is at 24,000. An ETF tracking it trades at about ₹250 a unit.

You buy 40 units for ₹10,000. You now own ₹10,000 of the Nifty 50 — a tiny piece of fifty of India's largest companies.

Six months later the Nifty is at 26,400, up 10%. Your ETF trades at about ₹275. Your 40 units are worth ₹11,000.

No fund manager chose anything. The ETF simply held what the index held.

## 8.2 – Why invest in an ETF?

Four reasons, in the order that actually matters.

**1. You get the whole market in one purchase.** ₹10,000 buys a slice of 50 companies. Buying those 50 shares individually would cost lakhs and dozens of transactions.

**2. The cost is very low.** An ETF typically charges 0.05% to 0.2% a year. An actively managed fund charges 0.5% to 1.2%. On a ₹10 lakh portfolio that is a difference of several thousand rupees every year, compounding for as long as you hold.

**3. Nobody's judgement can go wrong.** An active fund depends on a manager being right. An ETF follows a rule — if a company is in the index, the fund holds it. There is no manager to underperform, and none to leave.

**4. You can see exactly what you own.** An ETF publishes its holdings daily. You always know precisely which companies you hold and in what proportion.

### What you give up

Being honest about the trade-off:

- **You will never beat the market.** By design you get the index, minus a small cost. If a sector booms, you capture only your index weight in it
- **You fall with the market too.** A Nifty ETF fell sharply in 2020 along with everything else. There is no manager to move to cash
- **You need a demat account**, and you must place the order yourself

## 8.4 – Index fund vs ETF

Both copy the same index and hold the same companies. The difference is entirely in how you buy them.

| | Index fund | ETF |
|---|---|---|
| Where you buy | From the fund house or an app | On the stock exchange |
| What you need | KYC only | KYC **and** a demat account |
| Price you pay | The day's NAV, same for everyone | The live market price |
| When you can buy | Any time; processed at day end | Only during market hours |
| Expense ratio | 0.1% to 0.3% | Often 0.05% to 0.2% |
| Brokerage | None | On every trade |
| Bid-ask spread | None | On every trade |
| Demat charges | None | Annual maintenance |
| SIP | Automatic and easy | Clumsy, and costly |
| Minimum | ₹100 to ₹500 | The price of one unit |
| Can you overpay? | No — everyone gets the NAV | Yes, if the price is above value |

*Table 8.3 — the same portfolio, two wrappers*

### Which one should you choose?

The ETF's lower expense ratio is real, but small. Work out what it is actually worth:

An ETF might save 0.1% a year. On ₹1,00,000 that is **₹100 a year**.

If you invest monthly and pay spread plus brokerage of about ₹30 each time, that is **₹360 a year** in trading costs — to save ₹100.

![A monthly ETF purchase costs more in spread and brokerage than the expense ratio saves; a single large purchase held for years saves money](/diagrams/etf-cost.svg)

*Fig 8.1 — the cheaper wrapper is only cheaper if you trade rarely and in size. Illustrative.*

> **Do this. Monthly investing → index fund. Occasional lump sums → ETF.** If you invest a fixed amount every month, the index fund wins: no spread, no brokerage, and the SIP runs automatically. If you invest large amounts occasionally and hold for years, the ETF is genuinely cheaper.

| Choose an **index fund** if | Choose an **ETF** if |
|---|---|
| You invest monthly through a SIP | You invest large amounts occasionally |
| You have no demat account | You already have one |
| You want it automatic and forgettable | You will check the price before buying |
| It is your first investment | You want gold exposure |

*Table 8.4 — which suits you*

## 8.5 – How to select the best ETF

Six checks, in order. The first three describe the fund. The last three describe this moment — because a good fund bought at a bad price is still a bad purchase.

| # | Check | Good sign | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The index it tracks** | Broad and well known | A narrow sector or theme |
| 2 | **Tracking error / difference** | Small and consistent | Large, or jumping around |
| 3 | **Expense ratio** | Low *for that index* | High with no tracking advantage |
| 4 | **Daily traded volume** | Crores | Lakhs, or days with no trades |
| 5 | **Bid-ask spread** | A few paise | A rupee or more |
| 6 | **Price vs iNAV** | Within 0.5% | A visible premium |

*Table 8.5 — the six-point check, in order*

**Step 1 — Choose the index first, not the fund.** Decide what you want to own: the Nifty 50, a broader market index, gold. This decision matters far more than which fund house's version you pick. Five ETFs tracking the Nifty 50 all hold the same fifty companies.

**Step 2 — Then compare the funds on that index**, using checks 2 to 4 below.

**Step 3 — Then check the price** using checks 5 and 6, on the day you actually buy.

The rest of this chapter explains each of those numbers properly.

## 8.6 – NAV and iNAV

**NAV (Net Asset Value)** is everything the fund owns, minus what it owes, divided by the number of units. It is what one unit is genuinely worth.

For a mutual fund, NAV is calculated once after the market closes, and it is the price everyone pays that day.

For an ETF, the closing NAV still exists — but **you do not buy at it.** You buy at the market price, which is set by buyers and sellers.

### iNAV — the number that protects you

**iNAV** means indicative NAV. It is the NAV recalculated continuously through the trading day as the underlying shares move.

It answers one question: *what is this ETF actually worth, right now?*

Every ETF publishes iNAV through the day, and most brokers show it next to the market price. **Comparing the two before you buy is the single most useful habit in this chapter.**

| If the market price is | It is called | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Above iNAV | A **premium** | You are overpaying for the same portfolio |
| Below iNAV | A **discount** | A bargain going in, but the same gap may appear when you sell |
| Roughly equal | Fair value | What a healthy, liquid ETF looks like most of the time |

*Table 8.6 — premium and discount*

On a heavily traded Nifty ETF, price and iNAV stay within a few paise. On a thin one, or during a sharp market move, gaps of 1–3% happen.

### Why price usually stays close to value

Large institutions called **authorised participants** can go directly to the fund and swap a basket of the actual shares for a big block of new ETF units — a **creation unit** — or do the reverse.

So when an ETF drifts above its value, an authorised participant creates new units and sells them into the market. That extra supply pushes the price back down. When it drifts below, they buy cheap units and redeem them for the shares.

It is an automatic correction running all day, and it is why a well-traded ETF stays honest.

![An index fund has one NAV per day for everyone; an ETF has a live market price that can sit above or below the fund's true value](/diagrams/etf-vs-index.svg)

*Fig 8.2 — the same portfolio. One has a single daily price; the other has a live price that can drift.*

## 8.7 – When the price breaks free: the international ETF premium

This is what happens when the correction mechanism stops working, and it is the most expensive trap in ETFs.

India caps how much money can leave the country for investment, and individual funds have sub-limits inside that cap. **When a fund hits its limit it cannot accept new money — so it cannot create new units.**

1. The fund hits its overseas investment limit
2. No new units can be created, so supply is frozen
3. Demand does not stop — people still want to buy
4. Buyers bid against each other for the units that already exist
5. The price climbs above iNAV and keeps climbing, because nothing is pushing it back

![When a fund can create new units, price stays near iNAV. When creation is blocked, demand pushes the price far above the fund's true value.](/diagrams/etf-premium.svg)

*Fig 8.3 — the arbitrage that normally holds the price down stops working when creation is blocked.*

**What it costs.** Suppose the ETF trades at ₹115 while iNAV is ₹100. Put in ₹2,00,000 and you have bought about ₹1,74,000 of actual value. When limits reopen and the premium collapses, you lose roughly ₹26,000 — and the foreign index never moved.

> **Do not** buy an international ETF without checking iNAV against the market price. Premiums well into double digits have occurred in Indian international ETFs, and buyers at those levels lost money the underlying index never lost.

**If you want international exposure:** check iNAV and skip it if the premium is above about 1%. Or use a **fund of funds**, which transacts at NAV so you cannot overpay through a premium, at one extra layer of fees. Or simply wait — premiums close.

## 8.8 – Expense ratio

The yearly fee for running the fund, taken out daily. You never get a bill — the NAV you see is already after it.

On ₹1,00,000 in an ETF charging 0.1%, that is ₹100 a year, or about 27 paise a day.

| Product | Typical expense ratio |
|---|---|
| ETF | 0.05% – 0.2% |
| Index fund | 0.1% – 0.3% |
| Active equity fund (direct) | 0.5% – 1.2% |
| Active equity fund (regular) | 1.5% – 2.2% |

*Table 8.7 — what different products charge*

Lower is better — but only after you have checked tracking, which is the next section. The expense ratio is what you are *quoted*. Tracking difference is what you actually *pay*.

## 8.9 – Tracking error and tracking difference

An ETF promises to copy an index. These two numbers say how well it keeps that promise, and they are not the same thing.

**Tracking difference** is the gap between the index's return and the fund's return. If the Nifty gave 12% and the ETF gave 11.7%, the tracking difference is 0.3%. That is real money you did not receive.

**Tracking error** measures how *consistently* the fund tracks — how much that daily gap jumps around. A fund trailing by exactly 0.2% every single day has low tracking error. One that is sometimes ahead and sometimes far behind has high tracking error, even if it averages out to the same place.

| | Measures | Lower means |
|---|---|---|
| **Tracking difference** | How far behind the index you finished | You kept more of the index's return |
| **Tracking error** | How erratic the gap was | The fund copies the index reliably |

*Table 8.8 — the two tracking numbers*

### Why tracking is never perfect

- The fund holds a little cash for redemptions, which does not track the index
- It pays trading costs when the index rebalances and companies are added or removed
- Dividends arrive on different dates than the index assumes
- The expense ratio itself drags returns below the index

Small gaps are normal. Large or erratic ones mean poor management.

### What a small tracking difference actually costs

The numbers look trivial until you compound them. Take ₹1,00,000 in an index growing at 12% a year:

| | After 10 years | After 20 years |
|---|---|---|
| The index itself | ₹3,10,585 | ₹9,64,629 |
| A fund trailing by 0.15% | ₹3,06,450 | ₹9,39,117 |
| A fund trailing by 0.40% | ₹2,99,669 | ₹8,98,016 |
| **Cost of the worse tracker** | **₹6,781** | **₹41,101** |

*Table 8.9 — the same index, two funds, twenty years. Illustrative.*

![One lakh over twenty years: the index reaches 9.65 lakh, a fund trailing 0.15 percent reaches 9.39 lakh, and one trailing 0.40 percent reaches only 8.98 lakh](/diagrams/tracking-cost.svg)

*Fig 8.4 — a quarter of a percent, compounded for twenty years.*

A quarter of a percent sounds like nothing. Over twenty years it is ₹41,101 on a single lakh.

> **Do this — and most people get this backwards.** Compare **tracking difference before expense ratio**. A fund charging 0.05% that trails its index by 0.4% is worse than one charging 0.15% that trails by 0.15%. For an index-tracking product, how well it tracks matters more than what it charges.

## 8.10 – AUM and volume: the liquidity check

These two get confused constantly, and the confusion costs people money.

**AUM (Assets Under Management)** is how much money the fund holds in total.

**Daily traded volume** is how many units actually change hands on the exchange each day.

They are not the same thing.

> **Do not** judge liquidity by AUM. A ₹5,000 crore ETF held entirely by institutions who never trade is illiquid **for you**. A smaller fund with heavy daily turnover is far easier to get in and out of. Your broker shows daily volume — use that number, not the fund size.

### Why liquidity matters

Liquidity is how easily you can buy or sell without moving the price against yourself.

The trap is that low liquidity looks harmless when you buy. Putting ₹10,000 into a thin ETF works fine. The problem appears when you want to sell ₹5 lakh, in a falling market, and there are simply no buyers at a sensible price.

| Signal | Healthy | Concerning |
|---|---|---|
| Daily traded volume | In crores | In lakhs, or days with no trades |
| Bid-ask spread | A few paise on a ₹250 unit | A rupee or more |
| Price vs iNAV | Within 0.5% | A visible, persistent gap |
| Impact cost | Your order barely moves the price | Your own order moves the price |

*Table 8.10 — reading liquidity*

### The bid-ask spread

At any moment there are two prices. The **bid** is the most a buyer will pay. The **ask** is the least a seller will accept. The gap is the **spread**, and you pay it on the way in and again on the way out.

If the spread is ₹2 on a ₹250 unit, you are down 0.8% the instant you buy.

> **Careful.** The spread appears on no factsheet and in no expense ratio. An ETF advertising 0.05% a year, bought through a 1% spread, has cost you twenty years of that saving in a single transaction.

## 8.11 – The portfolio: what the ETF actually holds

Every ETF publishes its holdings daily. This is one of the genuine advantages over an active fund, where you usually see holdings only once a month.

**What to look at:**

| What | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Top 10 holdings** | In a Nifty 50 ETF the top 10 often make up more than half the fund. That is the index's concentration, not the fund's fault — but you should know it |
| **Sector weights** | Indian indices lean heavily towards financials. Check whether you are comfortable with that |
| **Number of holdings** | Should match the index. A Nifty 50 ETF holds 50 companies |
| **Cash holding** | A small amount is normal for redemptions. A large one means the fund is not fully tracking |

*Table 8.11 — reading an ETF's portfolio*

The portfolio should contain no surprises. If a "Nifty 50 ETF" holds something that is not in the Nifty 50, that is worth understanding before you buy.

## 8.12 – Things that happen while you hold an ETF

Five events that catch people by surprise. None is a reason to avoid ETFs, but you should know they exist.

### What happens to dividends

The companies inside the ETF pay dividends. Where does that money go?

In most Indian equity ETFs it is **reinvested into the fund**, which quietly raises the NAV. You do not receive cash, and there is nothing to declare on your tax return until you sell.

A few ETFs pay dividends out to unit holders instead. If yours does, that payout is taxable in the year you receive it.

> **Do this.** Check the scheme document for whether your ETF reinvests or distributes. Reinvesting is simpler and usually better — it compounds without creating a tax event.

### Index rebalancing, and why it costs you

Twice a year the index provider reviews which companies belong in the index. Some are added, some removed.

Every ETF tracking that index must trade on the same day, to match the new composition. Everyone knows in advance which shares will be bought and sold.

This is one of the quiet reasons tracking is never perfect. The fund pays trading costs, and it often transacts at prices that have already moved in anticipation. It does not appear as a fee — it shows up inside tracking difference.

You cannot avoid it. But it explains why two ETFs on the same index can have different tracking records, and why the better-managed one is worth choosing.

### Circuit limits

Exchanges set price bands. If an ETF moves too far too fast, trading in it can pause.

This matters because it tends to happen exactly when you most want to act — during a sharp fall. On a highly liquid index ETF this is rare. On a thin one it is a real risk.

> **Careful.** This is another argument for liquidity. An illiquid ETF can hit a price band on a day when the index itself is trading normally.

### If the ETF closes or merges

Occasionally a fund house closes an ETF that has not attracted enough money, or merges it into another.

You are not left stranded. You will be notified, given a window to exit, and if you do nothing your units are typically either merged into the surviving fund or redeemed at NAV and the money paid to you.

The practical annoyance is that a forced redemption is a **taxable event** — you may realise gains in a year you had not planned to.

> **Do this.** Prefer ETFs with healthy AUM *and* healthy volume. A fund with very little of either is the one most likely to be closed.

### Corporate actions

When a company in the index issues bonus shares or splits its stock, the ETF adjusts automatically. You do nothing, and your unit count does not change — the index and the fund both account for it.

This is one of the genuine conveniences of an ETF over holding fifty shares yourself.

## 8.13 – Currency risk in international ETFs

If you buy an ETF tracking a US index, you are making **two** bets at once, not one.

1. That the US index rises
2. That the rupee behaves in your favour against the dollar

The second one is invisible on the fund page and it is roughly half the story.

| What happens | Your actual return |
|---|---|
| US index +10%, rupee weakens 5% against the dollar | About **+15.5%** — the weaker rupee helps you |
| US index +10%, rupee strengthens 6% | About **+3.4%** — most of the gain is eaten |
| US index flat, rupee weakens 5% | About **+5%** — a gain from currency alone |

*Table 8.12 — the same index, three different outcomes. Illustrative.*

![A US index rising ten percent gives plus 15.5 percent if the rupee weakens, but only plus 3.4 percent if it strengthens](/diagrams/currency-risk.svg)

*Fig 8.5 — currency is roughly half the story in an unhedged international ETF.*

A weakening rupee helps an Indian investor holding foreign assets, because each dollar converts to more rupees. A strengthening rupee hurts.

> **Careful.** Most Indian international ETFs are **unhedged**, meaning currency movement passes straight through to you. That is not automatically bad — over long periods a weakening rupee has often helped Indian investors in foreign assets — but you should know you are taking the bet.

This is separate from, and additional to, the premium problem in section 12.7. An international ETF can hurt you through the premium, through currency, or both.

## 8.14 – How to actually place the order

You need a demat and trading account. Then four rules.

**1. Always use a limit order. Never a market order.** A market order says "buy at whatever price exists". On a thin ETF that can fill far above where you expected. A limit order says "buy at ₹248 or better" — and if nobody sells at that price, nothing happens, which is the correct outcome.

**2. Avoid the first and last fifteen minutes.** Spreads are widest at the open, when prices are still settling, and near the close. Mid-session is calmest.

**3. Avoid days when the index is moving violently.** Gaps between price and iNAV widen exactly when markets are turbulent.

**4. Check iNAV every single time.** Five seconds, and it is the only thing standing between you and a premium.

> **Do not** place a market order on an illiquid ETF. It is the most common way beginners lose money on ETFs — not through the market falling, but through paying 2% over fair value on the way in.

## 8.15 – Types of ETFs in India

| Type | What it tracks | Worth knowing |
|---|---|---|

| **Gold** | The gold price | Backed by physical gold held by the fund. The clearest ETF win |
| **Silver** | The silver price | Newer, and thinner trading than gold |

## 8.16 – Tax on ETFs

Tax depends on **what the ETF holds**, not on the fact that it is an ETF.

| ETF type | Taxed as |
|---|---|
| Equity ETF (65%+ Indian equity) | Equity — 20% under 12 months; 12.5% above ₹1.25 lakh a year over 12 months |
| Gold, silver, international, debt | Check the current rule for that year — these have changed more than once recently |

*Table 8.15 — tax follows the holding*

Two practical points. **There is no exit load on an ETF**, because you sell to another investor rather than back to the fund. And **every sale is a taxable event**, so frequent trading creates tax work even on small amounts.

## 8.18 – Questions people actually ask

#### What is the difference between NAV and iNAV?

NAV is calculated once after the market closes. iNAV is the same idea updated continuously through the trading day, so you can see what the ETF is worth at the moment you are buying. iNAV is the one that protects you.

#### Why is the ETF price different from the NAV?

Because the price is set by buyers and sellers, not by the fund. Small gaps are normal. Large gaps mean poor liquidity, a stressed market, or blocked unit creation — all reasons to wait.

#### Why do international ETFs trade above their value?

When Indian overseas investment limits fill up, the fund cannot create new units. Supply is frozen while demand continues, so the price rises above iNAV. The premium collapses when limits reopen.

#### What is tracking error, in plain words?

How faithfully the fund copies its index. Tracking *difference* is how far behind it finished; tracking *error* is how much that gap jumped around. For an index product, both matter more than the fee.

#### Do I really need a demat account?

Yes, for an ETF. That is the main practical barrier. If you do not want one, an index fund holds the same portfolio and needs only KYC.

#### Is an ETF cheaper than an index fund?

In expense ratio, usually yes. In total cost, only if you trade rarely and in size. Add brokerage, spread and demat charges before deciding.

#### Can I do a SIP in an ETF?

Some brokers offer it, but each instalment is a separate trade with its own brokerage and spread. For monthly investing an index fund is simpler and usually cheaper.

#### How much money do I need to start?

The price of one unit, often a few hundred rupees. But factor in brokerage — investing ₹500 at a time makes the trading cost a large share of the investment.

#### Are gold ETFs safe?

The gold is held by the fund and audited. You avoid making charges, purity doubts and storage risk. The gold *price* can still fall — that risk is unchanged.

#### What happens if nobody wants to buy when I sell?

On a liquid ETF this does not realistically happen. On an illiquid one you may have to accept a lower price. This is why daily traded volume matters more than fund size.

#### Is an ETF riskier than an index fund?

The portfolio carries identical market risk. The ETF adds *execution* risk — the chance of paying a poor price. That risk is entirely under your control, through limit orders and an iNAV check.

#### Should my first investment be an ETF?

Usually not. Start with an index fund and a SIP, get comfortable, and add ETFs when you have a reason — typically a lump sum to invest, or gold.

## Key takeaways

- An ETF is an index fund you buy on the stock exchange. Same portfolio as an index fund, different wrapper.
- The main advantages are a very low expense ratio, instant diversification, daily transparency and no manager risk.
- **Monthly investing → index fund. Occasional lump sums → ETF.** The 0.1% fee saving is worth ₹100 a year on ₹1,00,000, and twelve trades cost more than that.
- **iNAV is the number that protects you.** It is the live value of the fund. Check the market price against it before every order.
- Judge a fund on **tracking difference first, expense ratio second**. A cheap fund that tracks badly costs you more.
- Judge liquidity by **daily traded volume**, never by AUM.
- Always use a limit order. A careless market order on a thin ETF can cost more than a decade of expense ratio savings.
- International ETFs can trade far above fair value when overseas limits block new unit creation.
