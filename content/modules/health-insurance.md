## 4.1 – Two men, one hospital, one bill

Anil and Vikram are both 34. Both live in Hyderabad. Both are married with one child.

Both of them bought health insurance.

In March, both are admitted to the same hospital for the same surgery. Both stay five days. Both bills come to **₹4,50,000**.

Vikram pays **₹0**.

Anil pays **₹2,70,000**.

Both men were insured. The difference was four lines in their policy documents — four lines neither of them read at the time of buying.

This chapter is about those four lines, and everything around them.

> **What this chapter is**
>
> About 25 minutes of reading, for a complete beginner. By the end you will know what the words in a policy mean, how to eliminate bad policies in half an hour, and what to do when you actually claim.

## 4.2 – What health insurance actually is

Health insurance is one sentence:

**You pay a small amount every year. The insurance company pays your hospital bill if you get admitted.**

That is the entire product. Everything else — the sixty-page document, the difficult words — is detail about *when* they pay and *how much* they pay.

### Why would a company pay your ₹4 lakh bill?

It feels like the company must be losing money. It isn't.

Picture a village with 1,000 families. Every family puts ₹12,000 a year into one common box. The box now holds ₹1.2 crore.

That year, maybe 30 families face a serious hospitalisation. Their bills come to around ₹90 lakh, paid out of the box. The rest covers the company's costs and profit.

**Nobody knows in advance which 30 families it will be.** That is the whole idea. Everybody pays a little, so the unlucky few are not destroyed.

![A thousand families each pay twelve thousand rupees into a common pool of 1.2 crore; thirty of them draw three to ten lakh each](/diagrams/risk-pool.svg)

*Fig 4.1 — how risk pooling works. Figures simplified to explain the idea.*

### Why saving the money instead does not work

The most common objection: "Instead of paying ₹13,000 every year, I will save it."

Save ₹13,000 a year and after five years you have ₹65,000.

Now look at what hospitals cost. Medical costs in India rise far faster than everyday prices. At around 13% a year, hospital bills roughly double every five to six years.

| A treatment costing this today | In 5 years | In 10 years |
|---|---|---|
| ₹2,00,000 | ₹3,68,000 | ₹6,78,000 |
| ₹5,00,000 | ₹9,21,000 | ₹16,96,000 |
| ₹10,00,000 | ₹18,42,000 | ₹33,94,000 |

*Table 4.1 — medical inflation at 13% a year*

**Your savings grow slowly and in a straight line. A hospital bill arrives all at once, at a time you did not choose.** You could save carefully for fifteen years and still not have enough for one serious illness.

Insurance gives you ₹10 lakh of protection from the very first month. Saving cannot do that at any speed.

> **Careful.** You need both. Insurance handles the hospital bill. Your [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) handles the loss of income, the travel, the food, and the month of work you miss.

### The five moving parts of any policy

| Part | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Premium** | What you pay, usually once a year | ₹13,500 |
| **Sum insured** | The most the company will pay in one year | ₹10,00,000 |
| **Waiting period** | Time before certain illnesses are covered | 30 days to 3 years |
| **Claim** | How the money reaches the hospital or you | Cashless or reimbursement |
| **Renewal** | You must pay again every year to stay covered | Every 12 months |

*Table 4.2 — every policy in India is built from these five things*

### When does a claim actually qualify?

A health policy pays when you are **admitted as an inpatient for at least 24 hours**. Not when you visit a doctor. Not when you buy medicines. Not when you get a test done.

There is one important exception: **day care treatments**. Modern medicine finishes many procedures in two or three hours that once needed days in a bed — cataract surgery, dialysis, chemotherapy, tonsil removal, several kinds of stone removal. These are covered even though you go home the same day, as long as they appear on your policy's day care list.

> **Careful.** A ₹900 doctor visit is yours to pay. A ₹90,000 cataract surgery lasting two hours is the insurer's. The dividing line is not the amount or the time — it is whether the procedure counts as hospitalisation under your policy.

## 4.3 – The kinds of cover you will be offered

Agents and websites will put several products in front of you. They do different jobs, and confusing them is how people end up over-insured in one place and unprotected in another.

| Type | What it does | Who it suits |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual policy** | Each person gets their own separate sum insured | Anyone; essential if one person has health issues |
| **Family floater** | One shared sum insured for the whole family | Young couples and children |
| **Senior citizen plan** | Built for 60+; usually has co-payment and sub-limits | Parents |
| **Group or employer policy** | Free or cheap cover that ends with the job | Salaried people, as an extra layer only |
| **Super top-up** | Large extra cover that starts above a deductible | Almost everyone; the cheapest way to get big cover |
| **Critical illness plan** | Pays a *lump sum* on diagnosis of a listed illness | Sole earners, or those with family history |
| **Personal accident cover** | Pays on death or disability from an accident | Everyone, especially two-wheeler riders |
| **Government scheme (PM-JAY)** | Up to ₹5 lakh a year at empanelled hospitals | Eligible families and senior citizens aged 70+ |

*Table 4.3 — what you will be offered, and what each one is for*

**The distinction that matters most.** A regular health policy *reimburses your hospital bill*. A critical illness plan *pays you a fixed amount* on diagnosis, regardless of what the treatment cost — money you can use for anything, including the income you lose while not working.

They are not alternatives. A critical illness plan cannot replace a health policy, because it pays nothing for the ordinary hospitalisations that are far more likely to happen to you. If you buy one, buy it *on top of* proper health cover.

> **Do this.** If your household income is modest, check whether your family is eligible for PM-JAY before buying anything. It is free cover, and it does not stop you from buying a private policy alongside.

### What health insurance does not do

- Pay for normal doctor visits and everyday medicines, unless you buy a separate OPD add-on
- Pay for dental treatment, spectacles, contact lenses or hearing aids, unless they follow an accident
- Pay for cosmetic or plastic surgery done by choice
- Pay for infertility or IVF treatment, in most policies
- Pay for treatment taken outside India, unless the policy specifically includes global cover
- Pay for self-inflicted injury, or injury while under the influence of alcohol or drugs
- Pay for non-medical consumables — gloves, syringes, PPE kits — unless covered by an add-on
- Pay anything if you were treated without being admitted
- Return your money if you never claim, or grow it like an investment

### And some things it covers that people don't expect

People pay out of pocket for things their policy would have covered, simply because they never knew.

| Usually covered | What it means |
|---|---|
| Pre-hospitalisation | Tests and consultations in the 30–60 days before admission |
| Post-hospitalisation | Medicines and follow-ups for 60–180 days after discharge |
| Day care procedures | Cataract, dialysis, chemotherapy and similar, without a 24-hour stay |
| Modern treatments | Robotic surgery, oral chemotherapy and others, though some policies cap them |
| AYUSH treatment | Ayurveda, yoga, unani, siddha and homeopathy at a recognised hospital |
| Mental illness | Hospitalisation for mental illness, on the same footing as physical illness |
| Domiciliary treatment | Treatment at home when no bed is available or the patient cannot be moved |
| Organ donor expenses | The donor's hospitalisation costs during a transplant |
| Ambulance charges | Usually up to a small fixed limit per hospitalisation |

*Table 4.4 — claim these. They are already paid for.*

> **Do not** accept a policy that "returns all your premium if you don't claim". That is an investment product dressed as insurance — weaker protection at a much higher price. Keep insurance for protection and investing for growth.

## 4.4 – "But my office already gives me insurance"

Keep it. It is free protection. But it cannot be your only protection, for one simple reason: **it is not yours.**

| What happens | What it costs you |
|---|---|
| You resign, or are laid off | Cover stops the same day — when your income has also stopped |
| You retire at 58 | Cover stops at exactly the age you need it most |
| The company changes insurer | Terms change without asking you |
| You are covered for 10 years by the office | Your personal waiting periods still have not started |

*Table 4.5 — why office cover is a layer, not a foundation*

That last row is the one people miss. Ten years on your employer's policy builds you nothing. If you buy your own policy at 45, your three-year waiting period runs until you are 48 — the exact years when illnesses start appearing.

**The sensible structure:** keep the office policy, and buy your own alongside it. Your own policy stays with you for life. The office one becomes a useful extra layer on top.

### Why buying young matters so much

You can buy an umbrella on any dry day. You cannot buy one once you are already standing in the rain.

Insurance companies check your health before selling you a policy. Once a serious illness has been diagnosed, they may refuse you, or sell you a policy that permanently excludes that illness.

- **The premium is far lower.** A 28-year-old pays a fraction of what a 48-year-old pays, at every age slab afterwards
- **You get accepted without argument.** Nothing to declare, no loading, no exclusions
- **Your waiting periods finish quietly in the background**, during years when you were never going to claim anyway

**The best time to buy health insurance is when you are sure you don't need it.**

## 4.5 – The four words that decide your payout

Two people can both hold "₹5 lakh health insurance" and get completely different amounts on the same bill. The difference is hidden in these words.

You do not need to memorise them. You need to recognise them in a policy document.

| Word | What it means, plainly |
|---|---|
| Sum insured | The maximum paid in one year |
| Network hospital | A hospital where cashless treatment works |
| Cashless | The insurer pays the hospital directly |
| Reimbursement | You pay first, they repay you later |
| Room rent limit | A cap on how costly your hospital room may be |
| Co-payment | A fixed share of every bill you must pay yourself |
| Sub-limit | A cap on one specific treatment |
| Waiting period | Time before an illness becomes covered |
| Pre-existing disease | An illness you already had when buying |
| Deductible | An amount you pay before the insurer starts paying |
| Super top-up | Cheap extra cover that begins above the deductible |
| Restore benefit | Your cover refills if you use it all up in a year |
| No claim bonus | Free extra cover for a claim-free year |
| Moratorium | After 5 years, claims can't be rejected for non-disclosure |
| Portability | Switching insurer without losing your waiting periods |

*Table 4.6 — the vocabulary, in one place*

Now the four that cost people real money.

### 1. Room rent limit

**The technical meaning:** a cap on the daily room charge the insurer will accept — usually written as 1% of sum insured per day.

**What it actually means:** if you take a room costlier than your limit, the insurer cuts down **the entire bill** in proportion — not just the room charge.

**Why it hurts so much:** in Indian hospitals, surgeon fees, nursing charges, ICU charges and even operation theatre rates are linked to your room category. A costlier room quietly makes every other line on the bill costlier too. So the insurer scales down everything.

![A five thousand rupee room limit against a ten thousand rupee room halves the insurer's share of a four lakh bill](/diagrams/room-rent.svg)

*Fig 4.2 — you had ₹5,00,000 of cover, and still paid ₹2,00,000.*

**What to look for:** a policy with **no room rent limit**, or at minimum "single private room" eligibility.

### 2. Co-payment

**The technical meaning:** a fixed percentage of every approved claim that you must bear yourself.

**What it actually means:** with a 20% co-pay, you pay one-fifth of every hospital bill, every time, for as long as you hold the policy.

On a ₹5,00,000 bill, the insurer pays ₹4,00,000 and you pay ₹1,00,000. Co-payment makes the premium look cheap. It makes every future claim smaller.

**What to look for:** 0% co-payment. If a policy is unusually cheap, check this clause before anything else. It is normal in senior citizen plans; avoid it in a young family's policy.

### 3. Waiting periods

**The technical meaning:** defined periods after the policy starts during which specified conditions are not covered.

**What it actually means:** you cannot buy a policy today and claim tomorrow for an illness you already had. If that were allowed, people would only buy insurance after falling sick, and the common pool would collapse.

![A timeline: accidents covered from day one, most illnesses after 30 days, listed illnesses after one to two years, pre-existing disease after up to three years, and the five-year moratorium](/diagrams/waiting-periods.svg)

*Fig 4.3 — what is covered, and when. By regulation, no insurer may impose a pre-existing disease wait longer than 36 months.*

> **Careful — two things people confuse.** The 1–2 year "specific illness" wait applies *even if you were perfectly healthy* when you bought. It is a separate clause from the pre-existing disease wait. Check both.

**Maternity has its own clock.** If maternity is covered at all — many policies leave it out — it usually carries a waiting period of two to four years. This is the one waiting period you have to plan around rather than react to. If you expect a child in the next few years, check the maternity clause before you buy, not afterwards.

**What to look for:** the shortest pre-existing disease wait available. Two years beats three, and that gap matters enormously if anyone in your family has diabetes, BP or thyroid.

### 4. Deductible, and the super top-up

**The technical meaning:** a deductible is the amount of expense you bear before the policy begins to pay. A super top-up pays only above a chosen deductible, counting all your bills across the whole year.

**What it actually means:** this is the cheapest way in Indian insurance to get very large cover. A small base policy handles ordinary hospitalisations. The super top-up sits above it and handles the disasters.

![On a 22 lakh bill, a 10 lakh base policy pays the first 10 lakh and a 40 lakh super top-up pays the remaining 12 lakh](/diagrams/super-topup.svg)

*Fig 4.4 — why not simply buy ₹50 lakh directly? Because the top-up only pays above ₹10 lakh, which happens rarely — so it costs a fraction of the price.*

> **Careful.** Buy a **super** top-up, not a plain top-up. A plain top-up applies the deductible to each single claim; a super top-up applies it to your whole year.

### Three smaller ones worth knowing

**Sub-limits.** Some policies cap individual procedures — cataract at ₹40,000, knee replacement at ₹1.5 lakh. A ₹10 lakh policy with a dozen sub-limits is not really a ₹10 lakh policy.

**Restore benefit.** If you exhaust your cover during a year, the insurer refills it. The strong version restores for *any* illness, including the same one. The weak version only restores for a different illness, once.

**No claim bonus.** Your cover grows free of charge for each claim-free year — often 50% a year up to double. Pleasant, but never treat a future bonus as a reason to buy less cover today.

## 4.6 – So what happened to Anil and Vikram?

Now the story makes sense. Here are their two policies side by side.

| Clause | Anil's policy | Vikram's policy |
|---|---|---|
| Sum insured | ₹5,00,000 | ₹10,00,000 |
| Room rent limit | 1% a day (₹5,000) | None |
| Co-payment | 20% | 0% |
| Sub-limits | On several procedures | None |
| Annual premium | ₹6,200 | ₹13,500 |

*Table 4.7 — the same product, two very different contracts*

Anil chose the cheaper policy and saved ₹7,300 a year. Here is what that saving bought him on the day it mattered.

![Anil pays 2,70,000 of a 4,50,000 bill after a room rent cut and co-payment; Vikram pays nothing](/diagrams/anil-vikram.svg)

*Fig 4.5 — Anil saved ₹7,300 a year on premium. It cost him ₹2,70,000 on one hospital bill.*

His bill worked out like this:

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total bill | ₹4,50,000 |
| Cut for taking a costlier room | − ₹2,25,000 |
| Allowed after that cut | ₹2,25,000 |
| His 20% co-payment | − ₹45,000 |
| **Insurer paid** | **₹1,80,000** |
| **Anil paid from savings** | **₹2,70,000** |

*Table 4.8 — how ₹4,50,000 of cover became ₹1,80,000*

This is the single most important idea in this chapter. **"Having health insurance" is not the goal. Having health insurance that actually pays is the goal.** The gap between the two is written in the policy document, in plain sight, before you buy.

## 4.7 – How to choose: ten steps

You now know what the clauses do. This turns that into a decision. Keep a note open — you will write down six things.

**1. List everyone you need to cover.** Names, ages, and every existing health condition. Be completely honest in that last column — anything you take regular medicine for, any past surgery, any test result a doctor flagged. Everything you write here must eventually go into the insurance form.

**2. Decide how many policies you need.** Three rules settle this:

- **Parents go on a separate policy.** Adding a 63-year-old to your family floater raises the premium for everyone, and one large claim by them can exhaust the cover your child needs
- **You, your spouse and children can share a floater.** Cheaper than separate policies while everyone is healthy
- **Use a small base policy plus a super top-up** rather than one large policy

So a typical family ends up with four policies: a floater, a super top-up on it, and one senior policy each for father and mother. That sounds like more work. It costs less and protects better.

**3. Find out what treatment really costs where you live.** Everyone skips this step, and it decides whether your cover is real or imaginary.

Name the two hospitals your family would actually go to at 2 a.m. Then find out roughly what a serious treatment costs there — a bypass, a cancer treatment cycle, an accident with a few days in ICU. Ask the billing desk, or look at published package rates.

As a rough reality check, a major surgery at a good private hospital in a metro runs into several lakh, and a Tier-2 city may be meaningfully lower. Cancer care or a transplant can go well beyond. Now compare that to the cover you were planning to buy. This is where "₹5 lakh should be enough" quietly falls apart.

**Write down: the cost of a serious illness at your hospital.**

**4. Fix your sum insured.**

| Your situation | Base cover | Super top-up |
|---|---|---|
| Single, 25–35, metro | ₹10 lakh | ₹20–40 lakh |
| Couple, metro | ₹10–15 lakh floater | ₹40 lakh |
| Family of 4, metro or Tier-1 | ₹10–15 lakh floater | ₹40–50 lakh |
| Family of 4, Tier-2 or Tier-3 | ₹10 lakh floater | ₹25–40 lakh |
| Parents aged 60+ | ₹10 lakh each, separate | ₹20–25 lakh if available |

*Table 4.9 — educational starting points, not recommendations*

Two sanity checks: cover of roughly 50–100% of your annual income is a common reference, and whatever number feels right today will feel small in six years because of medical inflation.

**Write down: your base cover and your top-up cover.**

**5. Set a premium budget you can pay for thirty years.** A workable reference is 3–6% of your annual income for all your health policies together.

Then ask the insurer what this policy will cost at 45, 55 and 65. Premiums climb with age slabs, and a policy you are forced to drop at 55 is worse than a smaller policy you keep for life. If the premium hurts, do not solve it by buying a ₹3 lakh policy — reduce the base cover and raise the super top-up instead.

**Write down: your annual premium budget.**

**6. Shortlist three or four policies.** Only now do you look at actual products. Get quotes for your exact family, exact ages, exact cover. Three or four — not ten, or you will never finish comparing.

Ignore advertisements, celebrity faces, "No. 1 in India" claims and cashback offers. None of those pay hospital bills.

Then **download the full policy wording PDF** for each one — the 30–60 page document, not the two-page brochure. If a company makes that document hard to find, treat it as information about the company.

**7. Run the eight-clause elimination test.** This is the highest-value half hour in the whole process. Open each policy wording and use Ctrl+F, or the search icon on your phone, to find these eight things. Most policies fail at least one — that is the point. You are eliminating, not admiring.

| Search for | You want | Walk away from |
|---|---|---|
| "room rent" | No limit, or single private room | 1% of sum insured per day |
| "co-payment" | 0% | Any compulsory co-pay |
| "sub-limit" | None | Caps on cataract, knee, etc. |
| "pre-existing" | 2 years | Full 36 months |
| "specific" / "waiting" | Short list, 1 year | Long list, 2 years |
| "restore" | Any illness, repeatedly | Different illness only, once |
| "pre-hospitalisation" | 60 days before, 180 after | 30 days and 60 days |
| "exclusions" | Read the whole section | Consumables not covered, no add-on |

*Table 4.10 — the eight-clause test*

The first three rows carry the most weight. A policy that fails on room rent or co-payment should usually be dropped even when it is cheaper — that is precisely the mistake Anil made.

**8. Check the insurer, not only the policy.** A perfect policy from a company that fights every claim is not a perfect policy.

- **Your hospitals must be on their network list.** Open the insurer's hospital list, search your own pin code, and look for the two hospitals you named in step three. If they are missing, cashless will not work for you. Five minutes, and almost nobody does it
- **Look at the claim record.** The incurred claim ratio, the claim settlement percentage, and complaints per 10,000 policies, published in the IRDAI annual report
- **Find out who handles claims.** Some insurers do it in-house; others outsource to a third party administrator. In-house teams tend to be faster, and there is one less party to chase

**9. Fill the form yourself, and declare everything.** You have chosen well. Do not undo it here.

Never let an agent fill the proposal form "to save your time". You are legally answerable for every answer in that form, whoever typed it.

People hide diabetes hoping for a cheaper premium. What actually happens is that the claim gets rejected three years later, when the money is needed most, and the policy may be cancelled. What happens if you declare it is far milder: a slightly higher premium, or a waiting period on that condition.

> **Do this.** Declaring everything starts a clock in your favour. After **five continuous years of cover — the moratorium — the insurer can no longer reject your claim for non-disclosure**, except for proven fraud. Hiding starts nothing. And if a medical test is asked for, take it: a policy issued after a health check-up is much harder to dispute later.

**10. Do four things in the first month.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Read it in the free-look window** | If the document does not match what you were promised, cancel within **30 days** for a refund, minus stamp duty and the days covered |
| **Set up auto-debit** | If the policy lapses past the grace period, you lose every waiting period served and every bonus earned |
| **Tell your family where it is** | A policy nobody knows about is no use in a hospital lobby at midnight |
| **Set a review reminder for two years** | Medical inflation quietly halves your real cover every five to six years |

*Table 4.11 — the first month after buying*

> **Do this if you are buying today and have thirty minutes.** Take at least ₹10 lakh base cover with a super top-up above it. Check three clauses only — room rent limit, co-payment, sub-limits — and reject anything with a room rent cap or compulsory co-pay. Search your pin code on the network hospital list. Declare every health condition. Those four alone put you ahead of most buyers in India.

## 4.8 – What happens when you actually claim

There are two ways money moves, depending on whether your hospital is on the insurer's network list.

![The cashless claim flow: show your card, the hospital sends details, the insurer approves within an hour, you are treated, and final approval comes within three hours at discharge](/diagrams/cashless-claim.svg)

*Fig 4.6 — a cashless claim at a network hospital. Timelines shown are the maximums insurers must meet under current IRDAI rules.*

**Two things to remember.** For a planned surgery, tell the insurer two or three days in advance. For an emergency, tell them within 24 hours of admission.

**If the hospital is not on the network list**, it becomes a reimbursement claim. You inform the insurer within 24–48 hours, pay the hospital yourself at discharge, then submit the discharge summary, all original bills and receipts, pharmacy bills, every test report, doctors' prescriptions, and your policy and ID documents. The approved amount is credited to your bank account.

> **Careful.** Keep every receipt, including small pharmacy slips. Missing original bills is one of the most common reasons for a claim being paid only in part.

## 4.9 – Rights you already have

These protections exist under current Indian insurance rules. Most policyholders have no idea they are there.

| Your right | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| 30-day free look | Cancel a new policy within 30 days and get a refund |
| Maximum 36-month pre-existing wait | No insurer may make you wait longer |
| 5-year moratorium | After 5 years, no rejection for non-disclosure except proven fraud |
| No maximum entry age | You cannot be refused cover purely for being old |
| Lifetime renewability | Renewal cannot be refused because you aged or claimed |
| 1-hour cashless approval | Insurers must decide on pre-authorisation within an hour |
| 3-hour discharge approval | Final cashless approval must come within three hours |
| Cap on senior premium hikes | Senior citizens' premiums cannot rise sharply without regulatory approval |
| Portability | Switch insurer at renewal and carry your waiting periods across. Apply 45 days before renewal |
| Written reason for rejection | You can demand the exact clause, in writing |
| Escalation | Insurer first, then IRDAI's grievance portal, then the Insurance Ombudsman |

*Table 4.12 — protections you already have*

If a claim is rejected unfairly, you are not out of options. Get the reason in writing, check the clause cited against your own policy wording, and escalate.

## 4.10 – Tax and GST

**GST.** Since 22 September 2025, individual and family floater health insurance premiums are exempt from GST. Earlier, 18% was added on top — a ₹20,000 premium used to cost ₹23,600 and now costs ₹20,000. Group policies bought by employers still carry 18%, but the employer pays that, not you.

**Section 80D.** Health premiums are deductible — but only under the old tax regime. The new regime, now the default, does not allow it.

| Premium paid for | Deduction limit |
|---|---|
| Self, spouse, children (all under 60) | ₹25,000 |
| Self, spouse, children (any one 60+) | ₹50,000 |
| Parents (under 60) | ₹25,000 |
| Parents (60 or above) | ₹50,000 |
| **Maximum possible** | **₹1,00,000** |

*Table 4.13 — Section 80D limits*

Preventive health check-ups of up to ₹5,000 sit inside these limits, not on top. Premium must be paid by card, UPI or net banking — cash is not eligible. Under the Income-tax Act, 2025, effective 1 April 2026, this provision is renumbered as Section 126, with the same limits.

> **Careful.** Tax rules change often. Verify the current position for your assessment year before relying on this while filing.

## 4.11 – Your checklist before you pay

- The cover is large enough for a serious illness at my hospital
- I have considered a base policy plus a super top-up
- My parents are on their own separate policy
- No room rent limit, or single private room at minimum
- 0% co-payment, or I have consciously accepted it
- No disease-wise sub-limits
- The pre-existing waiting period is the shortest available to me
- I read the exclusions section myself
- I read the waiting periods section myself
- My two hospitals are on the network list
- Restore benefit is included
- Pre and post hospitalisation cover is 60 and 180 days
- I know whether claims are handled in-house or by a TPA
- I declared every illness, medicine and past surgery
- I filled the form myself and read every answer
- I can afford this premium every year, including at 60
- Auto-debit or a renewal reminder is set
- My family knows this policy exists and where to find it
- I have a reminder to review the cover in two years

## 4.13 – Questions people actually ask

#### Just tell me which policy is the best one.

No honest article can name one. It depends on your age, city, family and health, which is exactly what the ten steps work out.

But we can describe what a good policy looks like: no room rent limit, no co-payment, no sub-limits, the shortest pre-existing waiting period available to you, a restore benefit, and an insurer with a solid claim record whose network includes your hospitals. Any policy that clears all of those is a reasonable choice. Between two that clear them, take the cheaper one.

#### Is it worth buying if I am young and healthy?

That is precisely when it is cheapest, when you are accepted without conditions, and when your waiting periods run out harmlessly during years you were never going to claim in.

Accidents do not check your age. Neither does appendicitis, dengue, or a kidney stone.

#### I cannot afford a big cover right now. What should I do?

Start with something you can sustain, and build the structure correctly rather than buying a tiny policy.

A ₹5 lakh base with a ₹20 lakh super top-up costs far less than a ₹25 lakh base policy, and protects you far better than a ₹3 lakh policy. Raise the base as your income grows.

What you should not do is skip insurance entirely while you "wait until you can afford proper cover". The waiting periods only start when you buy.

#### We are planning a baby in the next two years. What should I look for?

Check the maternity clause before you buy anything. Many policies exclude maternity entirely, and those that include it usually impose a waiting period of two to four years.

That means the decision has to be made well before the pregnancy, not during it. Look at what the maternity benefit actually pays, whether newborn cover is included from day one, and whether vaccinations in the first year are covered.

#### An agent is pushing a critical illness plan. Do I need one?

Not instead of a health policy. A critical illness plan pays a fixed lump sum on diagnosis of a listed illness — it pays nothing for the ordinary hospitalisations that are far more likely to happen to you.

Where it genuinely helps is with the costs a health policy ignores: the months of income you lose, the travel, the help at home, a career break. If you are the only earner in your family, that is a real risk worth covering.

Sequence it correctly. Proper health cover first, then a critical illness plan on top if there is budget left.

#### My family qualifies for a government scheme. Is that enough?

It is a genuine safety net and you should absolutely use it if you are eligible — it costs you nothing.

Two limits: it works only at empanelled hospitals, which may not include the hospital you would choose, and the cover is capped at ₹5 lakh a year for the whole family, which one serious illness can exhaust.

Treat it as your base layer. A private policy or even a super top-up alongside it is much stronger than either alone.

#### Should I buy online, or through an agent?

Either works. Online is usually cheaper and lets you compare policy wordings side by side. A good agent is useful at claim time if they actually help you follow up.

What matters in both cases is the same: *you* read the policy wording, and *you* answer the health questions yourself.

#### The premium quoted to me is much lower than others. Should I take it?

Check three clauses before anything else: room rent limit, co-payment, and sub-limits. A low premium almost always has at least one of them.

Sometimes the discount is legitimate — a zone-based price for a smaller city, or a multi-year payment discount. But assume it is one of those three until you have checked.

#### I have diabetes or high BP. Can I still get a policy?

Yes. You cannot be refused cover purely for having a condition.

What usually happens is one of three things: a higher premium (called a loading), a waiting period on that specific condition, or in some cases a permanent exclusion for it. All three are far better than no policy, because everything unrelated to that condition is covered from day one.

Buy sooner rather than later. The waiting period only starts when the policy starts.

#### I bought a policy last year and forgot to mention an old surgery. What now?

Tell the insurer now, in writing, rather than waiting for a claim.

They may adjust your premium, add a waiting period, or simply record it. That is a much smaller problem than having a claim investigated later and finding an undisclosed history. Keep a copy of what you sent and their reply.

#### An agent told me not to mention a condition because "it will be covered after some years anyway".

Ignore that advice completely. It is the single most common reason claims are rejected in India.

The agent will not be there when your claim is investigated. You will be, and the form has your signature on it. Declare it, take whatever loading or waiting period comes, and your claim stays safe.

#### I have office insurance. Should I still buy my own?

Yes, and keep both. The office policy ends the day your job does, and permanently when you retire — at the age you need cover most.

Your own policy also builds your waiting periods in the background. Ten years on an employer policy builds you nothing.

#### I have both my own policy and my office policy. Which one do I claim from?

You can legally use both. For most bills, claiming from the office policy first is sensible — it keeps your personal policy claim-free, which protects your no claim bonus.

If a bill is larger than the office cover, claim what you can from it, get a settlement letter and attested copies of the bills, and claim the balance from your own policy. This is a normal, accepted process.

#### My parents are over 65. Is it too late?

No. The maximum entry age cap was removed, so insurers cannot refuse cover purely because of age.

Expect a higher premium, a compulsory co-payment, possibly some sub-limits, and a medical check-up before issue. Also check whether they qualify for any government scheme in your state.

#### A baby was just born. How do I add the child?

Most family floaters allow a newborn to be added, usually after 90 days, at the next renewal or sometimes mid-term. Call the insurer and ask for the endorsement process.

Adding a child is also the right moment to revisit the sum insured. A floater sized for two people is now shared by three.

#### I got married. Can I add my spouse to my policy?

Usually yes, at renewal, by converting an individual policy into a floater. Your spouse's own waiting periods start from the date they are added, not from when you bought.

Compare it against keeping two individual policies. If one of you has a health condition, separate policies sometimes work out better.

#### The hospital says cashless is not available for my policy. What do I do?

First, do not delay treatment over it. Get admitted.

Then call the insurer's helpline yourself, not just the hospital desk — sometimes it is a paperwork issue at the hospital, not a refusal. Ask directly whether pre-authorisation has been received and what is pending.

If cashless genuinely is not available, it becomes a reimbursement claim. Inform the insurer within 24 hours, keep every original bill and report, and claim afterwards. You are not losing the money, only paying it first.

#### The hospital is asking for an advance even though I have insurance.

This is common while pre-authorisation is still pending, and in emergencies where there was no time to get approval first.

Pay it if you need to, keep the receipt, and it is adjusted at discharge once approval comes through. If approval does not come through, that receipt becomes part of your reimbursement claim.

#### My treatment is going on and the bill has crossed my sum insured. What happens?

The insurer pays up to your sum insured. Everything beyond that is yours to pay, unless you have a super top-up or a restore benefit.

If you have a restore benefit, ask the hospital desk and the insurer to confirm it is being applied. If your super top-up is with a different insurer, tell them now rather than after discharge — they will need the base policy's settlement details.

This is the situation people are describing when they say a ₹5 lakh policy "was not enough".

#### Should I claim for a small hospital bill, or pay it myself?

Weigh it against your no claim bonus. A claim of ₹25,000 can cost you a bonus worth far more in added cover.

Many people pay small bills themselves and keep the policy for large events. There is no universal answer — it depends on the amount and on how your policy's bonus works.

#### My claim was rejected. What can I actually do?

You have a proper escalation path. Use it in order.

1. Ask for the rejection in writing, quoting the exact clause they are relying on
2. Open your own policy wording and read that clause. Rejections are sometimes based on a misreading of your file — a wrong admission date, a missing report, a condition recorded incorrectly
3. Write to the insurer's grievance officer with your evidence. They must respond
4. If unsatisfied, escalate to IRDAI through its grievance portal
5. If still unresolved, approach the Insurance Ombudsman. It is free, and you do not need a lawyer

Also check how long you have held the policy. After five continuous years, a claim cannot be rejected for non-disclosure except for proven fraud.

#### The insurer approved much less than my bill. Is that normal?

It is common, and there is usually a specific reason — a room rent limit causing a proportionate cut, a co-payment, a sub-limit on that procedure, or non-medical consumables that were never covered.

Ask for the deduction breakdown in writing. It will name each deduction. Then check each one against your policy wording. If a deduction does not match your policy, dispute it in writing.

#### The TPA keeps delaying and nobody answers properly.

Go around the TPA. Write directly to the insurer's grievance officer, whose contact details are in your policy document, mentioning the specific delay and dates.

Keep everything in writing or email. Phone calls leave no record, and a written trail is what the grievance process runs on.

#### I forgot to renew and the due date has passed.

Act immediately. Policies normally have a grace period of around 15 to 30 days in which you can pay and continue without losing your accumulated waiting periods and bonus. Illnesses occurring during the gap are usually not covered even if you renew within grace.

If the grace period has also passed, the policy lapses. You would have to buy fresh, and all your waiting periods restart from zero. This is one of the most expensive avoidable mistakes in insurance.

#### Can I switch insurers without losing my waiting periods?

Yes. This is called portability, and it is a right, not a favour.

Apply to the new insurer at least 45 days before your renewal date. The waiting periods you have already served carry across. The new insurer can still underwrite you — they may decline, or apply loading — so do not cancel the old policy until the new one is confirmed in writing.

#### I want to increase my cover. Should I raise the base or add a top-up?

Adding a super top-up is usually far cheaper for the same total protection, and is the better first move.

One thing to check: a new super top-up starts its own waiting periods, so the increase in real protection for pre-existing conditions is not immediate. Increasing the base cover at renewal may also apply fresh waiting periods to the increased portion — ask the insurer specifically how they treat the enhanced amount.

#### I moved to a different city. Does anything change?

Tell the insurer. Many policies are priced by zone, so a move from a smaller city to a metro can change your premium, and not telling them can complicate a claim.

Also re-check the network hospital list for your new pin code. The hospitals you would now actually use may be different ones.

## Key takeaways

- Health insurance pays your hospital bill in exchange for a small yearly amount. It does not pay for doctor visits, medicines or tests without admission.
- Buy it while you are healthy. Once an illness is diagnosed you may be refused, or covered with that illness permanently excluded.
- Four clauses decide how much you actually get: room rent limit, co-payment, sub-limits and waiting periods. Read those before you read the premium.
- A room rent limit cuts your *entire* bill in proportion, not just the room charge. This is what cost Anil ₹2,70,000.
- A small base policy plus a super top-up gives large cover for a fraction of the price of one big policy.
- Keep parents on a separate policy, and treat office cover as an extra layer rather than your protection.
- Declare every illness. Non-disclosure is the biggest single cause of rejected claims, and after five continuous years the moratorium protects you.
- Check your own hospitals are on the insurer's network list before you pay.
