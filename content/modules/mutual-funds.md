## 7.1 – Why mutual funds exist

Priya is 24. She earns ₹42,000 a month in Hyderabad. After rent, food and a little fun, about ₹6,000 is left.

Everyone tells her to invest. So she opens a share app. One share of a good company costs ₹3,400. Her ₹6,000 buys one share. Maybe two.

If that one company has a bad year, Priya has a bad year. That is not investing. That is a bet.

A mutual fund fixes exactly this.

Imagine 10,000 people like Priya. Alone, nobody can buy a proper mix of companies. Together they have crores. So they put the money in one pool, a licensed company looks after it, and a fund manager uses the pool to buy shares of 40 or 50 companies. Each person gets **units** in proportion to what they put in.

Priya's ₹6,000 now owns a small piece of 50 companies. Not half a share of one.

> **A mutual fund is** money from many people, put in one pool, managed by a professional, and invested in shares or bonds. Your units show your share of the pool.

![Many investors pay into one pool, which buys shares and bonds, and each investor receives units in return](/diagrams/mf-pool.svg)

*Fig 7.1 — money flows in, gets invested as one portfolio, and everyone holds units.*

**Why nobody can steal it.** Your money belongs to a **trust**, not to the fund company. If the company shuts down, your units are still yours. **SEBI** decides what each fund can hold, what it can be called, and how much it can charge.

## 7.2 – NAV, and the mistake almost everyone makes

NAV is the price of one unit today, calculated once every working day after the market closes.

A fund owns shares worth ₹500 crore and has 25 crore units. NAV = ₹20. Put in ₹10,000 and you get 500 units.

> **Do not** choose a fund because its NAV looks low. A ₹10 NAV is not cheaper than a ₹250 NAV. It only shows how old the fund is. What matters is the **percentage** the NAV rises — identical either way.

![Two funds with different NAVs turn the same 5,000 rupees into the same 5,600 after a 12 percent rise](/diagrams/nav-myth.svg)

*Fig 7.2 — the number of units differs. The money does not. A New Fund Offer at ₹10 is not a discount.*

**How your money actually grows.** The companies inside the fund do well, the pool's value rises, the NAV rises. Then your profit starts earning profit — compounding.

![A 5,000 rupee monthly SIP grows to 4.1 lakh in five years, 11.6 lakh in ten, 49.9 lakh in twenty and 1.76 crore in thirty](/diagrams/compounding.svg)

*Fig 7.3 — ₹5,000 a month at an assumed 12%. You put in ₹18 lakh over thirty years; growth added the rest. Illustration only.*

Two lessons from that shape. **Starting early beats investing more** — ten extra years at the beginning does more than doubling your amount later. And **the slow middle years are the price**; the first ten years look disappointing, which is why most people quit.

## 7.3 – Three things before your first rupee

> **Do this first.** An [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) of three to six months of expenses, because without it the first problem forces you to sell at the worst time. [Health insurance](/learn/health-insurance), because one hospital bill can wipe out years of investing. And clear any [credit card](/learn/credit-cards) balance — 36% a year cannot be beaten by any fund.

### The risk, stated honestly

This is the part people skip, and it is why most beginners lose money. Not because they picked a bad fund — because they picked a fund whose falls they could not sit through.

- **Nothing is guaranteed.** A mutual fund is not an FD. Returns can be negative for years at a stretch
- **Equity funds fall hard.** Drops of 30% or more have happened several times and will happen again. Small cap funds have fallen 40% and taken years to recover
- **The fall is not the risk. Selling during the fall is the risk.** A temporary fall becomes a permanent loss only when you sell

> **Careful — ask yourself one question.** If your ₹5 lakh became ₹3.2 lakh in eight months, and the news said it would get worse, would you keep your SIP running?
>
> If the honest answer is no, you do not need a braver mindset. You need a calmer type of fund.

## 7.4 – Direct or Regular: the costliest choice you will make

Two versions of every fund exist. Same shares, same manager, same NAV movement. The only difference is whether a commission is built into the fee.

**Direct** means you invested yourself — no commission. **Regular** means someone sold it to you and gets paid every year, from your money, for as long as you hold it. The gap is usually 0.6% to 1% a year.

![A direct plan reaches about 99.9 lakh over twenty years while a regular plan reaches 87.4 lakh — a gap of 12.5 lakh](/diagrams/direct-regular.svg)

*Fig 7.4 — ₹10,000 a month for 20 years, assuming 12% direct and 11% regular. You invested ₹24 lakh either way.*

> **Careful.** Open your statement. The fund name says either "Direct Plan" or "Regular Plan". If it says Regular and nobody is genuinely advising you, you are paying a commission for nothing.

**Also choose Growth, not IDCW.** Growth keeps the profit inside the fund, compounding. IDCW pays you cash but the NAV drops by exactly that amount — it is your own money coming back, taxed at your income slab rate. SEBI renamed it Income Distribution cum Capital **Withdrawal** so the word would be in the name.

## 7.5 – Types of mutual funds

SEBI does not let companies invent their own types. Every fund sits in one fixed box with a written rule about what it can hold.

Two sorting questions matter.

**Open-ended or not.** Open-ended means you can buy or sell any working day. That is what you want. Close-ended funds lock your money for a fixed term — skip them.

**Active or passive.** The biggest choice most beginners never realise they are making.

| | Active fund | Passive fund (index, ETF) |
|---|---|---|
| Goal | Beat the index | Copy the index |
| Who picks the shares | A fund manager | A rule — if a company is in the Nifty 50, the fund buys it |
| Usual cost | 0.5–1.2% direct | Often 0.1–0.3% |
| What can go wrong | The manager does badly, or leaves | Nothing surprising. You get the index minus a small cost |

*Table 7.1 — active versus passive*

An active fund must first earn back its higher fee before you are ahead. Many do not manage that every year. For a first-time investor, an index fund is the easiest thing to get right.

### Equity funds — all thirteen

Equity funds buy company shares. Highest growth over long periods, biggest falls on the way. **Large cap** is the top 100 listed companies, **mid cap** is 101 to 250, **small cap** is 251 and below.

| Type | What it must hold | Time needed | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Large Cap | 80%+ in the top 100 companies | 5 years+ | Lowest in equity |
| Large & Mid Cap | 35% large cap and 35% mid cap | 7 years+ | High |
| Mid Cap | 65%+ in companies 101 to 250 | 7 years+ | High |
| Small Cap | 65%+ in companies 251 and below | 10 years+ | Very high |
| Multi Cap | 25% each in large, mid and small cap | 7 years+ | High |
| Flexi Cap | 65%+ equity, no size rules | 7 years+ | High |
| Focused | 80%+ equity, maximum 30 shares | 7 years+ | Very high |
| Value | 80%+ equity, buys what looks cheap | 7 years+ | High |
| Contra | 80%+ equity, buys what others avoid | 7 years+ | High |
| Dividend Yield | 80%+ in dividend-paying companies | 7 years+ | High |
| Sectoral | 80%+ in one sector | 7 years+ | Very high |
| Thematic | 80%+ in one theme, across sectors | 7 years+ | Very high |
| ELSS Tax Saver | 80%+ equity, 3-year lock-in | 5 years+ | High |

*Table 7.2 — all thirteen equity categories*

**You will realistically use three.** A **flexi cap** or **index fund** as your main holding. A **mid or small cap** only if you already hold a broad fund and know you will not sell in a bad year. An **ELSS** only if you file under the old tax regime — on the new regime it gives you a 3-year lock-in and nothing else.

> **Do not** make a sectoral or thematic fund your first fund. They are sold loudest just after that sector has already gone up. A diversified fund can move out of a bad sector; a sectoral fund is not allowed to.

### Debt funds — all seventeen

Debt funds lend money instead of buying ownership. Steadier than equity, not fully safe. Two risks: **interest rate risk** (when rates rise, older bonds lose value, so the NAV dips — the longer the bonds, the bigger the swing) and **credit risk** (the borrower does not repay).

Sorted by how long the bonds run:

| Money needed in | Use this |
|---|---|
| Days to a month | Overnight Fund — the safest option in the whole mutual fund world |
| 1 to 3 months | Liquid Fund — good for part of your emergency money |
| 3 to 12 months | Ultra Short Duration, Low Duration, Money Market |
| 1 to 3 years | Short Duration Fund |
| 3 to 7 years | Corporate Bond, Banking & PSU, Medium Duration, Medium to Long Duration |
| With a view on rates | Gilt, 10-Year Constant Maturity Gilt, Long Duration, Dynamic Bond, Floater |
| Higher risk | Credit Risk Fund, Sectoral Debt Fund |

*Table 7.3 — all seventeen debt categories, grouped by timeline*

> **Careful.** A **Credit Risk Fund** buys weaker borrowers on purpose for a higher return. One default can damage the NAV badly. It looks safe because it is a debt fund. It is not an FD substitute.

### Hybrid and everything else

Hybrid funds hold equity and debt together, so the fund does the balancing for you.

| Type | Equity portion | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Conservative Hybrid | 10–25% | Mostly safe, small growth part |
| Balanced Hybrid | 40–60% | A real middle path |
| **Aggressive Hybrid** | 65–80% | Equity tax treatment, debt softens the falls. A sensible first fund for a nervous investor |
| Balanced Advantage | 10–80%, by formula | Raises equity when the market looks cheap, cuts it when costly |
| Multi Asset Allocation | 10%+ in three assets | Usually equity, debt and gold in one fund |
| Arbitrage | 65%+, fully hedged | Low real risk but equity tax. Used for short-term money in high tax brackets |
| Equity Savings | Part open, part hedged | Softer than full equity. Suits a 2–4 year goal |

*Table 7.4 — all seven hybrid categories*

| Type | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Index Fund** | Copies an index. Judge it on two things only: expense ratio and tracking error |
| **ETF** | An index fund traded on the exchange. Cheapest, but needs a demat account |
| Gold Fund | Follows the gold price. For balance, not growth — gold can stay flat for years |
| International Fund | 65%+ outside India. Adds country spread and currency movement |
| Life Cycle Fund | A target-year fund that cuts equity automatically as the year approaches |

*Table 7.5 — index, ETF and the rest*

## 7.6 – Past performance: what it can and cannot tell you

Every fund page leads with returns. Very few funds stay in the top group for many years running — a fund that was number one three years ago is often mid-table today.

> **Do this — read past performance as a character check, not a forecast.** It is good for checking whether a fund did what it promised, how it behaved when markets fell, and whether it was consistent. It is not good for predicting next year, or picking "the best fund".

### The fund's advertised return is not your return

A fund's "5-year return" assumes you put all your money in on one single day, five years ago. You did not — you invested every month, at different prices.

| Number | What it means | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute return | Total gain, ignoring time | Nothing. Useless until you know over how many years |
| CAGR | Average yearly growth | Comparing one-time investments over the same period |
| **XIRR** | The same, for a SIP with many dates | **Your real return.** Your statement shows it |
| **Rolling returns** | The return from every possible start date | The most honest number on the page. Use 5-year and 7-year |

*Table 7.6 — the four numbers called "returns"*

### One year tells you almost nothing

![Six yearly returns of plus 34, minus 9, plus 18, plus 2, minus 14 and plus 26 percent, averaging 9.5 percent — with no single year close to the average](/diagrams/volatility.svg)

*Fig 7.6 — an average of 9.5% a year, built from years that look nothing like it. If you had judged this fund at the end of Year 2 or Year 5, you would have sold. Both times you would have been wrong.*

This is why rolling returns matter. A one-year number depends entirely on which day you started.

**Also check how far it fell, and how long it took to recover.** Returns tell you what the fund earned; falls tell you whether you would still be holding it. A fund with slightly lower returns and much smaller falls is often the better fund for a real person.

**And compare it with its own benchmark**, not with other funds. A small cap fund is not supposed to beat a large cap fund — it is supposed to beat the small cap index. If your fund fell 18% and its whole category fell 20%, your fund did not fail.

| Looks convincing | Why it misleads |
|---|---|
| Last year's number one fund | Usually means that category has already had its run |
| Star ratings | Describe the past, change often, know nothing about your goal |
| A New Fund Offer | Nothing to check — no track record, no history of falls |
| A low NAV | Only means the fund is younger |
| A 10-year record | Check who created it. It means little if the manager joined last year |

*Table 7.7 — five convincing-looking traps*

## 7.7 – How to select a fund

There is no secret list of best funds. Anybody who gives you one without knowing your situation is selling something. What exists is a method.

**Step 1 — Write the goal and the year.** Not "I want good returns". Write "₹8 lakh for a down payment in 4 years", or "retirement, 27 years away".

**Step 2 — Let the year choose the type.**

| Money needed in | Where it should go |
|---|---|
| Less than 1 year | Liquid or overnight fund |
| 1 to 3 years | Short duration, arbitrage, equity savings |
| 3 to 5 years | Conservative or balanced hybrid, corporate bond |
| 5 to 10 years | Index, large cap, flexi cap, aggressive hybrid |
| 10 years or more | Flexi cap, multi cap, index. Add mid or small cap if you can handle it |

*Table 7.8 — the year decides the type*

Equity can fall 30% in a year. Never put money in equity if you have already promised it to something.

**Step 3 — Check it against your honest risk answer.** If you would not have held through a 35% fall, move one row up this table.

**Step 4 — Shortlist inside that one type only.** Compare index funds with index funds, small caps with small caps. Comparing across types only tells you which asset class had a good year.

**Step 5 — Run these ten checks.**

- The name says **Direct** and **Growth**
- It is the exact type you chose
- The expense ratio is at or below others of the same type
- It beat its own benchmark over most long periods, not just one year
- Rolling returns over 5 and 7 years look steady
- You have seen its worst fall and could live with it
- The current manager has been there three years or more
- The fund size is sensible for that type
- It does not overlap heavily with a fund you already own
- You know the exit load, lock-in, and how the profit is taxed

**Two to four funds is enough** for almost everybody. Each equity fund already holds 40 to 60 companies — a ninth fund adds nothing.

### When to sell, and when not to

| Good reasons | Bad reasons |
|---|---|
| You reached your goal, or it is under a year away | The market fell and the news sounds frightening |
| Your situation changed | A different fund did better last year |
| The fund changed what it does, or changed manager | Your fund is down 15% and you feel bad |
| It lost to its own benchmark for three years or more | Somebody online said a crash is coming |

*Table 7.9 — the honest test*

> **Do this.** When your goal is one to two years away, start moving money out of equity in steps, into a liquid or short duration fund. Do not wait for the last month and hope the market is kind that week.

## 7.8 – Tax, briefly

You pay no tax while the money grows. You pay only when you **sell**.

| Fund type | Held under 12 months | Held over 12 months |
|---|---|---|
| **Equity** (65%+ equity) | 20% | 12.5% on gains above ₹1.25 lakh a year |
| **Debt** | Your income slab rate | Your income slab rate — holding period makes no difference |

*Table 7.10 — capital gains. Rates exclude surcharge and cess.*

Three things that confuse people:

- **Every SIP instalment is separate**, with its own holding period. Funds sell oldest units first, so one withdrawal can create both short-term and long-term tax
- **The ₹1.25 lakh limit is for the whole year**, across all equity funds and shares together — not per fund
- **Switching is the same as selling.** Fund to fund, or Regular to Direct, is legally a sale plus a purchase. Tax applies, and exit load may too

## 7.9 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **Stopping the SIP when the market falls** | The most expensive habit in investing. A falling market is exactly when your fixed amount buys the most units |
| **Putting short-term money into equity** | The date does not move when the market falls, so you are forced to sell at the worst time |
| **Buying a Regular plan without knowing** | A silent 0.6–1% every year. Check your statement for the word "Direct" |
| **Buying last year's number one fund** | Usually the category that already had its run |
| **Holding eight funds that own the same companies** | Not eight times the safety. Two to four is enough |
| **Choosing because the NAV is low** | A ₹12 NAV is not cheaper than ₹340 |
| **Investing before an emergency fund** | The first hospital bill forces a withdrawal, during a fall, with exit load and tax |
| **Expecting 12% every single year** | It is a long-term average, not a yearly delivery |
| **Checking your fund every day** | Daily movement is noise. It turns a 15-year plan into 1,500 emotional decisions |
| **Not adding a nominee** | Your family goes through a long legal process for money already theirs |
| **Buying ELSS on the new tax regime** | A 3-year lock-in and zero benefit |
| **Making a sectoral fund your first fund** | Concentration risk with no diversified base to protect you |

## 7.10 – Questions people actually ask

#### Are mutual funds safe?

Two different questions. The *system* is well protected — your money sits in a trust, SEBI decides what each fund can hold, and the company cannot take your units. The *value* is not guaranteed; an equity fund can fall 30% or more.

#### Can I lose all my money?

In a diversified fund holding dozens of companies, losing everything would need the whole market to become zero. Big temporary falls are normal. Losing everything permanently is not, unless you are in an extreme single-sector or credit-risk fund.

#### What is the minimum amount?

Most funds allow a SIP from ₹100 to ₹500 upwards. Start small if you are unsure — in the first year the habit matters more than the amount.

#### Do I need a demat account?

No, for normal mutual funds. Only for ETFs, which are bought on the stock exchange.

#### Index fund or active fund?

An index fund gives you the market minus a very small cost, with no manager risk — the most forgiving choice for a beginner. An active fund can do better, but it must beat its index by more than its fee, and many do not manage this regularly.

#### SIP or lumpsum?

If the money comes monthly from a salary, SIP fits naturally. If you already have a large amount, both work — some people park it in a liquid fund and move it into equity slowly using an STP.

#### Does a SIP guarantee profit?

No. It averages your buying price and removes the timing decision. If the market is lower when you need the money, you still lose. Time in the market improves your chances, not the SIP itself.

#### What return should I assume?

Many people use 10–12% for long-term equity and 6–7% for debt. These are numbers for a calculator, not promises.

#### My fund did badly for one year. Should I exit?

First compare it with its own benchmark and its own category for the same period. If the whole category fell, your fund did not fail — that asset class had a bad year, which happens regularly.

#### Should I switch from Regular to Direct?

Usually yes, if nobody is really advising you. But it is a sale and a fresh purchase — tax may apply, exit load may apply, and the holding period restarts.

#### Should I increase my SIP every year?

Yes, if your income increases. This is one of the most powerful things you can do, and many platforms raise the amount automatically.

#### Is a New Fund Offer a good chance?

There is nothing to check — no track record, no rolling returns, no history of falls. The ₹10 price is not a discount.

#### What if the fund company shuts down?

Your money belongs to the trust, not the company. The funds get transferred to another company, or closed with the money paid back to unit holders.

#### What happens to my funds if I die?

A registered nominee can claim them fairly simply. Without a nominee, legal heirs must produce succession documents, which takes far longer. This is why the nominee step matters.

#### Can NRIs invest in Indian mutual funds?

Generally yes, using NRE or NRO accounts with completed KYC. But some fund houses do not accept investors from certain countries, and the tax rules differ.

## Key takeaways

- A mutual fund pools money so your ₹6,000 owns a piece of fifty companies instead of half a share of one.
- NAV is not a price tag. A ₹10 NAV is not cheaper than ₹250 — only the percentage rise matters.
- Always choose **Direct** and **Growth**. The 1% commission gap costs about ₹12.5 lakh over twenty years on a ₹10,000 monthly SIP.
- Before your first fund: emergency fund, health insurance, and clear any credit card balance.
- The year you need the money decides the fund type. Anything within three years should not be in equity.
- Past performance is a character check, not a forecast. The fund's advertised return is not your return — yours is the XIRR on your statement.
- Two to four funds is enough. Each equity fund already holds 40 to 60 companies.
- The fall is not the risk. Selling during the fall is the risk.
