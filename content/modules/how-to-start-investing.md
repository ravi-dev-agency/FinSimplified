## 5.1 – Why you have to invest at all

Most people think investing is about getting rich. It is not. For the majority of us it is about something much more basic: **not getting quietly poorer every year.**

Here is the problem, and it is the only reason this chapter exists.

Money sitting still loses value. Not in a crash, not in a single bad week — slowly, invisibly, every single year, while the number in your bank account stays exactly the same or even goes up.

That is inflation, and it is the opponent.

### What inflation actually does to your money

Inflation is the rate at which things get more expensive. India has averaged somewhere around 5–6% a year over long periods, though it moves about.

Take 6% and put ₹1,00,000 in a drawer.

| After | The number in the drawer | What it actually buys |
|---|---|---|
| Today | ₹1,00,000 | ₹1,00,000 |
| 5 years | ₹1,00,000 | ₹74,726 |
| 10 years | ₹1,00,000 | ₹55,839 |
| 20 years | ₹1,00,000 | ₹31,180 |

*Table 5.1 — the same ₹1,00,000, at 6% inflation. The number never changes. The money does.*

Twenty years of doing nothing costs you about **69% of what that money could buy**. Nobody stole it. No market crashed. You simply held cash while everything else got more expensive.

Turn it around and it is even clearer. Something that costs ₹100 today costs about ₹134 in five years, ₹179 in ten, ₹321 in twenty, and ₹574 in thirty.

![Cash held for twenty years keeps the same number while its purchasing power falls by two thirds](/diagrams/inflation-erosion.svg)

*Fig 5.1 — the balance stays flat, the purchasing power falls. This gap is the entire reason to invest.*

> **Do this.** Stop thinking of returns as "profit". Think of them as the speed you need to run just to stay in the same place. Anything above inflation is real progress. Anything below it is a slow loss with a comforting bank balance.

### The savings account trap

"But my money is in the bank, it earns interest."

A savings account pays roughly 3% a year. Inflation is roughly 6%. You are earning half the rate at which prices rise.

| ₹1,00,000 in a savings account at 3% | The balance | What it buys, after 6% inflation |
|---|---|---|
| After 5 years | ₹1,15,927 | ₹86,628 |
| After 10 years | ₹1,34,392 | ₹75,044 |
| After 20 years | ₹1,80,611 | ₹56,315 |

*Table 5.2 — the balance nearly doubles in twenty years and still loses almost half its value.*

This is the most important idea in the chapter. **Your balance going up does not mean your money is growing.** Watching ₹1,00,000 become ₹1,80,611 over twenty years feels like progress, and it is a 44% loss in what you can actually buy.

### Real return: the only number that matters

Strip out inflation and what is left is the **real return**. That is what your money actually gained.

Roughly: *real return ≈ nominal return − inflation*

At 6% inflation:

| Where the money is | Typical return | Real return |
|---|---|---|
| Savings account | ~3% | about −2.8% |
| Fixed deposit | ~6.5% | about +0.5% |
| PPF | ~7.1% | about +1.0% |
| Gold, long run | ~8% | about +1.9% |
| Index fund, long run | ~12% | about +5.7% |

*Table 5.3 — indicative only, not promises. Returns vary and so does inflation. Past performance does not guarantee future results.*

Now the part almost nobody accounts for: **tax comes out before inflation does.**

A fixed deposit paying 6.5%, if you are in the 30% slab, leaves about 4.55% after tax. Against 6% inflation that is a real return of **about −1.37%**.

A fixed deposit can lose you purchasing power while the balance grows. That is not an argument against FDs — they are the right tool for short-term money, as you will see in 5.6. It is an argument against using them for *everything*.

> **Careful.** Nothing here says "safe is bad". Safe is correct for money you need soon. The mistake is treating safe as permanently free of risk, when holding cash for twenty years carries the near-certainty of losing a third to two-thirds of its value.

## 5.2 – Before you invest a single rupee

Investing before the base is in place is how people end up selling at the worst possible moment.

Three things come first. Not because they earn well — two of them earn nothing — but because each one stops a predictable event from forcing you to sell your investments at a loss.

| Comes first | Why it comes before investing |
|---|---|
| **Emergency fund** | Without it, the first job loss or car repair is funded by selling investments, often in a falling market — see [Chapter 2](/learn/emergency-fund) |
| **Health insurance** | One hospital bill can run to several lakh. Without cover it comes out of your portfolio, or a loan — see [Chapter 4](/learn/health-insurance) |
| **Term insurance** | Only if someone depends on your income. It protects them from your absence, not you — see [Chapter 3](/learn/term-insurance) |

*Table 5.4 — the three things that come before your first investment*

![Emergency fund, health cover and term cover form a base, with investments resting on top of it](/diagrams/before-investing.svg)

*Fig 5.2 — investments sit on top of a base. Without the base, the first shock pulls the whole thing down.*

### Why this order is not negotiable

An investment only works if you can leave it alone. Equity falls 30% in a bad year and recovers over the following years — but only for people who did not sell during the fall.

What forces people to sell? Almost never the market itself. It is a job loss, a hospital admission, a family emergency. The emergency fund and health cover exist precisely so that a bad month does not reach into your investments.

**Skipping the base does not make you an aggressive investor. It makes you a forced seller.**

### And clear expensive debt first

One more, and this one is arithmetic rather than judgement.

A credit card charges roughly 36–45% a year. There is no investment that reliably returns that. Paying off a card balance is a guaranteed, tax-free return equal to the interest you stop paying.

| You have | Do this first |
|---|---|
| Credit card balance at ~42% | Clear it. Nothing you invest in beats that |
| Personal loan at ~14% | Usually clear it before investing |
| Home loan at ~8.5% | Reasonable to invest alongside it |

*Table 5.5 — expensive debt outranks investing*

If you carry a card balance, clearing it *is* your best investment. See [Chapter 12](/learn/credit-cards).

> **Do this before anything else in this chapter.** One month of expenses saved, health cover in place, term cover if anyone depends on you, and no credit card balance. That is the starting line.

### Ramu, who has just reached that line

You met Ramu in [Chapter 1](/learn/budgeting) and [Chapter 2](/learn/emergency-fund). He is 26, works in Hyderabad, and takes home ₹58,000 a month.

It took him two years, but he now has four months of expenses in a liquid fund, health cover through his employer plus a small policy of his own, and no card balance. Nobody depends on his income yet, so he has skipped term insurance for now.

For the first time he has money left over and no idea where to put it. His month looks like this:

| | |
|---|---|
| Essentials — rent, groceries, bike EMI, transport, bills, insurance | ₹32,789 |
| Eating out, shopping, subscriptions | ₹17,211 |
| **Left to invest** | **₹8,000** |

*Table 5.6 — Ramu's month, trimmed. The ₹8,000 at the bottom is what this chapter is about.*

He is the person the rest of this chapter is written for. We will come back to him in 5.15 and put that ₹8,000 somewhere.

## 5.3 – What return and risk actually mean

Before the categories, two words need to mean something precise.

### Return

Return is what your money earns, as a percentage per year. Three kinds matter.

| Type | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Nominal return** | The headline number. "This fund returned 12%" |
| **Post-tax return** | What is left after tax. Depends on your slab and the product |
| **Real return** | Post-tax, minus inflation. The only one that says whether you actually gained |

*Table 5.7 — three returns, and only the last is real*

### Risk

Risk is not "the chance of losing everything". For anything in this chapter that is close to zero. Risk means **how much the value moves about, and how long it can stay down.**

An index fund does not simply return 12%. It returns an *average* of roughly 12%, made of years like +38%, −24%, +15%, −9%. The average only shows up for people who stay through the whole run.

| Risk level | What it feels like | Where it belongs |
|---|---|---|
| **Very low** | Value never falls, returns lose to inflation | Money needed within a year |
| **Low** | Small movements, rare small falls | 1–3 years |
| **Medium** | Real falls of 5–15%, recovering in a year or two | 3–5 years |
| **High** | Falls of 30% or more possible, recovery over years | 7 years or more |

*Table 5.8 — risk is about movement and time, not about losing everything*

**The trade is simple and it has no exceptions:** returns that beat inflation come with movement you have to sit through. Anything promising high returns without movement is either misunderstood or a fraud.

> **Careful.** If a product offers well above FD returns while calling itself "guaranteed" and "no risk", walk away. Guaranteed high returns do not exist. That combination is the most reliable fraud detector in personal finance.

## 5.4 – The five categories, safest first

Every option in India fits one of five tiers. This is the map for the rest of the chapter.

| Tier | What is in it | Typical return | Can it fall? | For money needed in |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Risk-free** | Savings account, liquid funds | 3–6.5% | No | Days to 1 year |
| **2. Fixed income** | FD, RD, debt funds | 6.5–8% | Barely | 1–3 years |
| **3. Government schemes** | PPF, EPF, NPS, SSY | 7–8% | No, but locked | Very long, or retirement |
| **4. Hybrid and gold** | Balanced funds, gold | 8–10% | Moderately | 3–7 years |
| **5. Equity** | Index funds, ETFs, mutual funds, shares | ~12% long run | Yes, sharply | 7 years or more |

*Table 5.9 — the whole landscape. Returns are indicative, not promises.*

![Five tiers rising from risk-free to equity, with returns and volatility increasing together](/diagrams/investment-ladder.svg)

*Fig 5.3 — as you climb, returns rise and so does how much the value moves.*

Notice the shape of it. **Nothing in tiers 1 to 3 will make you wealthy, and nothing in tier 5 is safe over a short period.** Both are supposed to be true at once. That is not a flaw in the system — it is the system.

## 5.5 – Tier 1: risk-free

Money that cannot fall in value. You will not build wealth here, and that is not what it is for.

**Savings account.** Instant access, no risk, roughly 3%. Correct for money you are about to spend and money you might suddenly need. Wrong for anything else, for every reason in 5.1.

**Liquid funds.** A mutual fund holding very short-term instruments. Returns are typically closer to FD rates than savings rates, and money usually reaches your account in a working day.

| | Savings account | Liquid fund |
|---|---|---|
| Typical return | ~3% | ~6–7% |
| Access | Instant | Usually 1 working day |
| Can it fall? | No | Very rarely, very slightly |
| Good for | Spending money, part of the emergency fund | The larger part of an emergency fund |

*Table 5.10 — the two places short-term money belongs*

> **Do this.** Keep about one month of expenses in the savings account for instant access and the rest of the emergency fund in a liquid fund. You get most of the return without giving up practical access.

## 5.6 – Tier 2: fixed income

You lend money and get a fixed, known return. The value barely moves. Here the opponents are inflation and tax, not volatility.

### Fixed deposits

The most familiar product in India. You lock money for a chosen period at a rate fixed on day one.

**What people get wrong:** FD interest is taxed at your slab rate every year, whether or not you withdraw it. At the 30% slab a 6.5% FD returns about 4.55% after tax — below inflation, as shown in 5.1.

| FDs are right for | FDs are wrong for |
|---|---|
| Money needed in 1–3 years | Long-term wealth building |
| A known expense with a fixed date | Retirement, decades away |
| People who genuinely cannot tolerate any movement | Beating inflation after tax |

*Table 5.11 — the FD is a tool with a narrow, real purpose*

**Recurring deposits** are an FD you feed monthly. Same tax treatment, same role, useful for a fixed goal a year or two away.

**Debt mutual funds** hold bonds and similar instruments. They are more flexible than FDs and can be more tax-efficient in some cases, but they move slightly in value — a debt fund is not quite the fixed certainty an FD is.

## 5.7 – Tier 3: government schemes

Backed by the government, effectively no risk of loss, and in exchange your money is locked up for a long time.

Used correctly these are genuinely good products. The lock-in is the price of the safety and the return, not a defect.

| Scheme | Who it is for | Lock-in |
|---|---|---|
| **PPF** | Anyone wanting safe, tax-favoured long-term money | 15 years, partial withdrawal permitted later |
| **EPF** | Salaried employees, deducted automatically | Until retirement or specified conditions |
| **NPS** | Retirement specifically | Until 60, with a portion going to an annuity |
| **SSY** | Parents of a daughter | Long, tied to the child's age |

*Table 5.12 — the main government schemes and what each is for*

*[VERIFY: current PPF, EPF, NPS and SSY interest rates, annual contribution limits, tax treatment, and the exact withdrawal and loan rules before publishing. These change, and the "last checked" date must be true.]*

> **Careful.** A long lock-in is fine for money that genuinely has a long horizon. It is a serious problem for money you may need sooner. Never put emergency fund money into a scheme with a lock-in, whatever the rate.

## 5.8 – Tier 4: hybrid funds and gold

**Hybrid funds** hold both equity and debt. They move less than pure equity and more than pure debt, which suits a 3–7 year horizon where equity is too volatile and an FD too slow.

### Gold, honestly

Indians own a great deal of gold, mostly as jewellery, and mostly not as an investment.

Gold holds value against inflation over long periods. It produces nothing — no interest, no dividend, no earnings — so its price moves on demand and sentiment alone. It can go nowhere for years.

| Way to own gold | Worth knowing |
|---|---|
| **Jewellery** | Making charges and purity loss mean you give up a chunk on the way in and out. This is consumption, not investment |
| **Gold ETF** | Tracks the gold price, held in a demat account, no storage worry |
| **Gold mutual fund** | Similar exposure without needing a demat account |
| **Coins and bars** | Storage and security become your problem |

*Table 5.13 — four ways to hold gold, and only some are investments*

A common guideline is to keep gold to a modest slice of a portfolio — enough to diversify, not enough to depend on. It is a stabiliser, not an engine.

> **Do not** count the family jewellery as your gold investment. You already paid making charges on it, and it is rarely actually sold when money is needed. Treat it as possessions and plan separately.

## 5.9 – Tier 5: equity, where the growth actually is

This is the only tier that has reliably beaten inflation by a wide margin over long periods. It is also the only one that can fall 30% and stay down for a year or more.

Both facts are the same fact.

### Why equity grows

Buying equity means owning a small share of real businesses. Those businesses raise prices with inflation, grow, and earn more over time. That is why equity tends to outrun inflation while cash cannot — you own the things whose prices are rising.

### What it costs you

Volatility. Real, uncomfortable and unavoidable.

| A typical long run of equity years | |
|---|---|
| Good years | +15% to +40% |
| Ordinary years | +5% to +12% |
| Bad years | −10% to −30% |

*Table 5.14 — the average is made of years that look nothing like the average*

The "12% long-run return" is what you get by sitting through every one of those years. Someone who sold during the −30% year did not get 12%. They got −30%.

### Four routes into equity

| Route | What you are trusting | Effort | Covered in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Index fund** | The whole market | Almost none | [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds) |
| **ETF** | The whole market, bought on the exchange | Low, plus placing the order well | [Chapter 8](/learn/etfs) |
| **Active mutual fund** | One fund manager's judgement | Low, plus choosing well | [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds) |
| **Direct shares** | Your own research | Very high | [Chapter 6](/learn/stock-market) |

*Table 5.15 — four ways into equity, easiest first*

For almost everyone starting out, an index fund or a large diversified fund bought monthly is the sensible route. Direct shares require genuinely studying companies, and most people who try would have done better in an index fund.

## 5.10 – Compounding, and why starting now beats starting big

Compounding means your returns start earning returns. It is slow, and then it is not.

Take ₹10,000 a month at an assumed 12%, invested until age 60.

| Start at | Years invested | Total you put in | Roughly becomes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age 25 | 35 | ₹42,00,000 | ₹6.5 crore |
| Age 30 | 30 | ₹36,00,000 | ₹3.5 crore |
| Age 35 | 25 | ₹30,00,000 | ₹1.9 crore |

*Table 5.16 — illustration at an assumed 12%. Not a promise; actual returns vary and may be lower.*

Compare the first and last rows. Starting ten years earlier means putting in ₹12,00,000 more — and ending with roughly **₹4.6 crore more**.

![The same monthly amount started at different ages produces very different outcomes](/diagrams/compounding.svg)

*Fig 5.4 — the final years do most of the work, and you only get them by starting early.*

That gap is not because the early starter was cleverer. Their money simply had more years to compound.

> **Do this.** Start with whatever you can sustain, this month. ₹2,000 a month started now beats ₹10,000 a month started in five years, for exactly the reason above. The amount can grow as your salary does.

## 5.11 – What tax does to your returns

Section 5.1 established that only the real return counts — nominal, minus tax, minus inflation. Tax is the part people forget until they sell.

The rule that matters: **different products are taxed differently, and it changes which one is actually better.**

| Where the money is | How the gain is taxed, broadly |
|---|---|
| Savings account interest | Added to your income, taxed at your slab |
| Fixed deposit interest | Added to your income, taxed at your slab, every year |
| Equity funds and shares | Taxed on sale, at different rates for short and long holding |
| PPF | Tax-favoured — this is a large part of its appeal |

*Table 5.17 — the shape of it. Exact rates and thresholds change; see below.*

This is why the FD comparison in 5.1 was so unflattering. FD interest is taxed every year at your slab even if you never withdraw it, while an equity fund is taxed only when you sell — so its gains compound untouched in the meantime.

Two practical consequences:

- **Holding period matters.** Selling an equity investment early is usually taxed more heavily than holding it longer. Another reason the horizon rule in 5.13 pays twice
- **Headline returns are not comparable across products.** A 7% FD and a 7% equity return are not the same thing after tax, and the gap widens the longer you hold

*[VERIFY: current capital gains rates and holding-period thresholds for equity and debt, the tax treatment of dividends, and any exemption limits, before publishing. These have changed more than once in recent years and the "last checked" date must be true.]*

> **Careful.** Never pick an investment on tax alone. A product that saves tax but does not suit your horizon is still the wrong product. Tax is a tiebreaker between suitable options, not the first question.

## 5.12 – Why most people earn less than the fund they own

This section is the difference between knowing about investing and actually ending up with money.

Here is an uncomfortable fact. Take any long-running fund with a good record. The average investor in that fund usually earns **less** than the fund itself returned.

Not because of fees. Because of when they bought and when they sold.

The fund's return assumes you held it the whole time. Real people buy after a good year, and sell after a bad one. They get the falls without the recoveries.

### What a bad run actually feels like

Everyone agrees to "stay invested through falls" in theory. Here is the theory made concrete — ₹1,00,000 through five genuinely unpleasant years:

| Year | That year | Your money |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | −24% | ₹76,000 |
| 2 | +5% | ₹79,800 |
| 3 | −8% | ₹73,416 |
| 4 | +31% | ₹96,175 |
| 5 | +18% | ₹1,13,486 |

*Table 5.18 — five years, an illustration. Ends up 13% ahead, which is about 2.6% a year.*

Read year 3 again. Two years in, ₹1,00,000 has become ₹73,416, and the recovery has not started. That is where people give up — not at the bottom of year 1, but in year 3 when it has dragged on and nothing seems to be working.

The person who sold in year 3 locked in a 27% loss. The person who held got 2.6% a year, which is poor — and still far better.

> **Careful.** A fall needs a bigger rise to undo it. Down 20% needs +25% to get back. Down 30% needs +43%. Down 50% needs +100%. This is why avoiding the panic-sell matters more than picking the right fund.

### What it costs to flinch

Back to Ramu. Say he invests ₹4,000 a month in an index fund for 20 years at an assumed 12%.

| What Ramu does | After 20 years |
|---|---|
| Keeps investing every month | about ₹39,96,592 |
| Stops for 2 years after a crash, then resumes | about ₹36,36,939 |
| Sells everything in the fall, restarts 2 years later | about ₹9,29,356 |

*Table 5.19 — illustration at an assumed 12%. Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

Look at the middle row. Pausing for two years means ₹96,000 not invested — and it costs him about **₹3,59,652**.

Now the bottom row. Selling in the fall and coming back two years later costs him roughly **₹30 lakh**. He owned the right fund the whole time. The fund was never the problem.

**This is the single biggest reason people end up with less than they expected.** Not choosing the wrong fund. Not fees. Not tax. Simply not staying.

### What actually helps

- **Automate it.** A monthly auto-debit on a fixed date removes the decision, and the decision is what goes wrong
- **Do not check daily.** Checking a long-term investment every day gives you many chances to panic and no useful information
- **Write down why you started**, and the year you need the money. Read it when markets fall
- **Expect the falls.** A 30% fall is not a sign something broke. It is the normal cost of the returns in tier 5
- **Keep the emergency fund topped up**, so a bad month never forces a sale

> **Do this.** Decide now, while markets are calm, what you will do when your investment falls 30%. The honest answer should be "nothing". If it is not, you have too much in tier 5 — fix that today, not during the fall.

## 5.13 – How to choose: let the year decide

One rule does most of the work. **The date you need the money decides where it goes.** Not your age, not your income, not how confident you feel this week.

| Money needed in | Where it belongs |
|---|---|
| Under 1 year | Savings account, liquid fund |
| 1 to 3 years | FD, short duration debt fund |
| 3 to 5 years | Conservative or balanced hybrid fund |
| 5 to 10 years | Index fund, large cap fund |
| 10 years or more | Index fund, flexi cap, add mid or small cap if you can hold through falls |
| Retirement, decades away | Equity funds, EPF, NPS, PPF together |

*Table 5.20 — the year decides, not your mood*

![Money needed soon belongs in safe products, and only long-horizon money belongs in equity](/diagrams/horizon-map.svg)

*Fig 5.5 — match the holding period to the goal, not to how you feel about markets.*

**Why this works.** Equity falls hard and recovers over years. If your horizon is long, a fall is something you sit through. If your horizon is eighteen months, the same fall forces you to sell at the bottom — the date does not move because the market had a bad year.

> **Do this.** Write the goal and the year next to it before choosing anything. "₹8 lakh for a down payment in 4 years" answers the question. "I want good returns" does not.

## 5.14 – A sensible order to start in

1. **One month of expenses** in a savings account
2. **Health insurance** for you and your family
3. **Term insurance**, if anyone depends on your income
4. **Clear credit card and personal loan balances**
5. **Emergency fund to three to six months**, the bulk of it in a liquid fund
6. **Start a monthly investment** in one index fund or large diversified fund — the amount matters less than beginning
7. **Add a long-term safe layer** — PPF or NPS, if the lock-in genuinely suits you
8. **Increase the monthly amount** every time your salary rises

Most people do not need a complicated portfolio. They need the right things in the right order.

## 5.15 – Ramu's ₹8,000, actually allocated

Everything so far has been principles. Here is what they look like as a decision.

Ramu has ₹8,000 spare each month. He is 26, has four months of expenses saved, no dependants, and no debt. He wants to buy a house "some day" and retire eventually — nothing with a fixed date in the next three years.

Working down the tiers:

| Where | How much | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Liquid fund** | ₹2,000 | Takes his emergency fund from four months to six, then stops |
| **Index fund, monthly** | ₹4,000 | No goal inside 7 years, so this belongs in tier 5 |
| **PPF** | ₹2,000 | A safe long-term layer. He is comfortable with the lock-in |

*Table 5.21 — one example allocation, for one person's situation. Not a recommendation.*

**Why this shape.** He has no short-horizon goal, so almost nothing goes to tier 2 — an FD would just lose to inflation after tax. Most of it goes to equity because his horizon is decades, which is the only reason equity is appropriate. The PPF adds a floor that does not move when markets do.

**What changes it.** If Ramu were saving for a flat deposit in three years, that money would move to tier 2 and the index fund amount would shrink — the year decides, exactly as in 5.13. If his parents depended on his income, term insurance would come before any of this.

Once the liquid fund reaches six months, that ₹2,000 moves to the index fund. And each time his salary rises, the monthly investment rises with it.

> **Careful.** This is an illustration of a method, not advice. Your numbers, goals, dependants and comfort with falls are different from Ramu's, and the right allocation follows from those — not from copying this table.

## 5.16 – How to actually start, this week

Everything above is useless if the account never opens. The practical steps, in order:

1. **Complete KYC once.** PAN, Aadhaar, a bank account and a short online process. It is a one-time thing and every platform then works
2. **Pick one platform** — your bank, a fund house's own site, or an investment app. Direct plans cost less than regular plans over time; see [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds)
3. **Choose one fund.** One broad index fund is a complete starting portfolio. Not five funds. One
4. **Set a monthly auto-debit** for a date just after your salary arrives, so it happens before you can spend it
5. **Start with an amount you will not want to stop.** Smaller and permanent beats larger and abandoned
6. **Then leave it alone.** Check once a quarter at most

That is the whole process. It takes an evening.

> **Do this.** Pick the date and the amount before you close this page. "I will start investing" fails. "₹3,000 on the 3rd of every month, into one index fund" happens.

## 5.17 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **Keeping everything in a savings account** | Feels safe, loses about a third of its value every decade |

## 5.18 – Questions people actually ask

#### How much do I need to start?

Whatever you can continue every month. Many funds allow ₹500.

The amount matters far less than starting and not stopping. ₹1,000 a month for twenty years beats ₹10,000 a month for three.

#### Is investing the same as the stock market?

No. The stock market is one part of it — tier 5. A fixed deposit, PPF and a liquid fund are all investments and none of them touch the share market.

Where your money belongs depends on when you need it, not on which product sounds most like investing.

#### What return should I expect?

Honestly, it depends on the tier, and nobody can tell you in advance.

Broadly, safe products roughly match or slightly beat inflation, and equity has historically beaten it by a good margin over long periods with large falls along the way. Anyone quoting a precise future number is guessing or selling.

Past performance does not guarantee future results.

#### Should I invest if I have a home loan?

Usually yes, alongside it. A home loan at around 8.5% is not the emergency that a 42% credit card is.

Clear expensive debt first, then run investing and the home loan together.

#### Is it a bad time to invest right now?

Nobody knows, including everyone who says they do.

This is exactly why monthly investing exists — you buy at many different prices instead of betting everything on one. If your horizon is long, the entry date matters far less than how long you stay.

#### Can I lose all my money?

In a diversified index or mutual fund, effectively no. That would require every major company in the country failing at once.

You can certainly watch it fall 30% temporarily. Permanent loss happens when someone sells during that fall, which is a decision rather than a market event.

#### Where does gold fit?

As a modest stabiliser, not an engine. It holds value against inflation over long periods but produces no income and can stagnate for years.

And jewellery is consumption, however much it is worth.

#### Should I invest or repay my loan?

Compare the loan rate against a realistic expected return, and give weight to certainty. Clearing a 42% card is a guaranteed 42% return, which is unbeatable. Clearing an 8.5% home loan competes with an uncertain ~12% from equity, so reasonable people do both.

The higher the loan rate, the more obvious repaying becomes.

#### What if I need the money before my goal?

That is what the emergency fund prevents. It exists so that normal life does not reach into your investments.

If you genuinely might need a sum within a year, it does not belong in equity at all. It belongs in tier 1, however tempting the returns above it look.

#### Do I need a financial advisor?

For the plan in 5.14, no. It is deliberately simple enough to run yourself.

An advisor becomes useful with real complexity — business income, property, estate planning, several dependants. If you do use one, prefer a fee-based advisor, so you are paying for advice rather than for a product sale.

## Key takeaways

- Investing is not about getting rich. It is about not getting quietly poorer, because inflation takes roughly a third of your money's value every decade.
- At 6% inflation, ₹1,00,000 held as cash buys about ₹55,839 of goods after ten years and ₹31,180 after twenty.
- A savings account at 3% against 6% inflation loses value every year, even though the balance rises.
- Only the real return counts: nominal, minus tax, minus inflation. A 6.5% FD at the 30% slab works out to about −1.37% in real terms.
- Emergency fund, health insurance and term insurance come before any investment. Without them the first emergency forces you to sell at the worst time.
- Clear credit card debt first. Nothing you invest in reliably beats 42% a year.
- Higher returns always come with more movement. Guaranteed high returns do not exist.
- The year you need the money decides where it goes — not your age, your income, or how the market feels this week.
- Start now with a small amount rather than later with a large one. Starting at 25 instead of 35 can mean several times the final corpus.
- Most people earn less than the fund they own, because they sell during falls. Staying invested matters more than picking the right fund.
- Automate the monthly investment and check it rarely. The decision is what goes wrong, so remove the decision.
