## 1.1 – The question nobody can answer

Ask almost anyone what they earn and they will tell you exactly. Ask what they spent last month and you get a pause, then a guess, then a number that is usually wrong by twenty per cent.

That gap is the entire problem this chapter solves.

You are not bad with money. You simply have no record of where it went, and you cannot fix what you cannot see. Most people discover, the first time they actually look, that the leak is not where they assumed.

> **What this chapter is not**
>
> It is not a demand that you give up coffee, or track every ten rupees in a spreadsheet you will abandon by the 14th. Those systems fail because they take effort every single day. What follows takes about twenty minutes once, and ten minutes a month after that.

**This chapter will not tell you what to stop buying.** I do not know what your money is for, and nobody writing an article does. What you spend on your family, your food, your weekends — that is yours to decide.

What I can do is help you *see* it. Almost everyone who writes down one honest month has the same reaction: not guilt, but surprise. The number is somewhere they did not expect.

Once you can see it, you decide what to change. Or nothing — some people look, find they are comfortable with every rupee, and simply start saving before spending instead of after. That counts as a finished chapter too.

## 1.2 – The three buckets

Every rupee that leaves your account falls into one of three buckets. That is the whole framework.

| Bucket | What goes in | Can you change it this month? |
|---|---|---|
| **Fixed** | Rent, EMIs, insurance premiums, school fees, subscriptions | No. These are locked in |
| **Living** | Groceries, electricity, transport, phone, medicines | Somewhat, with effort |
| **Free** | Eating out, shopping, films, trips, gifts | Yes. This is the bucket you control |

*Table 1.1 — the three buckets*

Most people believe their problem is the fixed bucket — the rent is too high, the EMI is too big. Usually it is not. The fixed bucket is large but predictable, and you already knew about it.

The money that is hardest to account for is almost always in the third bucket, in amounts small enough that none of them felt like a decision. That is not a failing — it is just how small, frequent spending works. It is also why writing it down changes anything.

## 1.3 – Find your real numbers in twenty minutes

You do not need an app. You need your bank statement for last month, and your credit card statement if you have one.

Open both. Go through every line and write it into one of the three buckets. That is it.

Two rules that make this work:

- **Use last month, not a typical month.** There is no typical month. Whatever last month was, that is real data
- **Do not tidy the numbers.** If you spent ₹4,380 on food delivery, write ₹4,380. Rounding it to ₹4,000 is how you end up with a budget that does not match your bank balance

> **Careful.** Do this from statements, not memory. Memory reliably undercounts the free bucket, because each individual purchase felt too small to remember. The statement does not have that problem.

### What Ramu found

Ramu earns ₹58,000 a month after tax. He assumed he was saving around ₹15,000. Here is what one month actually looked like:

| Bucket | Item | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed | Rent | ₹16,000 |
| Fixed | Bike EMI | ₹4,200 |
| Fixed | Health insurance premium | ₹1,850 |
| Living | Groceries | ₹6,400 |
| Living | Electricity and water | ₹1,340 |
| Living | Transport and fuel | ₹2,100 |
| Living | Phone and internet | ₹899 |
| Free | Food delivery and eating out | ₹7,260 |
| Free | Shopping | ₹5,400 |
| Free | Subscriptions (four of them) | ₹1,847 |
| Free | Weekend trip | ₹3,900 |
| | **Total spent** | **₹51,196** |
| | **Actually left** | **₹6,804** |

*Table 1.2 — one real month, written down*

Ramu thought he saved ₹15,000. He saved ₹6,804.

The difference was not rent or the EMI. It was ₹18,407 in the free bucket — nearly a third of his salary — arriving in pieces of ₹200 and ₹400 that never felt like spending.

## 1.4 – Pay yourself first

Here is the single change that does more than every other tip combined.

Most people save what is left at the end of the month. There is never anything left. Not because they are careless, but because spending expands to fill whatever is in the account.

So reverse it. **On the day your salary arrives, move your savings out before you spend anything.**

| The usual way | The way that works |
|---|---|
| Salary arrives → spend the month → save what remains | Salary arrives → move savings out → spend what remains |
| Savings are whatever is left over | Savings are a fixed bill you pay to yourself |
| Some months nothing is left | Every month the amount goes out |

*Table 1.3 — the order matters more than the amount*

> **Do this on payday.** Set an automatic transfer for the day after your salary lands. Send it to a separate account at a bank you do not use daily — same-day access if you need it, but enough friction that you do not spend it by accident.

**How much?** Start with whatever you are already saving, rounded up slightly. Ramu was saving ₹6,804 by accident; he set up ₹8,000 by instruction. That small increase was absorbed by the free bucket without him feeling deprived.

Do not start at ₹20,000 because an article said to save 30%. An amount you cancel in month two is worse than a smaller amount you keep for five years.

## 1.5 – The order money should move each month

Knowing where your salary goes is only half of it. The other half is knowing what order to send it in — because the order decides whether anything survives.

Most people work down this list in roughly the reverse direction. They invest first because investing feels like progress, then discover there is no cushion when the car breaks, and the investment gets sold at whatever price the market offers that week.

![The order money should move: salary, fixed bills, savings out on payday, emergency fund, insurance, clearing costly debt, then investing](/diagrams/salary-flow.svg)

*Fig 1.2 — the same six steps, every month. Each one protects the ones below it.*

Here is why that order and not another:

| Step | Why it comes here |
|---|---|
| **1. Fixed bills** | Already committed. Nothing below happens until these clear |
| **2. Move savings out** | Do it today, not at month end. Every step below depends on money actually leaving the account |
| **3. Emergency fund** | Without a cushion, the first bad month goes onto a credit card and undoes everything |
| **4. Insurance** | A hospital bill or a death can wipe out a decade of saving in a week. [Health](/learn/health-insurance) before [term](/learn/term-insurance) — illness is likelier than death |
| **5. Clear expensive debt** | A credit card at 36–42% a year beats any return you will earn investing. Paying it off *is* the best investment available to you |
| **6. Invest** | Only now. With a cushion underneath and cover in place, you can leave this money alone for ten years — which is the only way investing actually works |

*Table 1.4 — the order, and the reason for it*

> **Careful.** You do not need to finish one step before starting the next. One month of expenses saved is enough to begin looking at health insurance. The order is about priority when money is short, not a set of locked gates.

> **Do not** skip to step six because the market is doing well. Investing without a cushion is not investing — it is a savings account you will be forced to empty at the worst possible moment.

## 1.6 – The 50-30-20 rule, and why it often fails in India

You will meet this rule everywhere: 50% on needs, 30% on wants, 20% on savings.

It is a reasonable starting point and a poor rule.

The problem is rent. In Bengaluru, Mumbai, Hyderabad or Pune, a single earner can pay 35–40% of take-home on rent alone. Add an EMI and the fixed bucket is past 50% before a single need is met. The rule then tells you that you have failed, which is neither true nor useful.

> **Careful.** Any rule quoted as a percentage was probably written for a different city, a different rent, and a different salary. Use it as a direction to move in, not a test you pass or fail.

A more honest version:

| Your situation | A realistic savings target |
|---|---|
| Rent is under 25% of take-home | 20% or more |
| Rent is 25–35% | 10–15%, rising as income grows |
| Rent is over 35% | 5–10%, and treat raising income as the real fix |
| Supporting parents or siblings | Whatever remains after that, without guilt |

*Table 1.5 — savings targets that account for where you actually live*

The direction matters more than the number. Saving 8% consistently beats saving 20% for two months and stopping.

## 1.7 – Where the money usually leaks

Six patterns come up again and again. None of them is dramatic, which is exactly why they persist.

| The leak | What it looks like | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Subscriptions you forgot** | Four or five small amounts, auto-debited, one barely used | List every recurring debit on your statement. Cancel anything you did not consciously use last month |
| **Food delivery** | ₹250 at a time, twenty times a month | Do not ban it. Set a number — say eight orders a month — and count them |
| **Sale buying** | ₹5,400 of things you did not plan to buy | Keep a list. If an item is still on it after a week, buy it. Most items do not survive the week |
| **The convenience tax** | Auto instead of bus, instant delivery fees | Fine when you choose it. Expensive when it becomes the default |
| **Paying for the same thing twice** | Two music apps, two cloud storage plans | Look for duplicate categories in your subscription list |
| **Untracked cash** | ₹2,000 withdrawn, no memory of where it went | Cash is invisible to statements. Use the card for anything you want to be able to see later |

*Table 1.6 — six leaks, and the fix for each*

> **Do not** try to fix all six in one month. Pick the largest one. For most people that is food delivery or subscriptions, and dealing with just that recovers more than a month of careful economising everywhere else.

## 1.8 – The ten-minute monthly check

Once the setup is done, this is all the maintenance the system needs.

Once a month, on a fixed date — the day after payday works well — open your statement and answer three questions:

1. **Did the savings transfer go out?** If it failed, fix it today
2. **What was the largest free-bucket item?** Not to feel guilty about, just to notice
3. **Anything recurring I did not expect?** New subscriptions have a way of appearing

That is the whole review. Ten minutes, twelve times a year.

> **Do this.** Put it in your calendar as a repeating event with a reminder. A system that depends on you remembering is a system that stops in March.

## 1.9 – What to do with what you save

Saving without a destination tends to drift back into spending. Send it somewhere with a job, in this order:

1. **One month of expenses** in a separate savings account — the first cushion
2. **Three to six months**, which is the [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) in the next chapter
3. **Insurance premiums** for the year, so they never arrive as a shock
4. **Then invest**, once the first three are genuinely in place

Ramu's ₹8,000 a month reaches one month of expenses in about six months, and a full emergency fund in a bit over two years. That sounds slow. It is not — at three months saved he can survive a job change, and the fund is doing its job long before it is finished.

## 1.10 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tracking every rupee** | The spreadsheet with forty categories gets abandoned by week three. Three buckets survive |
| **Budgeting from memory** | Memory undercounts the free bucket every time. Use statements |
| **Saving what is left** | There is never anything left. Move it on payday instead |
| **Starting too aggressive** | A ₹20,000 target you cancel in month two loses to ₹5,000 you keep for five years |
| **Keeping savings in the salary account** | Money in the account you spend from gets spent. This is an account design problem, not a discipline problem |
| **Treating a percentage rule as a verdict** | 50-30-20 was not written for a ₹16,000 rent on a ₹58,000 salary |
| **Giving up after one bad month** | A wedding month or a medical month is not a failed system. Restart next month |

## 1.11 – Questions people actually ask

#### I have tried budgeting apps and always stop. Why would this work?

Because there is nothing daily to do.

Apps fail for the same reason gym memberships fail — they demand a small effort every single day, and one missed day makes the record useless, so you stop. Three buckets and one monthly review demand effort twelve times a year.

If you already use an app and it works for you, keep it. This chapter is for everyone who has abandoned three of them.

#### My income is irregular. I am freelance or on commission.

Then budget on your *worst* recent month, not your average.

Take the lowest month from the past year and treat that as your baseline income. Fixed and living buckets must fit inside it. In good months, the surplus goes straight to savings rather than raising your spending — the trap with irregular income is letting a good quarter reset what feels normal.

Aim for a larger cushion than a salaried person too. Nine months of expenses rather than six, because your gap between payments is less predictable.

#### What if my expenses are already more than my income?

Then this is the most useful chapter on the site, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: you can only cut spending or raise income, and cutting has a floor.

Work in this order. Cancel everything in the free bucket that is recurring. Then look at the largest fixed item — usually rent — because one change there is worth twenty small economies. A ₹2,000 cheaper flat saves ₹24,000 a year without a single daily decision.

If credit card debt is involved, that comes before saving anything at all. At 36–42% a year it is the most expensive money in your life.

#### Should I save or repay my loan first?

Depends entirely on the interest rate.

Credit card debt at 36–42% a year — repay first, always. No investment reliably beats that.

Personal loan at 14–18% — repay first in most cases.

Home loan at 8–9% — build the emergency fund first, then decide. A home loan is the cheapest borrowing most people get.

The exception: keep one month of expenses aside even while repaying. Without it, the next emergency goes back onto the card and you have gained nothing.

#### How do I budget when I support my parents?

Put it in the fixed bucket, with the rent and the EMI. Not the free bucket.

It is not discretionary spending and treating it as such creates a quiet guilt every time you look at your numbers. It is a commitment you have made, it sits with your other commitments, and your savings target adjusts around it.

#### My partner and I have separate accounts. How does this work?

Do the exercise separately first, then together. Both of you need to see your own numbers before you can see the shared ones.

Then agree three things: which bills come from which account, one shared savings goal, and a threshold above which you discuss a purchase before making it. That threshold matters more than the exact figure — for some couples it is ₹5,000, for others ₹500.

#### Where should I keep the money I save?

For the first month of expenses and the emergency fund, a separate savings account at a bank you do not use daily. Same-day access, no market risk, and the small friction of a different login stops casual spending.

Once that is in place, longer-term money belongs elsewhere — that is what the investing chapters cover.

#### Is it worth budgeting if I only earn ₹20,000 a month?

More, not less. When the margin is thin, a ₹1,500 leak matters far more than it does on ₹1,00,000.

The buckets are the same. The savings amount will be smaller — perhaps ₹1,000 to start — and that is fine. The habit is what compounds, and the amount grows every time your income does.

#### What about festivals, weddings and one-off expenses?

Plan for them, because they are not surprises. You know roughly when Diwali falls and roughly how many weddings a year you attend.

Add a line to the fixed bucket — say ₹2,000 a month into a separate "annual expenses" pot. It covers festival spending, gifts, insurance premiums and the yearly bills that otherwise arrive as a shock and get charged to a card.

## Key takeaways

- You cannot fix what you cannot see. One month of statements, sorted into three buckets, shows you more than any app.
- Fixed, living, free. The unexplained money is almost always in the free bucket, arriving in amounts too small to feel like decisions.
- Move your savings out on payday, before you spend. Saving what is left over never works, because nothing is ever left.
- Start with an amount you will not cancel. Consistency beats size.
- Percentage rules like 50-30-20 were not written for Indian city rents. Use them as a direction, not a verdict.
- Fix the biggest leak first rather than economising everywhere at once.
- Ten minutes a month is the entire maintenance cost of this system.
