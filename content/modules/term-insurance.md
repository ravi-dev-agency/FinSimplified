## 3.1 – What term insurance is

You pay a small amount every year to an insurance company.

If you die during the agreed years, the company pays your family **one large amount of money**.

That is the whole product. Nothing more complicated than that.

### A simple example

**Arun is 32.** He lives in Hyderabad, earns ₹12 lakh a year, and has a wife, a small daughter, and a ₹40 lakh home loan. (Ramu from the earlier chapters is 26 and single — nobody depends on his income, so he does not need this yet.)

He buys a term plan:

| | |
|---|---|
| Cover | **₹2.5 crore** |
| Runs until | **Age 60** (28 years) |
| He pays | **About ₹18,000 a year** — roughly ₹1,500 a month |

*Table 3.1 — the three numbers that make up a term plan*

**If Arun dies at 40:** his wife receives ₹2.5 crore. She clears the home loan. The house is permanently theirs. The rest, invested sensibly, gives the family more each year than they currently spend. His daughter stays in her school.

**If Arun lives to 60:** the policy ends. He gets nothing back. Over 28 years he paid about ₹5 lakh.

### Why do you get nothing back?

Because that is exactly what makes it cheap.

You are not saving money here. You are buying protection for a period of time — like car insurance, or a fire extinguisher. Nobody calls a fire extinguisher a waste because the house never burned.

The moment a policy promises money back, the price jumps two to three times and the cover shrinks dramatically. More on that in section 3.4.

### Why your family needs it

Your family does not depend on you. Your family depends on **your income**.

When you die, your salary stops the same month. Nothing else does.

![Your salary stops on day one, while the home loan EMI, school fees, rent, medicines and groceries all continue](/diagrams/what-stops.svg)

*Fig 3.1 — without cover, families are forced to sell the house, move the child to a cheaper school, or borrow from relatives — while still grieving.*

### Who needs it, and who doesn't

> **If you died next month, would anyone's life get financially harder?**

**Yes — you need it.** A wife or husband, children, parents who depend on you, a home loan, an education loan. Any one of these is enough.

**No — you can skip it.** A student with no dependants. Single with no loans. Retired with settled children and enough savings.

> **Do not** assume your office cover is enough. "My office gives me insurance" ends the day the job ends — and jobs often end because of the very illness you needed cover for. The amount is also small, usually one to three times your salary. Take it as a bonus. Buy your own anyway.

### What term insurance is not

| Term insurance | Is not |
|---|---|
| Protection for your family | A savings plan |
| Pays only if you die | A way to grow money |
| The cheapest form of life cover | A tax-saving scheme |
| Ends with no maturity amount | A retirement product |

*Table 3.2 — what it is, and what it is repeatedly mis-sold as*

If someone offers you life cover that also "gives returns", they are selling you something else. Section 3.4 explains exactly what.

## 3.2 – How much cover you need — the number most people get wrong

This is the most important number in the whole decision. Work it out yourself before you talk to anyone selling anything.

| Step | Your working |
|---|---|
| Yearly income × 15 | |
| + all loans | |
| + children's education | |
| − savings and investments | |

*Table 3.3 — fill this in before reading further*

The multiplier depends on your age, because you have fewer earning years left as you get older:

| Your age | Multiply yearly income by |
|---|---|
| Under 35 | 15–18 times |
| 35–45 | 12–15 times |
| 45–55 | 8–10 times |
| Over 55 | 5–7 times |

*Table 3.4 — income multiples by age*

**Arun's working:** ₹12 lakh × 15 = ₹1.80 crore. Plus ₹40 lakh home loan = ₹2.20 crore. Plus ₹30 lakh for his daughter's education = ₹2.50 crore. Minus ₹8 lakh savings = ₹2.42 crore. Round up to **₹2.5 crore**.

> **Careful — now check your answer.** Divide your cover by 20. That is roughly what your family can spend each year without the money running down.
>
> Arun: ₹2.5 crore ÷ 20 = ₹12.5 lakh a year, about ₹1 lakh a month. His family spends ₹50,000. Comfortable.
>
> Now try the amount most Indians actually buy. **₹50 lakh ÷ 20 = ₹2.5 lakh a year**, or ₹20,000 a month. No family runs on that for twenty years. This is why most people are badly under-insured — ₹50 lakh *sounds* enormous.

**Do not forget inflation.** At 6% a year, ₹1 crore twenty years from now buys roughly what ₹31 lakh buys today — while your family's monthly costs will have roughly tripled. So round up, never down.

> **Do this.** ₹2 crore does not cost double of ₹1 crore. Insurers charge less per lakh at higher amounts. Being generous here is surprisingly cheap.

## 3.3 – For how many years should the policy run?

Until the day you stop earning.

**60 − your age today = your policy term.**

Age 32 → 28 years. Age 40 → 20 years.

**They will offer you cover till age 99.** Ask yourself: at 90, who depends on my salary? Usually nobody — children are earning, loans are cleared. That extra premium belongs in your retirement fund instead.

**But do not go too short either.** A plan ending at 45 leaves you bare while your children are still studying, and buying fresh cover at 45 costs far more than at 32. Buy long, once.

## 3.4 – Which type to buy, and six to refuse

Only one. A **pure term plan**. Search using exactly those words.

Everything else in the life insurance aisle is a mix of insurance and investment, and it does both jobs badly.

| If they say | They're selling |
|---|---|
| "You get all your money back" | Return of premium — 2–3× the cost for the same cover |
| "Insurance plus market returns" | ULIP — small cover, charges eat into returns |
| "Guaranteed savings plan" | Endowment — modest returns, tiny cover |
| "Secure your child's future" | Child plan — ordinary product, emotional name |
| "Lifelong cover till 99" | Whole life — expensive, rarely needed |
| "Loan protection" at the bank | Overpriced. Your term plan already covers the loan |

*Table 3.5 — what the sales language actually means*

See the difference for yourself. Same ₹20,000 a year, age 30:

![Bars to scale: two crore for a pure term plan, seventy lakh for return of premium, five lakh for endowment, three lakh for a ULIP](/diagrams/same-premium.svg)

*Fig 3.2 — same money, same person, same year. The difference in protection is enormous.*

**Why does everyone push the others?** A pure term plan earns the seller almost no commission. Endowment, ULIP and child plans earn a great deal. That single fact explains nearly every insurance conversation you will ever have.

When they say *"but sir, you get nothing back"*, the answer is: *"Yes. That's exactly what I want."*

> **Do this.** Insurance is for protection, investment is for growing money. Never mix them. Buy a term plan to protect, and a mutual fund or PPF to grow.

## 3.5 – Which company to choose, and the ratio nobody checks

Shortlist three. Not twenty. Check three numbers, all free in the IRDAI Annual Report on irdai.gov.in.

| Check | What's good | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Claim settlement ratio **by count** | Above 97%, steady over 3 years | How often they pay |
| Claim settlement ratio **by amount** | Close to the count figure | Whether they pay the *big* ones |
| Solvency ratio | Above 1.5 | Financial strength |

*Table 3.6 — the only three numbers worth comparing*

> **Careful — the trick almost nobody checks.** "By count" treats a ₹2 lakh claim and a ₹2 crore claim as the same event. A company can look spotless while quietly fighting the large claims.
>
> Across the industry in FY 2024-25: about **98.3% paid by count, but about 97.2% by value**. If one company shows a wide gap between those two, be careful — especially if your cover is large.
>
> Also watch volume. A 99.7% ratio built on 300 claims says far less than 97.6% built on eight lakh claims.

Compare the **same cover and the same number of years** across all three, or the comparison means nothing.

## 3.6 – Every option on the buying page, explained

This is where most people get stuck. A real term insurance page throws ten or more choices at you, several with similar-sounding names, and each one quietly changes what you pay and what your family gets.

Here is every option you will meet, and what to pick.

| Option on the form | What to choose |
|---|---|
| **Cover amount** | Your number from section 3.2 |
| **Policy term** | Till around age 60 — your number from 3.3 |
| **Premium paying term** | **Regular pay** — pay every year till the end |
| **Payment frequency** | **Yearly** if you can manage it |
| **Cover type / variant** | **Level cover** — the same amount throughout |
| **Payout to family** | **Lump sum** |
| **Return of premium (TROP)** | **No** |
| **Whole life / cover till 99** | **No** |
| **Special exit value / "zero cost"** | Yes *only if* it is free and does not stretch your term |
| **Terminal illness benefit** | Yes, if included free |
| **Riders / add-ons** | At most two |

*Table 3.8 — the quick answer to every box on the form*

Follow only that table and you will do better than most buyers. The rest of this section explains the four that genuinely confuse people.

### Level, increasing or decreasing cover?

| | |
|---|---|
| **Level cover** | Same amount from day one to the end. **The default. Choose this** |
| **Increasing cover** | Rises 5–10% each year to fight inflation. Costs more upfront. Worth comparing — but often cheaper to simply buy a bigger level cover today |
| **Decreasing cover** | Falls each year, usually designed to track a home loan. Cheap, but your family needs more than just the loan cleared. Avoid as your main policy |
| **Life stage / smart cover** | Lets you raise cover later on marriage, a child, or a home loan — without a fresh medical test. **Genuinely useful if it is free** |

*Table 3.9 — cover variants*

### The "zero cost" option, and its hidden cost

This one is new, heavily advertised, and misunderstood.

**What it is:** a window near the end of your policy — say between ages 60 and 65 — where you can voluntarily exit and get back all the base premiums you paid, minus GST. Unlike return-of-premium, it usually costs **no extra premium**. It is a feature built into certain plans.

**The catch nobody mentions:** insurers generally offer it only on very long policy terms — 35 or 40 years. So to get the exit option, you often have to buy cover till age 70 or 75 instead of 60. That longer term costs more every single year. You may end up paying extra for fifteen years of cover you did not need, in order to get your own money back later.

> **Do this.** Ask for two quotes — your normal term till 60, and the long term with the exit option. If the exit version costs the same or close, take it; it is free flexibility. If it costs noticeably more each year, skip it and buy the plain plan.
>
> Also note: it is **not** a maturity benefit. You must actively exit inside the window. Miss it, and the policy simply runs on and ends with nothing.

### How your family receives the money

| | |
|---|---|
| **Lump sum** | Whole amount at once. **The default. Choose this.** Only this can clear a ₹40 lakh home loan on day one |
| **Monthly income** | Fixed amount monthly for 10–20 years. Only if you genuinely fear your family cannot handle a large sum |
| **Lump sum + monthly** | Part now, rest monthly. Reasonable if you have both a loan to clear and running expenses to fund |
| **Increasing monthly income** | Monthly amount rises each year. Sounds good, but check the total — often less than the plain lump sum |

*Table 3.10 — payout options*

Whatever you pick, **check the total your family actually receives** under each option, not just the monthly figure.

### Can you pay monthly instead of yearly?

Yes. Every insurer lets you pay monthly, quarterly or half-yearly instead of once a year, and monthly is the most popular choice — ₹1,500 a month is far easier to find than ₹18,000 in one go.

But it is not free. Most insurers add a small loading for paying in instalments, so twelve monthly payments usually come to slightly more than one yearly payment for the identical cover. The quote page shows both figures side by side. Compare them before you choose.

| If you pay | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Yearly** | Cheapest overall. One date to remember. Best if you can set aside the amount |
| **Half-yearly or quarterly** | A middle path. Slightly more than yearly, much easier on the pocket |
| **Monthly** | Easiest to afford and the most common choice. Usually costs a little more over the year |

*Table 3.11 — how often to pay*

![Yearly is one payment and cheapest; monthly is twelve small payments plus a small instalment loading](/diagrams/how-often-to-pay.svg)

*Fig 3.3 — same cover either way. The only question is what you can actually keep paying.*

> **Do this if money is tight.** Choose monthly and set auto-debit. A slightly higher premium you actually pay is infinitely better than a cheaper one you miss — and one missed premium can end the cover completely.
>
> If you can manage yearly, there is a simple trick: set aside a twelfth of the premium every month in a separate account, and pay once a year from it. You get the lower price and the monthly cash flow.

> **Careful.** Whichever you choose, the *first* payment must clear before the policy is issued. And monthly mandates fail more often than yearly ones — a changed bank account or an expired card can silently lapse your cover. Check once a year that the debit is still going through.

### Riders — which add-ons are worth paying for

A rider is an extra benefit bolted onto the policy for extra premium. Websites pre-tick several. Uncheck everything, then add back only what you understand.

| Rider | What it does | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Waiver of premium** | Future premiums waived if you become disabled or critically ill | **Often worth it** — keeps cover alive exactly when you cannot pay |
| **Accidental total and permanent disability** | Pays if an accident leaves you permanently disabled | **Often worth it** — income stops *and* costs rise |
| **Critical illness** | Lump sum on diagnosis of listed illnesses | Depends — read the warning below |
| **Accidental death benefit** | Extra payout if death is by accident | Usually skip. Your family's need is identical whatever the cause |
| **Income benefit** | Monthly income to family on top of the cover | Skip. Buy more cover instead — simpler and usually cheaper |
| **Hospital cash** | Fixed daily amount during hospitalisation | Skip. This belongs in your health insurance |

*Table 3.12 — riders worth paying for, and the rest*

> **Careful — accelerated versus additional critical illness.** These sound the same and are completely different.
>
> **Accelerated:** the payout comes *out of* your life cover. ₹1 crore cover with a ₹20 lakh accelerated rider — if you claim for cancer, your family later receives only ₹80 lakh.
>
> **Additional:** the payout is *on top*. Your family still gets the full ₹1 crore. It costs more.
>
> Most cheap critical illness riders are the accelerated kind. Check which one you are being sold, and read the exact list of illnesses and the survival period.

> **Do not** trust the defaults on aggregator websites. Comparison sites are useful for seeing prices side by side, but the options they pre-select are not always cheapest or best for you. Change the settings yourself, then check the same plan on the insurer's own website before paying.

## 3.7 – Filling the form — the step that decides whether your family gets paid

This step decides whether your family actually receives the money. More claims are rejected for **hiding information** than for any other reason.

You must declare all of this:

- **Tobacco in any form** — cigarettes, gutkha, khaini, pan masala, vape. Even "only two a week". Even "I stopped last year"
- **Alcohol** — how much, how often
- **Any illness** — sugar, BP, thyroid, asthma, heart trouble, mental health conditions
- **Past surgeries or hospital stays**, and any regular medicine
- **Family illness** — parents or siblings with heart disease, cancer, sugar
- **Your real income**, your real job, risky hobbies, and any other policies you hold

> **Careful — do the arithmetic.** Suppose declaring that you smoke raises your premium from ₹12,000 to ₹24,000 a year. That extra ₹12,000 makes your family's ₹1 crore **certain**. Hiding it to save ₹12,000 is the most expensive decision you will ever make — and you will not be there to fix it.

**The law behind this.** Under Section 45 of the Insurance Act, the company can question your policy over wrong information for the first **three years**. After that it generally cannot. But you do not get to choose when you die, so honesty on day one is the only safe option.

> **Do this.** Say yes to the medical test. "No medical test needed" sounds convenient but works against you. When the company checks your health and accepts you anyway, nobody can argue later that you hid something. The insurer normally arranges and pays for it, though a few ask you to claim it back after the policy is issued — confirm before booking.

**Then wait.** The company calls to confirm details, sends someone for the test, and takes about two to four weeks to decide. **You are not covered until the policy is actually issued** — not when you pay.

![A timeline showing you are not covered from the day you pay until the policy is issued around day thirty](/diagrams/after-you-apply.svg)

*Fig 3.4 — the gap between paying and being covered. Most people assume there isn't one.*

If they ask for a higher premium because of your health, that is normal and is called loading. Accept it, or try another company. Never go back and change your answers.

## 3.8 – The nominee, and four things people forget

| | |
|---|---|
| **Who to name** | Your wife, husband, children or parents. The law treats these as **beneficial nominees** — the money is legally theirs, and other relatives cannot claim a share. Name anyone else and they are only a collector who must pass it to your legal heirs |
| **If the nominee is a child** | Also name an adult **appointee** who will manage the money until the child turns 18 |
| **If you run a business** | Ask about the **MWP Act**. A policy bought under the Married Women's Property Act creates a trust for your wife and children, so the money is kept out of reach of your creditors. It is a free extra form, but must be chosen while buying. It cannot be added later |

| | |
|---|---|
| **Tell your family** | Write down the company name, policy number and where the document is kept. A policy nobody knows about is a policy nobody claims |

| **Read it within 30 days** | Ask for the **Customer Information Sheet** — a short plain-language summary instead of 40 pages. Check your name, date of birth, cover, years, nominee and health details. You can cancel for a refund in this window |

## 3.9 – Your checklist before you pay

- Someone genuinely depends on my income
- I calculated my own cover and ran the ÷20 test
- The plan runs till I stop earning, around 60
- It is a pure term plan — nothing else
- I compared three companies on both claim ratios
- I compared the same cover and same years
- I filled the form myself and declared everything
- I took the medical test
- The nominee is correct, with an appointee named if a child
- My family knows this policy exists
- Auto-pay is set with a backup reminder
- I will read it within the 30-day window

Tick all twelve and you have done this better than most people ever manage.

## 3.11 – Questions people actually ask

#### If I don't die, do I really get nothing back?

Yes, nothing. That is the design, not a trick.

You are buying protection for a period of time, like car insurance. That is exactly why ₹2 crore costs about ₹1,500 a month. The moment a plan promises money back, the price jumps two to three times.

If you want your money to grow, do that separately in a mutual fund or PPF. You will end up with far more.

#### Can the company increase my premium later?

No. For a normal term plan the premium is fixed on the day you buy and never changes — not as you age, not if you fall ill later.

Buy at 30 and you are still paying a 30-year-old's price at 58. This is the strongest reason to buy early.

#### What happens if I stop paying?

You get 15 to 30 days of grace. After that the policy lapses and the cover ends completely. If you die then, your family gets nothing.

Most companies allow revival within about five years by paying the dues plus interest. But the three-year contestability clock restarts from the revival date, so do not treat it as a safety net.

#### Can I have more than one policy?

Yes, with no limit, as long as the total cover fits your income.

Some people deliberately split — say ₹1 crore each from two companies — so that if one creates trouble at claim time, the family still receives the other. Just declare your existing policies when applying for a new one.

#### Is LIC safer than private companies?

Every insurer in India is regulated by IRDAI and must maintain a minimum solvency ratio. Private companies are not automatically riskier.

Instead of guessing by brand, check the actual numbers: both claim ratios, solvency, and years in business. Let the data decide.

#### What if the insurance company shuts down?

An insurer in India cannot simply close and walk away. IRDAI monitors solvency continuously and has the power to direct that policies be transferred to another insurer so cover continues.

This is also why the solvency ratio matters when comparing companies.

#### Term insurance or whole life — what is the difference?

Term insurance covers you for a fixed number of years, usually till around 60. Whole life covers you till 99 or 100.

Whole life costs considerably more. The question to ask is simple: at 90, who depends on my salary? For most families, nobody. It mainly makes sense for estate planning, or if you have a dependant who will need support for life.

#### Isn't ₹1 crore too much?

It feels huge against your bank balance. Compare it to 25 years of family expenses instead.

₹1 crore ÷ 20 = ₹5 lakh a year, about ₹40,000 a month. If your family spends more than that, it is not enough. And if ₹40 lakh goes straight to clearing the home loan, only ₹60 lakh is left for them to live on.

#### What is the maximum cover I can get?

It depends on your income. As a rough guide, insurers often allow around 20 to 25 times annual income under age 35, reducing to around 10 to 15 times as you get older.

So on ₹12 lakh income at 30, ₹2.5 crore is comfortably within range. Asking for ₹10 crore on that income will be refused — the company checks that the amount matches what your family would actually lose.

#### I am 25 and unmarried. Should I buy now?

No urgency if nobody depends on you.

But buying now locks in a low premium and your current good health permanently. If marriage or a home loan is coming in the next two to three years, buying today is sensible.

#### I am 45. Is it too late?

No. It costs more than at 30, but your family's need is the same.

At 45, use a multiple of 8 to 10 times your income rather than 15, and run the plan to 60 or 65. Buying at 45 is far better than buying at 50.

#### Can I increase my cover later?

Two ways. Some plans have a built-in option to increase cover after marriage, a child or a home loan — check this before buying.

Otherwise simply buy a second policy later. That is completely allowed. But it will be priced at your age and health *then*, not now.

#### I have diabetes or BP. Will they reject me?

Usually not. Most companies accept at a higher premium, called loading.

Declare it fully. If one company refuses or quotes very high, apply to another — they judge conditions differently. A no from one is not a no from all.

Never hide it. A higher premium is survivable. A rejected claim is not.

#### I smoke occasionally. Can I just not mention it?

No. This is the most common reason families lose their claim.

Nicotine shows up in the medical test. And at claim time the company can examine medical records and chemist bills.

Note that each company defines "smoker" differently — some ask about the last 12 months, others 24 months, others ever. Read the exact question and answer that question truthfully.

#### I am self-employed with no salary slip. Can I get a policy?

Yes. Companies accept income tax returns, usually the last two to three years, plus bank statements or audited accounts.

Your cover is set on the income you can prove, so filing returns properly actually matters here.

#### My wife is a homemaker. Does she need cover?

Usually not, since there is no income to replace.

Two things are worth considering though: if the family would have to pay for childcare and household help without her, that is a real cost. And if she is a joint borrower on the home loan, that loan needs covering.

Many companies also have minimum income rules for homemakers, so options may be limited.

#### Can I buy an Indian policy if I live abroad?

Yes. NRIs and OCI card holders can buy Indian term insurance, and it is often much cheaper than cover in the country they live in.

You can apply during a visit to India, or remotely — many companies now do the medical check by video or accept a report from an approved centre abroad.

Pay from your NRE or NRO account. The claim is normally settled in India, in rupees, to your nominee. Declare your country of residence honestly, as premiums differ by country.

#### Does it cover death by illness, or only accident?

Almost any cause — illness, accident, natural causes. It is not an accident policy.

Exclusions are few and printed in your policy. Read that one page when the document arrives.

#### Are there situations where they will not pay?

Yes, and they are listed in your policy. The standard one is suicide within the first 12 months, where normally only part of the premiums paid is returned.

Others commonly include death while committing a crime, and in many policies, death while under the influence of alcohol or drugs — including drunk driving.

Wordings differ between companies. This is exactly why you read the exclusions during the 30-day free look window.

#### What if I die abroad?

Most Indian term plans cover you worldwide, provided you declared your travel or residence plans honestly when applying.

If you move abroad after buying, inform the company in writing. Do not assume.

#### How long does my family wait for the money?

Under IRDAI's 2024 rules: **15 days** if no investigation is needed, **45 days** if it is. After that the company owes interest at bank rate plus 2%.

The biggest cause of delay is not the company — it is the nominee's name not matching their bank account. Check that today.

#### What documents will my family need to claim?

The claim form, the policy document, the death certificate, the nominee's ID and address proof, and the nominee's bank details with a cancelled cheque.

If death followed an illness, also the hospital records and discharge summary. For accidental or unnatural death, the FIR, post-mortem report and police final report.

#### What if the claim is rejected?

Do not give up. Families win these regularly; most simply never try.

Get the rejection in writing with the exact reason and the policy clause used. Complain to the company's grievance officer and give them 30 days. Then register on IRDAI's Bima Bharosa portal, or call 155255.

**One limit most articles miss:** the Insurance Ombudsman only handles disputes up to ₹50 lakh. If your cover is ₹1 crore or more, your family must approach the **consumer commission** instead. Tell them this now, so they do not lose months at the wrong door.

#### Is "zero cost" term insurance actually free?

Not quite. The exit feature itself usually costs no extra premium — that part is true, and it is different from return-of-premium plans which charge two to three times more.

The cost is hidden in the policy term. Insurers typically offer this only on 35 to 40 year plans, so you end up buying cover till 70 or 75 instead of 60. Those extra years cost you every single year.

Get two quotes — plain cover till 60, and the long version with the exit option. If the difference is small, take it. If not, skip it.

Also remember it is not a maturity benefit. You have to actively exit inside the window. Miss it and the policy just runs on to the end with nothing.

#### What is the difference between accelerated and additional critical illness cover?

This catches a lot of people, because the names sound similar and the difference is large.

**Accelerated** pays out of your life cover. If you have ₹1 crore cover with a ₹20 lakh accelerated rider and you claim for cancer, your family later receives ₹80 lakh, not ₹1 crore.

**Additional** pays on top — your family still gets the full ₹1 crore. It costs more.

Most inexpensive riders are the accelerated kind. Check which one you are being offered, and read the exact illness list and survival period. Often a separate health or critical illness policy is the better route.

#### Should I trust comparison websites?

They are genuinely useful for seeing prices side by side, and for filtering by claim ratio.

But the options they pre-select are not always the cheapest or the best for you — payout type, riders and cover variant are often set to defaults that raise the premium. Change every setting yourself.

Then check the same plan, with the same settings, on the insurer's own website before paying.

#### Online or through an agent?

Online, from the company's own website, is usually cheaper for the identical plan — no commission is built into the price.

If you want help, that is fine. Just remember an agent paid by commission has a reason to steer you toward endowment and ULIP. Walk in already knowing your cover amount and your term.

#### What documents do I need to buy?

PAN card, Aadhaar or address proof, a passport-size photo, income proof (salary slips, Form 16 or ITR), and bank details for the premium.

Keep your nominee's full name, date of birth and relationship to you ready as well.

#### Can I change my nominee later?

Yes, any time, free of cost, through the company's form, app or website. Get written confirmation.

Update it after marriage, a birth, a death or a divorce. An outdated nominee is one of the most common and most painful mistakes.

**One exception:** if you bought under the MWP Act, the nominee cannot be changed. That is the trade-off for the creditor protection it gives.

#### If I cancel midway, do I get any money back?

No. A pure term plan has **no surrender value**. If you stop, the cover simply ends and there is nothing to take back.

That is normal and expected — you were buying protection, not building a balance.

The only exception is the 30-day free look window right after buying, where you can cancel and get a refund minus small deductions.

#### Health insurance or term insurance first?

**Health insurance first**, for most people. A hospital bill is far more likely than death, and it can wipe out savings while everyone is still alive.

The order that works: emergency fund → health insurance → term insurance → clear expensive debt → invest.

They do different jobs. Health insurance pays hospital bills while you live. Term insurance pays your family when you die. Neither replaces the other.

#### I already have an endowment policy. Should I cancel it?

Do not cancel in a hurry — surrendering early usually means a real loss.

First, buy a proper term plan so your family is actually protected. That is the urgent part.

Then look at the old policy. If you have paid for many years and it is close to maturity, continuing often makes sense. If you have just started, you can make it "paid-up" — stop paying and keep a reduced benefit, without losing everything. Ask the company for both the surrender value and the paid-up value in writing, then compare.

#### My bank is offering insurance with my home loan. Should I take it?

Be careful. These are often sold while you are tired of signing papers, and they are frequently more expensive than a plain term plan.

Your own term plan already covers the loan — that is exactly what you accounted for when you added loans to your cover amount.

A bank cannot make insurance compulsory for a loan. If someone implies it is, ask for that in writing.

#### What about tax benefits?

Three things. **GST is zero** on individual life insurance since September 2025 — a ₹10,000 premium costs ₹10,000, not ₹11,800. **The premium** qualifies for a deduction up to ₹1.5 lakh a year, but only under the old tax regime (what everyone called 80C became Section 123 from April 2026). **What your family receives is fully tax-free**, under either regime.

But do not buy insurance in March to save tax. That is how people end up with endowment policies they regret for twenty years.

#### Do I need to inform them if I change jobs or start smoking later?

You answer the health and habit questions as they stand **on the day you apply**. Starting to smoke five years later does not invalidate an honestly-filled policy.

But if you take up a genuinely hazardous occupation or move abroad, inform the company in writing. And if your income rises a lot, treat it as a signal to increase cover.

#### Does my office group cover continue if I resign?

No, it ends. But many group policies allow you to **convert** to an individual policy when you leave, often without a fresh medical test.

This matters enormously if your health worsened while employed. Ask HR about the conversion option *before* your last working day — the window is usually short.

## Key takeaways

- Buy term insurance only if someone would struggle financially without your income.
- Cover is yearly income × 15, plus loans and education, minus savings — then divide by 20 to check it is enough.
- Run the policy until you stop earning, usually age 60. Not to 99.
- Buy a pure term plan. Every product promising money back costs far more for far less cover.
- Check the claim settlement ratio by amount, not just by count.
- On the buying page: level cover, lump sum payout, regular pay, yearly premium, and at most two riders.
- Declare every illness, habit and rupee of income. Hidden information is the leading cause of rejected claims.
- Name a spouse, child or parent as nominee so they become a beneficial nominee, and tell your family the policy exists.
- The Insurance Ombudsman only handles disputes up to ₹50 lakh; above that it is the consumer commission.
