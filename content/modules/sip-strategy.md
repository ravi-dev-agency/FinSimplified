## 10.1 – What this chapter is for

[Chapter 9](/learn/sip) covered what a SIP is and how to start one. If you have not set one up yet, do that first — starting matters more than optimising, and everything here works just as well on a SIP you began last month.

This chapter answers the next question: **you have ₹5,000 a month going out. How should it be divided?**

Three things decide it:

| What decides your split | Why |
|---|---|
| **How long until you need the money** | More time means you can hold more equity |
| **How much risk you can actually handle** | An allocation you abandon in a crash is worthless |
| **What changes as you go** | Salary rises, markets drift, goals get closer |

*Table 10.1 — the three inputs*

> **Do this first, if you are new.** Put your whole SIP into one broad large cap or index fund and come back in six months. That is a genuinely good allocation, not a compromise. Nothing below will help if you never start.

## 10.2 – The four building blocks

Everything in this chapter is made from four pieces. You do not need all four.

| Block | What it is | How it behaves |
|---|---|---|
| **Large cap or index fund** | The biggest, most established companies | The steadiest part of equity. Your core |
| **Mid and small cap** | Smaller, faster-growing companies | Higher growth, much deeper falls |
| **Gold** | A gold fund or gold ETF | Often holds up when equity falls |
| **Debt or hybrid** | Bonds, or a mix of equity and debt | Barely moves. Protects money you need sooner |

*Table 10.2 — four blocks. Most beginners only need the first.*

What each of these actually is, in detail, is in [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds). Here we only care about how to combine them.

**Keep it to two or three funds.** Five funds mostly hold the same companies — that is not diversification, it is bookkeeping.

## 10.3 – Your age decides the shape

The logic is simple: **the further away you need the money, the more equity you can hold**, because you have time to sit through falls.

| Your age | Large cap / index | Mid & small cap | Gold | Debt / hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **20s and 30s** | 70% | 20% | 10% | — |
| **40s** | 60% | 10% | 10% | 20% |
| **50s and above** | 40% | — | 10% | 50% |

*Table 10.3 — a starting point, not a rule*

On a ₹5,000 monthly SIP:

| Your age | Large cap / index | Mid & small cap | Gold | Debt / hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **20s and 30s** | ₹3,500 | ₹1,000 | ₹500 | — |
| **40s** | ₹3,000 | ₹500 | ₹500 | ₹1,000 |
| **50s and above** | ₹2,000 | — | ₹500 | ₹2,500 |

*Table 10.4 — the same split, in rupees*

![In your twenties most of the money goes to equity; by your fifties most of it has moved to debt](/diagrams/age-allocation.svg)

*Fig 10.1 — as the years to retirement shrink, the equity share shrinks with them.*

**Why age works as a proxy.** It is really standing in for *time until you need the money*. If you are 30 but saving for a flat deposit in three years, that money is short-horizon regardless of your age — it belongs in debt, as [Chapter 5](/learn/how-to-start-investing) explains.

## 10.4 – Then adjust for the risk you can handle

Age is only half of it. The other half is honest self-knowledge, and it changes the split.

Ask yourself one question: **if your investment fell 30% next year, what would you actually do?**

| Your honest answer | You are |
|---|---|
| "I would sell before it gets worse" | **Careful** |
| "I would feel uneasy but keep going" | **Balanced** |
| "I would keep investing, maybe add more" | **Adventurous** |

*Table 10.5 — answer as you really are, not as you would like to be*

Now the same age band — someone in their 20s or 30s — split three ways by that answer:

| | Large cap / index | Mid & small cap | Gold | Debt / hybrid | Equity total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Careful** | 55% | — | 10% | 35% | 55% |
| **Balanced** | 60% | 15% | 10% | 15% | 75% |
| **Adventurous** | 65% | 25% | 10% | — | 90% |

*Table 10.6 — same age, three temperaments*

On a ₹5,000 monthly SIP:

| | Large cap / index | Mid & small cap | Gold | Debt / hybrid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Careful** | ₹2,750 | — | ₹500 | ₹1,750 |
| **Balanced** | ₹3,000 | ₹750 | ₹500 | ₹750 |
| **Adventurous** | ₹3,250 | ₹1,250 | ₹500 | — |

*Table 10.7 — the same three, in rupees*

Two dials move as you go down: **equity rises from 55% to 90%**, and **mid and small cap rises from nothing to a quarter**. Gold stays put — it is a stabiliser, not a lever.

### Where age and risk disagree

| | What to do |
|---|---|
| **Older and careful** | Take the age row, then move 10–15% more from equity into debt |
| **Older but adventurous** | Hold a little more equity than the age row suggests — but only money you will not need for ten years |
| **Young and careful** | Use the Careful row. Being young does not oblige you to hold mid and small cap |
| **Young and adventurous** | The Adventurous row is a reasonable ceiling. More than this is not bravery, it is concentration |

*Table 10.8 — where the two meet*

There is no prize for holding more equity than you can live with. **A 55% equity plan you keep through a crash beats a 90% one you abandon** — because the one you abandon gets sold at the bottom, which is the only way a fall becomes a permanent loss.

> **Careful.** Mid and small cap funds fall hardest — 40% or more has happened, taking years to recover. Hold them only if you already own a broad fund and are certain you will not sell in a bad year. If unsure, add that portion to your large cap or index fund instead.

## 10.5 – A full portfolio, written out

The tables so far give you percentages. Here is what an actual plan looks like when you write it down properly — every asset class, what it is for, and what it might become.

This is a ₹15,000 monthly SIP, for someone in their 20s or 30s who has already built an emergency fund and taken health cover.

| Asset class | Allocation | Monthly | Role in the portfolio | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nifty index fund or ETF** | 30% | ₹4,500 | Core stability | Moderate |
| **Mid cap** | 20% | ₹3,000 | Growth | Moderate–high |
| **Small cap** | 20% | ₹3,000 | Aggressive growth | High |
| **REIT / real estate** | 20% | ₹3,000 | Diversification | Moderate |
| **Gold ETF** | 10% | ₹1,500 | Hedge | Low–moderate |
| **Total** | **100%** | **₹15,000** | | |

*Table 10.9 — one example portfolio. Not a recommendation.*

**The "role" column is the important one.** Every holding should have a job. If you cannot say what a fund is *for*, you are collecting funds rather than building a portfolio.

| Role | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Core stability** | The steady base. Most of your money, least drama |
| **Growth** | Expected to do better than the core, with bigger swings |
| **Aggressive growth** | The highest potential and the deepest falls |
| **Diversification** | Moves differently from equity, so the whole thing is steadier |
| **Hedge** | Tends to hold up when equity does badly |

*Table 10.10 — five roles. A portfolio needs the first; the rest are optional.*

### What it might become

Holding the same ₹15,000 a month, here is roughly where it could reach:

| Asset class | Monthly | After 10 years | After 20 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nifty index | ₹4,500 | ₹10,45,526 | ₹44,96,166 |
| Mid cap | ₹3,000 | ₹6,97,017 | ₹29,97,444 |
| Small cap | ₹3,000 | ₹7,86,274 | ₹39,49,039 |
| REIT | ₹3,000 | ₹5,84,897 | ₹20,18,688 |
| Gold | ₹1,500 | ₹2,61,142 | ₹7,85,948 |
| **Total** | **₹15,000** | **₹33,74,856** | **₹1,42,47,284** |

*Table 10.11 — illustration only, from the same formula as the [SIP calculator](/calculators/sip). You would have put in ₹18,00,000 over ten years and ₹36,00,000 over twenty.*

> **Careful.** These figures are an illustration of the method, not a prediction. Growth does not arrive smoothly either — in a bad year every column here can be negative at once, because equity assets tend to fall together.

### The mistake to avoid when reading a plan like this

Compare each holding over **the same number of years**. It is tempting to show small cap over 30 years and the index over 10, because small cap then produces a spectacular number — but that is comparing a longer wait against a shorter one, not comparing two assets.

If you see a portfolio table where different rows use different time periods, the totals underneath it mean nothing. Every row in Table 10.11 uses the same horizon, which is why the columns can honestly be added up.

## 10.6 – Why diversification actually matters

Splitting money across asset classes is not about earning more. **It is about not depending on any one thing being right.**

Here is the same idea on a lump sum of ₹50,000 rather than a monthly amount:

| Asset class | Allocation | Amount | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nifty ETF** | 25% | ₹12,500 | Stability |
| **Mid cap ETF** | 20% | ₹10,000 | Growth, higher risk |
| **Small cap ETF** | 20% | ₹10,000 | High growth, high risk |
| **REIT / real estate** | 15% | ₹7,500 | Diversification and rental yield |
| **Gold ETF** | 10% | ₹5,000 | Inflation protection, equity hedge |
| **Liquid fund** | 10% | ₹5,000 | Money you can reach quickly |
| **Total** | **100%** | **₹50,000** | |

*Table 10.12 — the same thinking applied to a one-time amount*

### What diversification does and does not do

| It does | It does not |
|---|---|
| Stop one bad holding from ruining the outcome | Prevent losses in a broad crash |
| Smooth the ride, so you are likelier to stay invested | Increase your returns |
| Let you hold riskier things safely, in small doses | Work if everything you own moves together |

*Table 10.13 — an honest scorecard*

That last row is the one people miss. **Five equity funds are not diversification** — they fall together. Gold, debt and REITs earn their place precisely because they often do not move with equity.

> **Do this.** For each holding, write one line saying what it is for. If two holdings have the same answer, you probably need one of them, not both.

## 10.7 – Step up every time your salary rises

This is the highest-value habit in the whole chapter, and almost nobody does it.

Most people set a SIP once and never change it. Their salary doubles over a decade; their investing does not move.

Compare two people, both starting at ₹5,000 a month for 20 years at an assumed 12%. One never changes it. The other raises it 10% each year.

| | Total invested | Becomes |
|---|---|---|
| **Flat ₹5,000** | ₹12,00,000 | ₹49,95,740 |
| **Raised 10% a year** | ₹34,36,632 | ₹99,44,656 |

*Table 10.14 — illustration at an assumed 12%. Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

Roughly **double the outcome**. And the increases are gentle — by year 20 the monthly amount has grown from ₹5,000 to about ₹30,580, rising alongside two decades of salary growth.

### Why this is easier than it sounds

The increase comes out of a raise you have not yet got used to spending. If your salary rises by ₹4,000 and your SIP rises by ₹500, you still take home ₹3,500 more than last month.

Many platforms offer a **step-up SIP** that raises the amount automatically each year, so the decision happens once.

> **Do this.** Set a 10% annual step-up now, or put a reminder in your calendar for the month you usually get your appraisal. Increasing by ₹500 a year is worth more than any fund selection you will agonise over.

## 10.8 – Rebalancing: putting the split back

Your allocation does not stay where you set it. Equity grows faster than debt, so its share quietly rises.

Take a 70/30 equity–debt split, left completely alone, with equity growing at 12% and debt at 6.5%:

| After | Equity | Debt |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | 71% | 29% |
| 3 years | 73% | 27% |
| 5 years | 75% | 25% |
| 10 years | 79% | 21% |

*Table 10.15 — illustration only. The split drifts on its own.*

![A seventy-thirty split drifting to seventy-nine twenty-one over ten years](/diagrams/allocation-drift.svg)

*Fig 10.2 — the risk you end up holding is not the risk you chose.*

After ten years you are holding 79% equity — a noticeably riskier portfolio than the one you chose, and you never made a decision to take that risk.

**Rebalancing means putting it back.** Sell a little of what has grown, buy a little of what has not, and return to your intended split.

### The easy way to do it

Selling has costs and tax. There is a simpler method for anyone still investing monthly:

| Method | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| **Redirect new money** | Point new SIP instalments at whichever block has fallen behind | Almost everyone. No selling, no tax |
| **Sell and switch** | Actually move money between funds | Larger portfolios where redirecting is not enough |

*Table 10.16 — two ways to rebalance*

For most people the first method is enough. If equity has run ahead, send the next few months of instalments to your debt fund until the proportions come back.

### How often

**Once a year is plenty.** Pick a date you will remember — your birthday, or the start of the financial year — and check then.

Rebalancing more often costs money in fees and tax, and achieves very little. Some people also rebalance when a block drifts more than 10 percentage points from target, whichever comes first.

> **Careful.** Rebalancing feels wrong every single time. You will be selling the thing that has done well to buy the thing that has done badly. That discomfort is the point — it is a rule that forces you to take profits high and buy low, which is the opposite of what instinct does.

## 10.9 – What to change as goals get closer

An allocation is not set once for life. It should shift as the date you need the money approaches.

| Years until you need it | What to do |
|---|---|
| **More than 7** | Leave it alone. Equity heavy is fine |
| **5 to 7** | Start reducing mid and small cap first |
| **3 to 5** | Begin moving money into debt gradually |
| **Under 3** | The money for that goal should be out of equity entirely |

*Table 10.17 — the glide down*

The mistake people make is leaving everything in equity until the month they need it. A 30% fall in the final year can undo a decade of patience — and the date does not move because the market had a bad year.

**Move gradually, not all at once.** Shifting a portion each year as the goal approaches avoids having to pick a single day to exit.

### One portfolio per goal

If you are saving for two things with different dates — a car in three years, retirement in thirty — they need different allocations. Run a separate SIP for each rather than one blended pot, so you can move the short-horizon money out without touching the long-horizon money.

## 10.10 – When to change your allocation, and when not to

This distinction saves a lot of money.

| Change it when | Do not change it when |
|---|---|
| Your goal date moves closer | Markets fell |
| Your income changes significantly | A different fund did better last year |
| You have a new dependant or responsibility | Someone on the internet is confident |
| You have learnt you cannot handle the falls | Your fund is down and its whole category is down |
| Your annual rebalancing date arrives | You are bored |

*Table 10.18 — the honest test for any change*

**A fall in the market is not a reason to change your allocation.** It may be a reason to admit your allocation was wrong for your temperament — but fix that after the recovery, not during the fall. Changing in a panic converts a paper loss into a real one.

## 10.11 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **Five SIPs in five similar funds** | Overlapping holdings, no extra diversification |
| **Never stepping up** | Salary doubled and the SIP did not. Costs more than any fund choice |
| **Never rebalancing** | A 70/30 split becomes 79/21 and you never chose that risk |
| **Rebalancing every month** | Fees and tax, for no benefit |
| **Copying someone else's allocation** | Their age, income, goals and temperament are not yours |
| **Holding mid and small cap you cannot sit through** | The falls are 40%+, and selling at the bottom is the only real loss |
| **Leaving goal money in equity until the last month** | One bad year undoes a decade |
| **Changing allocation because markets fell** | That is the moment the plan is meant to hold |
| **Picking the split before starting the SIP** | Optimising something that does not exist yet |
| **Comparing holdings over different time periods** | Small cap over 30 years next to an index over 10 is not a comparison |
| **Treating the tables here as advice** | They are a starting point for your own thinking |

## 10.12 – Questions people actually ask

#### How many funds should I have?

One is genuinely enough to start. Two or three once you want a real split.

Beyond that the funds overlap so heavily that you are adding paperwork rather than diversification.

#### Should I change my allocation when the market crashes?

No. That is precisely when the allocation is doing its job.

If a crash makes you want to sell everything, the lesson is that your equity share was too high for your temperament — but act on that after markets recover, not in the middle of a fall.

#### Do I really need gold?

No. It is a stabiliser, not essential. If holding it complicates things, skip it and put that portion in your large cap or index fund.

It earns its place mainly by often holding up when equity falls, which makes the whole portfolio a little easier to live with.

#### What if I cannot afford to split ₹2,000 across four funds?

Then do not. Put it in one broad fund.

Splitting a small amount across four funds means four tiny investments and four things to track. Split once the amount is large enough that the split is meaningful — many people find that is somewhere past ₹5,000 a month.

#### How do I rebalance without selling?

Redirect your new SIP instalments to whichever block has fallen behind, until the proportions come back. No selling, so no exit load and no capital gains.

This works well for anyone still investing monthly, which is most people reading this.

#### My mid cap fund has done brilliantly. Should I put more in?

That is exactly backwards, and it is what rebalancing exists to prevent.

A block that has run ahead now occupies more of your portfolio than you intended. The disciplined move is to send new money elsewhere until the split is restored.

#### I am 25 but very nervous about losing money. What should I do?

Use the Careful row in Table 10.6, and ignore anyone who says you must hold 90% equity because you are young.

An allocation you can hold for thirty years beats an aggressive one you abandon in year two. You can always increase equity later, once you have been through a fall and know how you react.

#### Should my EPF or PPF count as part of this?

Yes, ideally. They are your debt allocation, and they are often substantial.

If a large EPF balance is already giving you a solid debt layer, your SIP can lean more towards equity than the tables suggest. Look at everything you own together, not fund by fund.

#### How long before I should review all this?

Once a year. Put it next to your rebalancing date so it is one annual task rather than two.

Between those dates, the correct amount of attention is none.

#### Is there a tax cost to rebalancing?

Yes, if you sell. Moving money between funds counts as a sale, so gains may be taxed depending on how long you held.

That is why redirecting new money is the better default for most people.

## Key takeaways

- If you are starting out, one broad large cap or index fund is a complete allocation. Start there.
- Three things decide your split: time until you need the money, how much risk you can handle, and what changes along the way.
- Age sets the shape — more equity when the money is far away, more debt as it gets closer.
- Temperament adjusts it. At the same age, equity can reasonably range from 55% to 90% depending on how you would react to a 30% fall.
- A 55% equity plan you keep through a crash beats a 90% one you abandon.
- Step up your SIP every time your salary rises. A 10% annual increase roughly doubles the twenty-year outcome.
- Allocations drift. A 70/30 split becomes about 79/21 in ten years if left alone.
- Rebalance once a year, preferably by redirecting new instalments rather than selling.
- Move money out of equity gradually as a goal gets within three to five years.
- Never change your allocation because markets fell. That is when it is meant to hold.
