## 6.1 – Why the stock market exists at all

Forget the screens full of red and green numbers for a moment. Start with a company that needs money.

Imagine a business making solar water heaters in Hyderabad. It is doing well, and it needs **₹10 crore** to build a bigger factory. It does not have that money. Where does it get it?

There are only two real answers.

### Option 1 — Borrow it

The company goes to a bank and takes a loan at, say, 10% a year.

| | |
|---|---|
| Interest every year | ₹1 crore |
| Must it be paid in a bad year? | **Yes. Every year, profit or loss** |
| After 5 years, paid in interest | ₹5 crore |
| And still owes | ₹10 crore |
| Who owns the company? | Still the original owners, entirely |

*Table 6.1 — the cost of borrowing ₹10 crore*

The bank does not care whether the factory succeeds. It wants its interest on the due date regardless. If the company has a bad year and cannot pay, the bank can take back what was pledged against the loan.

**The money is cheaper if things go well, and dangerous if they do not.**

### Option 2 — Sell small pieces of the company

Instead of borrowing, the owners sell a portion of the company itself to the public.

| | |
|---|---|
| Interest every year | **None** |
| Must anything be repaid? | **No. Ever** |
| What is given up instead | A share of all future profits, permanently |
| Who owns the company now? | The original owners **and** the new investors |

*Table 6.2 — the cost of raising ₹10 crore by selling shares*

Nothing has to be repaid and there is no interest. But the owners have permanently given away a slice of every rupee the company will ever earn, and the new owners get a say in how it is run.

**That is the trade.** A loan is cheap but must be serviced. Selling shares costs nothing upfront and everything later, if the company does well.

![A loan costs interest every year and must still be repaid; selling shares costs nothing upfront but gives away a share of profits permanently](/diagrams/loan-vs-shares.svg)

*Fig 6.1 — the same ₹10 crore, raised two ways.*

### Where you come in

Those small pieces are called **shares**. When a company sells them to the public for the first time, that is an **IPO**.

After the IPO, those shares do not disappear — people buy and sell them among themselves. The place where that happens is the **stock market**.

So when you buy a share, you are stepping into the shoes of an earlier owner. You are buying a slice of a real business, with a real factory, real customers and real profits.

> **Careful.** One detail that surprises people: **when you buy a share on the market, the company gets nothing.** It raised its money at the IPO, possibly decades ago. You are paying another investor, not funding the business. The company only receives money when it issues new shares.

### So what do you actually own?

If a company has divided itself into 1,00,00,000 shares and you buy 100 of them, you own **0.001%** of that company.

Small, but completely real. You are a part-owner of the business. If it earns ₹50 crore in profit that year, ₹5,000 of that profit belongs to your slice — either paid to you as a **dividend**, or kept inside the company to grow it further, which makes your slice more valuable.

| If the company | Then your shares |
|---|---|
| Earns more each year | Become worth more |
| Pays a dividend | Put cash in your account |
| Stops growing | Stop rising |
| Fails completely | Can become worthless |

*Table 6.3 — you share in the outcome of a real business, good or bad*

### Why prices move up and down

A share price is just the price at which the last buyer and last seller agreed. Nothing more.

When more people want to buy than sell, the price rises. When more want to sell, it falls. Day to day, that balance shifts on news, mood, rumour and fear — none of which tell you much about the business itself.

But over many years, a company that keeps earning more becomes worth more, and its price follows.

> **Do this.** Remember the difference: **short term, prices follow opinion. Long term, they follow earnings.** Almost every mistake in this chapter comes from confusing the two.

### The words you will hear

| Word | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Share (or stock, or equity)** | One small piece of ownership in a company |
| **Exchange** | The marketplace itself — NSE and BSE in India |
| **Broker** | The company that lets you place orders on the exchange |
| **Demat account** | Holds your shares electronically, like a bank account for shares |
| **Dividend** | A share of the profit, paid out to owners |
| **Index** | A basket that tracks many companies together, to show how the market is doing |
| **SEBI** | The regulator that sets the rules |

*Table 6.4 — the vocabulary, once*

## 6.2 – The numbers people quote, explained

Every conversation about shares uses the same handful of terms. They sound technical and they are not. Each one is a single division, and once you can read them, most market talk stops being intimidating.

We will use **one company throughout**, so every number connects.

> **Our example company.** Share price ₹500. It has issued 2,00,00,000 shares. Last year it earned ₹20 crore in profit. The owners' money in the business is ₹100 crore, and it owes ₹40 crore. It paid ₹10 per share as dividend.

### Market capitalisation — what the whole company costs

**Market cap = share price × number of shares**

₹500 × 2,00,00,000 = **₹1,000 crore**

This is the price tag on the entire business. It is the number that tells you how big a company is — not the share price.

| Category | Roughly means |
|---|---|
| **Large cap** | The biggest, most established companies. Steadier, slower |
| **Mid cap** | Medium-sized. More growth potential, bigger falls |
| **Small cap** | Small companies. Highest potential, highest risk |

*Table 6.5 — the three size buckets. Mutual funds are often named after these.*

### EPS — profit per share

**EPS (earnings per share) = profit ÷ number of shares**

₹20,00,00,000 ÷ 2,00,00,000 = **₹10 per share**

Each share earned ₹10 of profit last year. Rising EPS over several years is one of the clearest signs a business is genuinely growing.

### P/E — what you pay for ₹1 of profit

The most quoted number in the market, and the most misunderstood.

**P/E (price to earnings) = share price ÷ EPS**

₹500 ÷ ₹10 = **50**

You are paying ₹50 for every ₹1 of annual profit. Another way to read it: at this rate of profit, it would take 50 years to earn back the price.

**Why P/E matters more than share price.** Two companies, both earning ₹1 crore a year:

| | Company A | Company B |
|---|---|---|
| Price per share | ₹100 | ₹2,000 |
| Number of shares | 10,00,000 | 10,000 |
| Whole company costs | ₹10 crore | ₹2 crore |
| Annual profit | ₹1 crore | ₹1 crore |
| **P/E** | **10** | **2** |

*Table 6.6 — the ₹2,000 share is the cheaper business*

The ₹2,000 share is five times cheaper than the ₹100 share, measured properly. **Share price alone tells you nothing about whether something is expensive.**

![Company A at one hundred rupees a share is worth ten crore; Company B at two thousand rupees is worth two crore, so B is the cheaper business](/diagrams/pe-explained.svg)

*Fig 6.2 — the higher share price is the cheaper company. This is what P/E measures.*

> **Careful.** A low P/E is not a buy signal. It often means the market expects profits to fall, and the market is frequently right. A high P/E is not automatically bad either — it may reflect a business growing quickly. P/E is a question to investigate, never an answer.

### Book value and P/B

**Book value per share = owners' money in the business ÷ number of shares**

₹100 crore ÷ 2,00,00,000 = **₹50 per share**

**P/B (price to book) = ₹500 ÷ ₹50 = 10**

Book value is roughly what the company owns minus what it owes. P/B compares the price against that. It matters most for banks and asset-heavy businesses, and much less for companies whose value is in brands or software.

### ROE — how well they use the owners' money

**ROE (return on equity) = profit ÷ owners' money**

₹20 crore ÷ ₹100 crore = **20%**

The company turns every ₹100 of owners' money into ₹20 of profit a year. A consistently high ROE across many years usually signals a genuinely good business.

### Debt to equity — how much it owes

**Debt ÷ owners' money** = ₹40 crore ÷ ₹100 crore = **0.4**

For every ₹1 the owners have put in, the company has borrowed 40 paise. Low is generally safer. High debt is what turns an ordinary bad year into a crisis, for a company just as for a household.

### Dividend yield — the cash you receive

**Dividend per share ÷ share price** = ₹10 ÷ ₹500 = **2%**

You get 2% a year in cash, on top of any price movement. Some companies pay generously, many growing companies pay nothing at all and reinvest instead — neither is automatically better.

### All of it on one page

| Term | Formula | Our company | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Market cap** | price × shares | ₹1,000 crore | How big the company is |
| **EPS** | profit ÷ shares | ₹10 | Profit earned per share |
| **P/E** | price ÷ EPS | 50 | What you pay for ₹1 of profit |
| **Book value** | owners' money ÷ shares | ₹50 | The accounting value per share |
| **P/B** | price ÷ book value | 10 | Price against accounting value |
| **ROE** | profit ÷ owners' money | 20% | How well owners' money is used |
| **Debt / equity** | debt ÷ owners' money | 0.4 | How much it has borrowed |
| **Dividend yield** | dividend ÷ price | 2% | Cash returned to you each year |

*Table 6.7 — every term, one company, all connected*

### A few more you will hear

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Volume** | How many shares traded. Low volume means it can be hard to sell |
| **52-week high / low** | The highest and lowest price over the past year |
| **Bull market** | A long stretch of rising prices |
| **Bear market** | A long stretch of falling prices, usually 20% or more |
| **Correction** | A shorter fall, typically around 10% |
| **Blue chip** | Informal term for large, long-established companies |
| **Portfolio** | Everything you own, taken together |
| **Circuit limit** | A cap on how far a price may move in a day, to curb panic |

*Table 6.8 — market vocabulary you will meet constantly*

> **Do this.** You do not need to calculate any of these yourself — every broker app shows them. What matters is knowing what they mean, so a number on a screen becomes information rather than decoration.

## 6.3 – Before you put money in equity, please read this

This is the most important section in the chapter, and it comes before the methods deliberately.

**The stock market is not a place where money grows automatically. It is a place where you buy pieces of businesses — and if you buy without understanding what you are buying, you can genuinely lose money.**

Not "returns were lower than expected". Actually lose a large part of what you put in.

### What "risk" honestly means here

| What can happen | How likely |
|---|---|
| Your investment falls 10–30% in a bad year | Normal. Expect this repeatedly |
| It stays down for two or three years | Happens. This is what breaks people |
| A single company you own fails completely | Possible, if you own few companies |
| A diversified fund goes to zero | Effectively impossible |

*Table 6.9 — the realistic range of outcomes*

The last two rows are the difference between the methods in 6.4. One company can fail. Fifty companies do not all fail at once.

### Get some knowledge first

Equity is the tier with the highest long-term returns, and it earns them by being uncomfortable. Before putting serious money in, you should genuinely understand:

1. **Why prices fall**, and that a fall is normal rather than a sign of something broken
2. **What you actually own** — a business, not a moving number
3. **Your time horizon.** Money needed within seven years does not belong here at all
4. **That your base is in place** — emergency fund, health cover, no credit card debt. See [Chapter 5](/learn/how-to-start-investing)

> **Careful.** If you cannot say what a company sells and how it makes money, you are not investing in it — you are guessing at a price. Guessing sometimes works, and it is not a plan.

**Start small.** There is no prize for putting in a large amount early. Start with an amount that would not hurt to lose while you learn, and increase it as your understanding grows — not as your confidence grows. They are different things.

## 6.4 – The three ways to invest in the stock market

Here is the part that matters. There are three routes into the same market, and they differ enormously in risk, effort and how much knowledge you need.

| | Direct shares | Mutual funds | ETFs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What you buy** | Individual companies you choose | A pool run by a manager | A basket tracking an index |
| **Who decides** | You | A professional fund manager | Nobody — it copies an index |
| **Companies you end up owning** | However many you buy | Usually 40–50 | Often 50 or more |
| **Knowledge needed** | High | Low | Low |
| **Effort** | Ongoing and serious | Very little | Very little |
| **Risk** | **High** | Moderate | Moderate |
| **Reward** | **Potentially high** | Market-linked | Market-linked |
| **Cost** | Brokerage per trade | Expense ratio | Lower expense ratio, plus brokerage |
| **Bought through** | A broker | The fund house or an app | A broker, on the exchange |
| **Suits** | People who study businesses | Almost every beginner | Beginners comfortable with a demat account |

*Table 6.10 — the three routes compared*

![The same market reached three ways: buying companies yourself, through a mutual fund, or through an ETF](/diagrams/three-routes.svg)

*Fig 6.3 — three doors into the same market. The difference is who does the choosing, and how much can go wrong.*

Now each one properly.

### Route 1 — Direct shares: high risk, high reward

You open a demat account, research companies, and buy the ones you choose. You own those specific businesses and nothing else.

**Why people are drawn to it.** If you pick well, nothing beats it. A single good company held for years can outperform any fund. You keep every rupee of the gain, and you control exactly what you own.

**Why it is genuinely risky.** Everything depends on your choices, and the mathematics of concentration is unforgiving:

| If you own | One company failing costs you |
|---|---|
| 1 company | 100% |
| 3 companies | 33% |
| 5 companies | 20% |
| 50 companies | 2% |

*Table 6.11 — why owning a handful of shares is the risky part, not the market*

![Owning one company means one failure costs everything; owning fifty means it costs about two percent](/diagrams/concentration-risk.svg)

*Fig 6.4 — the same company failing costs you 100%, 20% or 2%, depending only on how many you own.*

Most beginners with ₹6,000 a month can afford one or two shares of one company. That is not a portfolio. That is a single bet with your savings.

**What it demands of you.** Reading annual reports, understanding how a business makes money, judging whether its profits will continue, and doing that repeatedly for years. It is a genuine skill and it takes real time.

> **Careful.** Direct shares is the hardest version of investing, and most beginners attempt it first because it is the most exciting. If you want to try, keep it to a small slice — see 6.6.

### Route 2 — Mutual funds: the sensible default

You give money to a fund house. A professional manager pools it with thousands of other investors and buys 40 or 50 companies. You own units representing your share of that pool.

**Why this suits almost everyone.** Your ₹500 or ₹5,000 buys a slice of dozens of companies immediately. Someone qualified does the choosing. One company failing barely registers. You can invest a fixed amount monthly without ever opening a trading screen.

**What it costs.** An annual fee called the expense ratio, taken from the fund. You are paying for diversification and professional management, and for most people that is money well spent.

**What to know.** Funds come in many categories with very different risk levels — a large-cap fund and a small-cap fund are not remotely the same thing. That choice is covered properly in [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds).

### Route 3 — ETFs: the low-cost middle path

An ETF is a fund that trades on the exchange like a share. Most ETFs simply copy an index rather than trying to beat it.

**Why people choose them.** Costs are usually lower than an active mutual fund, because nobody is being paid to pick stocks. You get the same broad diversification. And you can buy or sell any time the market is open.

**What to watch.** You need a demat account, you pay brokerage on each trade, and the price on the exchange can drift slightly from the fund's true value. Those details matter and are covered in [Chapter 8](/learn/etfs).

### So which one?

| If this sounds like you | Start with |
|---|---|
| "I want to invest but I don't want to study companies" | Mutual funds |
| "I want low cost and I'm fine using a broker app" | ETFs |
| "I genuinely enjoy reading about businesses and will keep doing it" | Mutual funds first, then a small slice of direct shares |
| "I want to double my money quickly" | None of these. That expectation is the problem |

*Table 6.12 — choosing your route*

**For almost every beginner, the honest answer is a mutual fund or an ETF.** Not because direct shares are bad, but because they require a skill you do not have yet — and the market charges real money for learning it.

## 6.5 – How to actually start

Your KYC, once done for any investment, works everywhere — so the only real difference between the routes is whether you need a broker.

### If you chose mutual funds

1. Pick one broad, diversified fund. One is enough to start
2. Set up a monthly auto-debit for a date just after your salary arrives
3. Leave it alone. Check once a quarter at most

No demat account and no trading screen required.

### If you chose ETFs or direct shares

1. **Choose a broker** and compare charges per trade and the annual maintenance fee
2. **Open a demat and trading account** — they open together. The demat holds your shares; the trading account places orders
3. **Transfer money** from your bank to the broker
4. **Place your order** — and use a limit order

### Limit orders, not market orders

| Order type | What it does | When to use |
|---|---|---|
| **Market order** | Buys immediately at whatever price is available | Rarely — you accept any price |
| **Limit order** | Buys only at your price or better | Almost always |

*Table 6.13 — the one mechanical habit worth forming*

A market order on a thinly traded share can fill far from the price on your screen. A limit order simply does not execute if nobody meets your price, which is the safer way to fail.

> **Do this.** Use limit orders from your very first trade. An order that does not fill costs you nothing. A bad fill costs real money.

## 6.6 – If you want to try direct shares anyway

That is a legitimate choice. Here is how to do it without damaging your finances.

### Keep it a small slice

Put the bulk of your equity in funds, and allow a small satellite for shares you choose yourself.

Take Ramu from [Chapter 5](/learn/how-to-start-investing), investing ₹8,401 a month:

| | Amount | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Core** | ₹7,561 | Diversified funds — this is his actual plan |
| **Satellite** | ₹840 | Direct shares, chosen by him |

*Table 6.14 — one example split. Around ten percent is a common ceiling, not a rule.*

If his picks do badly, his retirement is untouched. If they do well, he has learnt something real. Either way his future does not depend on his stock-picking.

### Six questions before buying any company

1. **What does it sell, and to whom?** If you cannot explain it simply, stop
2. **Is it making a profit now?** Not "will it one day"
3. **Is it growing?** Look across several years, not one
4. **How much debt does it carry?** Debt turns a bad year into a crisis
5. **What would have to go wrong?** Write it down. If that happens, you sell
6. **Would I buy the whole company at this price?**

If you cannot answer these, buy the fund instead. That is not defeat — it is the right decision for most people, including many professionals.

### Costs add up faster than you think

Every trade costs brokerage, exchange fees, stamp duty, GST and transaction tax. Assume a round trip costs roughly 0.3%:

| Trades a year | Cost as a share of your capital |
|---|---|
| 12 | 3.6% |
| 50 | 15.0% |
| 200 | 60.0% |

*Table 6.15 — illustration at an assumed 0.3% round trip. Actual costs vary by broker and trade size.*

![Twelve trades a year costs 3.6% of capital, fifty costs 15%, two hundred costs 60% — more than equity has historically returned](/diagrams/trading-cost.svg)

*Fig 6.5 — costs rise with every trade, while returns do not.*

Someone trading 200 times a year needs a **60% return just to break even**. Against the roughly 12% a year equity has delivered over long periods historically, that is not a strategy.

**Trading frequently is the most reliable way to lose money in the stock market** — more reliable than picking the wrong company.

## 6.7 – Things to stay away from

| | |
|---|---|
| **Intraday trading** | Buying and selling the same day. Costs are brutal and most participants lose |
| **Futures and options** | Leveraged. Losses can exceed what you put in |
| **Penny stocks** | Tiny, illiquid companies, and a favourite target of price manipulation |
| **Tips from social media groups** | Frequently coordinated schemes where you are the exit |
| **Borrowing to invest** | Turns a fall you could have waited out into a forced sale |
| **"Guaranteed return" schemes** | No such thing exists in equity. A fraud signal, every time |

*Table 6.16 — where beginners lose the most money*

> **Do not** trade futures and options as a beginner. They exist for hedging by people with a specific exposure to manage, and the great majority of individual traders in them lose money. The marketing around them is aggressive and should be read as advertising.

## 6.8 – Tax, briefly

Gains from shares and equity funds are taxed when you sell, at different rates depending on how long you held. Dividends are taxed too.

The practical consequence matches everything else here: **holding longer is generally treated more favourably than selling quickly.**

## 6.10 – Questions people actually ask

#### I have never invested before. Where do I start?

A diversified mutual fund, with a small monthly amount, through an auto-debit. Not direct shares.

Give it a year. You will learn how falls feel without risking much, and that experience is worth more than any amount of reading.

#### Can I really lose money in the stock market?

Yes, genuinely — especially over short periods, and especially if you own few companies or use borrowed money.

What makes losses permanent is usually selling during a fall, or owning so few companies that one failure matters. Diversification and time are the defences against both.

#### Direct shares are high risk and high reward. Is that worth it?

Only if you will do the work, and only with money whose loss would not change your plans.

The "high reward" half is real but it is not automatic — it goes to people who choose well and hold for years. The "high risk" half applies to everyone immediately.

#### How much money do I need to start?

For a mutual fund, often ₹500 a month. For shares, the price of one share.

The better question is what proportion of your savings belongs in equity at all. If you need the money within seven years, the answer is none.

#### What is the difference between a mutual fund and an ETF?

Both give you many companies at once. A mutual fund is bought from the fund house, often monthly and automatically. An ETF is bought on the exchange through a broker, like a share, and usually costs less each year.

For a beginner investing monthly, a mutual fund is usually simpler. [Chapter 8](/learn/etfs) covers ETFs in full.

#### Everyone around me is making money. Am I missing out?

You are hearing about the wins. Nobody posts their losses.

In periods when everything rises, everyone feels skilled. The skill shows up in the fall that follows, and that is when most of those stories quietly stop.

#### Should I apply for IPOs?

Be careful. The company and its early investors choose the timing and the price, and they generally pick conditions that favour them.

There is no rule that an IPO is priced attractively for you. Judge it as you would any company — the business and the price — not the excitement around it.

#### The market crashed and my investment is down 25%. What do I do?

If the money is not needed for years and you own a diversified fund: nothing. Keep your monthly investment running. Falls are when it buys the most.

If you are tempted to sell, that is a sign you have more in equity than your horizon or temperament supports. Fix the allocation after it recovers — not during the fall.

#### How long should I stay invested?

Seven years at minimum for equity, and ideally much longer. Historically, the longer the holding period, the more reliably equity has beaten inflation.

#### Do I need to watch the market daily?

No. For a monthly investment in a diversified fund, checking once a quarter is plenty.

Watching daily does not improve returns. It mostly supplies opportunities to make an emotional decision.

## Key takeaways

- A share is a small piece of a real company. The stock market is simply where those pieces are bought and sold.
- Short term, prices follow opinion. Long term, they follow company earnings.
- You can genuinely lose money in equity, especially without understanding what you own. Learn first, and start with an amount you can afford to be wrong about.
- There are three routes into the same market: direct shares, mutual funds and ETFs.
- Direct shares are high risk and high reward. They need real knowledge and ongoing effort, and owning only a few companies is the dangerous part.
- Mutual funds are the sensible default for beginners — dozens of companies, chosen professionally, from ₹500 a month.
- ETFs give similar diversification at lower cost, but need a demat account and a broker.
- If you try direct shares, keep them to a small satellite of around ten percent, with the core in funds.
- Trading often is the most reliable way to lose money. At 0.3% a round trip, 200 trades a year needs 60% just to break even.
- Money you need within seven years does not belong in the stock market at all.
