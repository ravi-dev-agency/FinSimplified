## 12.1 – A free loan, every month, for 45 days

Here is the part almost nobody explains properly.

**Every time you buy something on a credit card, the bank pays the shop and lends you the money — free — for up to about 45 days.**

No interest. No fee. You pay the bank back on your due date and it has earned nothing from you.

That is not a promotion or a trick. It is how the product works, and it is the only genuinely free credit most people will ever be offered.

### Why the bank does this

Banks make money from credit cards three ways. The shop pays them a small fee on every swipe. Some customers pay an annual fee. And **some customers do not clear their balance, and pay 3–3.75% a month on it.**

That third group funds the whole thing. The bank is happy to give you 45 free days because enough people will not use them properly.

**The entire skill of using a credit card is staying out of that third group.** Everything else in this chapter is detail.

### What "free for 45 days" actually looks like

Buy a ₹12,000 phone on a card. The bank pays the shop today. Depending on where you are in your billing cycle, you pay the bank back somewhere between 20 and 50 days later.

For those weeks, you are holding ₹12,000 that is not yours, at no cost. Your own ₹12,000 stays in your bank account until the due date.

Do that with your normal monthly spending — groceries, fuel, phone bill — and you have a permanent, rolling, interest-free loan that renews every month.

> **Do this.** Route spending you were going to do anyway through the card, and set an auto-debit for the **full statement amount**. That single setting is what keeps it free.

### The same card, two completely different outcomes

Ramu and Kiran both take home ₹58,000 a month in Hyderabad, and both spend the same way.

Not everything can go on a card — rent goes to the landlord by transfer, the bike EMI is an auto-debit, and fuel and autos are mostly UPI. What is left is groceries, the phone bill, eating out, shopping and subscriptions: **about ₹21,806 a month** for each of them. (Ramu's full budget is in [Chapter 1](/learn/budgeting).)

One difference. Ramu set an auto-debit for the full statement amount the day he got the card. Kiran pays the minimum due most months.

| | Ramu | Kiran |
|---|---|---|
| Spent on the card **each month** | ₹21,806 | ₹21,806 |
| Over **twelve months** | ₹2,61,672 | ₹2,61,672 |
| Interest paid in the year | **₹0** | **about ₹11,144** |
| Rewards earned at ~1% | ₹2,617 | ₹2,617 |
| Free credit, all year | Yes | No — it ended in month one |
| Credit score | Building nicely | Damaged |
| **After one year** | **₹2,617 ahead** | **₹8,528 behind** |

*Table 12.1 — illustrative, at about 3.5% a month on a revolving balance. The ₹2,61,672 is a year's spending, not a month's — it is the same ₹21,806 twelve times over.*

Nothing separates them except one instruction to the bank.

### It is a loan, and it is free — until it is not

A credit card is a short-term loan you take every time you use it. It is not your money and it is not a payment method like UPI. The bank pays the shop on your behalf, and you owe the bank.

What makes it unusual is that the loan is genuinely free if you repay in full, on time. It becomes one of the most expensive borrowings in India the moment you do not.

**There is no middle setting.** You are either in Ramu's column or Kiran's, and the gap is not gradual — it opens the first month you fail to clear the balance.

### The one number that matters

Credit card interest in India runs at roughly **3% to 3.75% a month**. Banks quote it monthly because the annual figure sounds alarming, which it should.

Monthly 3.5% is about **42% a year** once it compounds.

| | Roughly costs a year |
|---|---|
| Home loan | 8–9% |
| Car loan | 9–12% |
| Personal loan | 12–20% |
| **Credit card** | **36–45%** |

*Table 12.2 — where card debt sits among Indian borrowing*

There is almost nothing you can invest in that reliably beats 42% a year. This is why clearing card debt comes before investing, every time.

## 12.2 – Where the 45 days come from

The free period is not a fixed 45 days. It depends on two dates, and knowing them is what lets you use it deliberately.

Your card has a **statement date**, when the bank totals up everything you spent that month. Then a **due date**, usually about 15 to 20 days later, by which you must pay.

Spending in one cycle is not billed until the next statement. So depending on when you buy, you get somewhere between about 20 and 50 days before payment is due.

![A purchase early in the cycle gets about fifty interest-free days; one made just before the statement gets about twenty](/diagrams/billing-cycle.svg)

*Fig 12.1 — the interest-free period depends entirely on when in the cycle you spend.*

### How to get the full 45–50 days

Say your statement is generated on the 1st and payment is due on the 18th:

| You buy on | It appears on | Payment due | Interest-free days |
|---|---|---|---|
| The 2nd, just after the statement | Next month's statement | The month after | **about 47** |
| The 15th, mid-cycle | Next month's statement | The month after | about 34 |
| The 30th, just before the statement | *This* statement | This month | about 19 |

*Table 12.3 — the same purchase, three different free periods*

![A purchase just after the statement gets 47 free days; just before it gets 19](/diagrams/free-period.svg)

*Fig 12.2 — the same rupees, very different free periods.*

**A large purchase made just after your statement date gets nearly seven weeks of free credit. The same purchase two days earlier gets under three.**

This is not a trick and the bank does not mind — it is how the product works. If you are buying something expensive and the timing is flexible, check your statement date first.

### What that free money is actually worth

Be realistic about the size of it. On Ramu's ₹21,806 a month, keeping that money in his savings account a few weeks longer earns him around **₹650 a year**. The float alone is not the reason to bother.

The bigger benefits are elsewhere:

| Benefit | Roughly worth |
|---|---|
| The float itself | about ₹650 a year on Ramu's spending |
| Rewards at ~1% | about ₹2,617 a year on ₹2,61,672 of annual spending |
| Credit history | Cheaper loans later — potentially lakhs, as [Chapter 11](/learn/credit-score) shows |
| Fraud protection | The disputed money is the bank's, not yours, while it is investigated |

*Table 12.4 — why a well-used card is worth having*

> **Do this.** Route your normal, already-budgeted spending through the card — groceries, fuel, bills, subscriptions. Not extra spending. The same money you were going to spend anyway now earns rewards, buys you weeks of free credit, and builds your credit file.

> **Careful — the arithmetic that ends this.** One month of revolving Ramu's ₹21,806 balance costs about ₹763 in interest. Six months costs about ₹4,999 — which is **nearly two years of rewards**, wiped out. Twelve months costs about ₹11,144, or more than four years of rewards. The free period is only free for people who never miss.

### Where it stops being free

On the due date, one of three things happens.

| What you do | What it costs |
|---|---|
| **Pay the full amount** | Nothing. No interest, no fee. The free loan worked |
| **Pay part of it** | Interest on the *entire* balance, usually from the purchase date — not just on what is left |
| **Pay nothing** | Interest, plus a late fee, plus a mark on your credit report |

*Table 12.5 — the three outcomes on the due date*

> **Careful — the detail that costs people the most.** If you do not pay in full, most banks charge interest from the **date of each purchase**, not from the due date. Your interest-free period disappears retrospectively. And your *new* purchases start accruing interest immediately too, until the balance is fully cleared.

## 12.3 – The minimum due trap

Your statement shows a "minimum amount due" — typically about 5% of the balance.

Paying it keeps your account in good standing and protects your credit score. It does almost nothing about the debt.

Take a ₹60,000 balance at 42% a year, paying only the minimum each month:

| | Paying minimum due | Paying ₹5,500 a month |
|---|---|---|
| Time to clear | Over 10 years | 14 months |
| Interest paid | More than the original balance | About ₹16,000 |
| Total paid | Nearly ₹1,58,000 | About ₹76,000 |

*Table 12.6 — illustrative, at about 40% a year. The minimum-due path costs more in interest than the thing you bought.*

![Paying the minimum due takes over ten years and costs more in interest than the original purchase; a fixed payment clears it in fourteen months](/diagrams/minimum-due.svg)

*Fig 12.3 — the minimum due is designed to keep the balance alive, not to clear it.*

The minimum due is not a payment plan. It is the smallest amount that stops the account defaulting, and the balance is designed to survive it.

> **Do this if you are carrying a balance.** Pay a fixed amount every month that is far above the minimum, and stop using the card until it is cleared. Any new spending simply refills the hole.

## 12.4 – The charges nobody reads about

Interest is the big one, but these catch people out.

| Charge | What triggers it | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| **Late payment fee** | Missing the due date | ₹500–1,300, by balance |
| **Cash withdrawal** | Taking cash from an ATM on the card | 2.5–3% of the amount, **plus interest from day one** — no free period at all |
| **Overlimit fee** | Spending past your limit | A fee, plus the utilisation damage |
| **Annual fee** | Simply holding the card | ₹0–10,000, often waived on spending |
| **Foreign transaction markup** | Spending abroad or on foreign sites | 1.5–3.5% on top of the exchange rate |
| **Fuel surcharge** | Buying petrol, often waived up to a cap | About 1% |
| **EMI conversion** | Converting a purchase to instalments | Interest plus a processing fee |

*Table 12.7 — the fee list, roughly. Exact figures vary by card.*

> **Do not** withdraw cash on a credit card. It is the single most expensive thing the card can do: a fee of around 3% immediately, and interest from the moment of withdrawal with no interest-free period whatsoever. If you need cash urgently, almost any other source is cheaper.

### "No cost EMI" is usually not

When a purchase is converted to EMI at "no cost", the interest is often built into the price, or shown as a discount you would otherwise have received. Sometimes there is also a processing fee, and GST applies to the interest component.

It may still be reasonable. But ask one question: **what is the price if I pay in full today?** If that number is lower, the EMI was not free.

## 12.5 – How to use one so it costs you nothing

The rules are short.

1. **Treat the limit as a boundary, not a budget.** A ₹2 lakh limit is not ₹2 lakh you can spend
2. **Only buy what you could pay for today** from your bank account. The card is a payment convenience, not extra income
3. **Set auto-debit for the full statement amount**, not the minimum. This single setting prevents almost every problem in this chapter
4. **Keep utilisation under 30%** of your limit, measured on the statement date — see the [credit score chapter](/learn/credit-score) for why
5. **Never withdraw cash**
6. **Check the statement every month.** Wrong charges and subscriptions you forgot are common
7. **Pay before the statement date** if a large purchase would push utilisation high

Do those seven and the card costs you nothing, builds your credit history, and gives you 20 to 50 days of free credit on every purchase.

### What a card is genuinely good for

- **Building credit history** cheaply — the main reason to have one in your twenties
- **Fraud protection.** Disputed card transactions are usually easier to reverse than a UPI transfer, and your own money is not gone while it is investigated
- **A real emergency**, when the alternative is a costlier loan — though the [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) is the proper answer
- **Genuine rewards**, if you were going to make the purchase anyway

> **Do not** spend to earn rewards. Spending ₹10,000 to earn ₹200 of points is a ₹9,800 loss. Rewards are a small discount on spending you had already decided on, nothing more.

## 12.6 – How to choose your first card

This is where beginners get lost, because every card is marketed as the best one. It is simpler than it looks, and it comes down to four questions in order.

### Step 1 — Can you even get one?

Approval depends on your income and your credit history. Be honest about which situation you are in:

| Your situation | What to apply for |
|---|---|
| **Salaried, no credit history** | Start with the bank where your salary is credited. They can see your income and approve more readily |
| **No history, rejected already** | A **secured card** against a fixed deposit. The bank holds your FD, so approval is near-automatic |
| **Student or no income yet** | A secured card, or an add-on card on a parent's account |
| **Existing borrower with a clean record** | Most cards are open to you. Choose on the rest of this section |

*Table 12.8 — the realistic starting point*

A secured card is not a lesser product. The bank takes no risk because it holds your deposit, so it approves almost anyone — and it reports to the bureaus exactly like any other card, which is the whole purpose.

### Step 2 — Match the rewards to what you actually buy

This is the step people get wrong. They chase the card with the biggest headline reward rate, then discover the rewards are on categories they never spend in.

Look at your own last three months of spending first. For Ramu, the card-able spending is groceries, eating out, shopping and subscriptions — so a card rewarding only fuel and travel would earn him almost nothing.

| If most of your spending is | Look for a card that rewards |
|---|---|
| Groceries and everyday shopping | Supermarkets, or a flat rate on everything |
| Fuel | Fuel, with the surcharge waiver |
| Online shopping and food delivery | Online spends, or partnered platforms |
| Travel and flights | Travel, but only if you genuinely travel often |
| A bit of everything | **A flat-rate cashback card.** Simplest and hardest to get wrong |

*Table 12.9 — start from your spending, not from the card*

> **Do this.** For a first card, a **flat-rate cashback card with no annual fee** is the safest choice. You cannot mis-optimise it, the reward arrives as money rather than points, and there is nothing to track.

### Step 3 — Check the four things that actually cost you

Ignore the marketing. These four decide whether the card is good:

| Check | What you want | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual fee** | ₹0, or waived on spending you will actually reach | A ₹1,000 fee needs ₹1,00,000 of spending at 1% just to break even |
| **Reward rate and cap** | A clear percentage, and a monthly cap you will not hit instantly | "5% cashback" capped at ₹100 a month is really ₹1,200 a year |
| **Redemption** | Cashback to the statement, or points that convert simply | Points that expire, or need 10,000 to redeem, are decoration |
| **Joining conditions** | No compulsory insurance or bundled product | If you must buy something to get the card, the card is not free |

*Table 12.10 — the four numbers that matter*

**The fee-waiver trap.** Many cards waive the annual fee if you spend a certain amount in a year. That sounds generous until you notice it can push you to spend more than you planned, purely to avoid a ₹500 fee. Check the threshold is something your normal spending already reaches.

### Step 4 — Take the right limit, not the biggest one

Banks often offer a larger limit than you asked for. Whether that is good depends entirely on you.

| | A higher limit helps | A higher limit hurts |
|---|---|---|
| **If you always pay in full** | Yes — it lowers your utilisation percentage, which helps your score | |
| **If a bigger number changes how you spend** | | Yes — it is a temptation dressed as a benefit |

*Table 12.11 — you know which of the two you are*

### What not to choose a card for

| Do not choose based on | Because |
|---|---|
| Airport lounge access | Nice, but you will pay for it in the annual fee |
| A big joining bonus | One-time. The ongoing rate matters more over years |
| Co-branded offers with one retailer | Locks your rewards to a shop you may stop using |
| "Lifetime free" alone | Check the reward rate too — free and useless is still useless |
| What your friend has | Their spending is not your spending |

*Table 12.12 — common reasons people pick the wrong card*

### A reasonable first card, in one line

**No annual fee, flat-rate cashback on everything, from the bank where your salary is credited — and a secured card if you are rejected.**

That is not the highest-reward card available. It is the one hardest to get wrong, and for a first card that matters more.

> **Careful.** One card is enough to start. A second becomes useful later — it raises your total limit, which lowers utilisation, and gives you a backup if one is blocked. Beyond two, you are managing due dates for very little gain.

## 12.7 – If you are already carrying a balance

This is common and it is fixable. The order matters.

1. **Stop using the card entirely.** Move to UPI or debit until the balance is zero
2. **List every card**, with its balance and its rate
3. **Pay the highest rate first** while paying the minimum on the others. This costs the least in total
4. **Consider a cheaper source.** A personal loan at 14% to clear a card at 42% is a real saving — provided you do not then re-use the card
5. **Ask the bank about converting the balance to EMI.** Rates are typically far below the card rate. Get the total cost in writing before agreeing
6. **Talk to the bank early if you genuinely cannot pay.** Restructuring is far better for your record than a settlement, which is close to the worst mark a credit report can carry

> **Careful.** A balance transfer to another card buys you a low-rate window, not a solution. If the balance is not cleared inside that window, the rate reverts and you are back where you started, with one more card open.

## 12.8 – When things go wrong

Most of this chapter assumes things go normally. They sometimes do not. These are the situations worth knowing *before* they happen, because all of them are time-sensitive.

### Card lost or stolen

**Block it first, then report.** In that order — blocking takes seconds in the app and stops further misuse immediately.

1. **Block the card** through the app, netbanking, or the bank's 24-hour number
2. **Report it formally**, and note the date and time you did
3. **Check the statement** for transactions you did not make
4. **Dispute those in writing**, not just by phone
5. **Ask for a replacement**, which usually arrives in about a week

Under RBI rules on unauthorised electronic transactions, your liability is limited when you report promptly and the loss was not due to your own negligence. **Speed is what protects you**, so report the same day you notice — do not wait to be sure.

> **Do this now, before you need it.** Save your bank's card-blocking number in your phone. During an actual theft you will not want to be searching for it, and the minutes matter.

### A transaction you did not make

This happens without the card leaving your pocket — card details get stolen online far more often than cards get physically lifted.

| Do | Do not |
|---|---|
| Report within a day or two of noticing | Wait to "see if it reverses on its own" |
| Put the dispute in writing, keep a copy | Rely only on a phone call |
| Block the card if details are compromised | Keep using a card you suspect |
| Check older statements too | Assume one fraud is the only one |

*Table 12.13 — disputing a charge*

The real advantage of a credit card shows here: **the disputed money is the bank's while it is investigated, not yours.** With a debit card or UPI, your own money is gone until it is resolved.

### A refund that has not arrived

Refunds go back to the card, not to your bank account, and they take longer than the purchase did — often a week or two.

If a refund is pending when your due date arrives, **you still owe the full statement amount.** Pay it. The refund will land later and sit as a credit balance on the card, which the next month's spending uses up. Paying less because "a refund is coming" is a missed payment.

### The auto-debit failed

Auto-debit is the single best setting on a card, and it does fail sometimes — an expired debit mandate, insufficient balance on the wrong day, or a changed account.

| Why it failed | What to do |
|---|---|
| Not enough balance that morning | Pay manually the same day. Expect a bank penalty for the failed mandate |
| Mandate expired or account changed | Pay manually, then set the mandate up again |
| You did not notice for weeks | Pay in full immediately, then call and ask for the fee to be reversed |

*Table 12.14 — a failed auto-debit is fixable if you catch it*

This is why the reminder matters. **Set a calendar alert two days before your due date**, even with auto-debit running. The alert is what catches the failure.

### Spending abroad or on foreign websites

Two costs that surprise people:

| Charge | Roughly |
|---|---|
| **Foreign transaction markup** | 1.5–3.5% on top of the exchange rate |
| **Dynamic currency conversion** | The shop offers to bill you in rupees, at a worse rate than your bank's |

*Table 12.15 — the cost of spending in another currency*

> **Do this abroad.** When a terminal or website asks whether to charge you in rupees or the local currency, **always choose the local currency.** Being billed in rupees sounds convenient and means the merchant picks the exchange rate, which is reliably worse than your bank's. This applies to foreign websites too.

Also tell your bank before you travel, so a legitimate foreign transaction is not blocked as suspicious.

### Closing a card properly

If you do decide to close one:

1. **Clear the balance to zero** first, including any pending EMI conversions
2. **Redeem any reward points** — they usually vanish on closure
3. **Move any subscriptions** billed to that card. This is the step people forget, and a failed renewal can cut off a service
4. **Ask for written confirmation** of closure and a no-dues confirmation
5. **Check your credit report** after a month or two to confirm it shows as closed

Remember from 12.6 that closing a card raises your utilisation and shortens your history. **Asking the bank to downgrade a fee-charging card to a free variant keeps the account alive** and is usually the better move.

### If you genuinely cannot pay

Talk to the bank early, before the due date if possible. Banks have options for customers who contact them and far fewer for customers who disappear.

| Option | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Convert the balance to EMI** | A fixed repayment at a much lower rate than 42% |
| **Restructuring** | Revised terms. Affects your report, but far less than default |
| **Settlement** | Paying less than you owe. **Avoid if at all possible** |

*Table 12.16 — what to ask for, in order of preference*

A **"settled"** status is close to the worst mark a credit report can carry, and it follows you for years — see [Chapter 11](/learn/credit-score). Restructuring is almost always the better route.

> **Careful.** Ignoring it is the worst option. Unpaid card debt goes to recovery, the interest keeps compounding at over 40% a year, and the record damage lasts far longer than the temporary difficulty that caused it.

## 12.10 – Questions people actually ask

#### Should I have a credit card at all?

If you can pay in full every month, yes — it is the cheapest way to build a credit history, and it gives you fraud protection that UPI does not.

If you have carried a balance before and found it hard to stop, be honest about that. A debit card and a good credit history built through a small consumer loan is a reasonable alternative.

#### Is it bad to use my card a lot if I always pay in full?

Not for your finances, but watch the utilisation. If you spend ₹1,60,000 on a ₹2,00,000 limit and pay in full, your score can still drop, because utilisation is measured on the statement date.

Pay part of the bill before the statement is generated, or ask for a higher limit.

#### How many cards should I have?

One is enough to start. Two is reasonable once you are comfortable — a second card raises your total limit, which lowers utilisation, and gives you a backup if one is blocked.

Beyond that you are managing due dates for little benefit.

#### The bank keeps offering to increase my limit. Should I accept?

Usually yes, *if* your spending will not rise with it. A higher limit lowers your utilisation percentage for the same spending, which helps your score.

Decline it if a bigger number would change how you spend. You know which of the two you are.

#### I missed a due date by two days. How bad is it?

Pay it immediately, in full. Expect a late fee and interest on the whole balance.

Then call the bank. If your record is otherwise clean and this is a first lapse, many will reverse the fee as a courtesy. Ask politely — it works more often than people expect.

A single late payment, quickly cleared, does limited lasting damage. A pattern is what hurts.

#### What is the difference between a credit card and a debit card?

A debit card spends your own money from your bank account. A credit card borrows the bank's money and you repay later.

That is why only the credit card builds a credit history, and why only the credit card can cost you interest.

#### Should I convert a big purchase to EMI?

Compare it against paying in full. If you can pay in full without emptying your emergency fund, do that.

If you genuinely cannot, EMI on the card is usually much cheaper than letting the balance revolve at 42%. Ask for the total amount payable, including processing fee and GST, and compare that against the cash price.

#### My card was used fraudulently. What do I do?

Report it to the bank immediately, by phone and then in writing, and block the card. Under RBI rules on unauthorised electronic transactions, a customer's liability is limited when the fraud is reported promptly and is not due to their own negligence — so speed genuinely matters.

Keep a written record of when you reported it. This is one real advantage of a credit card: the disputed amount is the bank's money while it is investigated, not yours.

#### Does closing a card hurt my score?

It can, especially if it is your oldest card or a large part of your total limit. Closing removes that limit from the utilisation calculation and shortens your average account age.

If a card has a fee you do not want to pay, ask the bank to downgrade it to a free variant instead of closing it.

#### Are reward points and cashback worth chasing?

Only on spending you would have done anyway. Typical returns are around 1% — pleasant, not transformative.

The moment a card's rewards influence *what* or *how much* you buy, the card is winning and you are not.

- A credit card is a short-term loan. It is free if you repay in full and on time, and one of India's costliest borrowings if you do not.
- Used properly it is genuinely free credit for 20 to 50 days, plus around 1% back on spending you were doing anyway, plus a credit history worth lakhs later.
- Only part of your spending can go on a card. Rent, transfers and most transport cannot — so the realistic amount is smaller than your salary.
- A purchase just after your statement date gets about 47 interest-free days. The same purchase just before it gets about 19.
- Card interest runs at roughly 36–45% a year. Nothing you invest in reliably beats that, which is why clearing card debt comes before investing.
- If you do not pay in full, interest is usually charged from each purchase date — the interest-free period disappears retrospectively.
- The minimum due is not a payment plan. On a ₹60,000 balance it takes over ten years and costs more in interest than the original purchase.
- Never withdraw cash on a credit card. A fee immediately, and interest from day one.
- Set auto-debit for the full statement amount. That one setting prevents almost every problem in this chapter.
- Keep utilisation under 30% of your limit, measured on the statement date.
- For a first card: no annual fee, flat-rate cashback, from the bank where your salary lands. A secured card against an FD if you are rejected.
