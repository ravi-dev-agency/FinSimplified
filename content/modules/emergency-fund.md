## 2.1 – What an emergency fund is

An emergency fund is money you keep aside for unexpected problems.

You do not use it for shopping. You do not use it for a trip. You do not invest it.

You keep it in a bank account and you forget about it. It sits there and waits. The day something goes wrong, that money is ready.

**Technical meaning:** money held in a safe and easily reachable place, used only for unexpected and urgent expenses, or when your income stops.

**Simple meaning:** money kept aside so that when something goes wrong, you do not have to borrow.

### A simple example

Ramu is 26. He works in Hyderabad and takes home ₹58,000 a month.

He needs ₹32,789 a month for the things he cannot skip — rent, groceries, his bike EMI, transport, insurance, electricity and phone.

So he keeps **₹32,789 × 6 = about ₹1,96,700** in a separate savings account.

If he loses his job tomorrow, he can live normally for six months while he looks for a new one.

## 2.2 – Why it matters

Because problems will come. Nobody can stop them.

- Your company can suddenly remove you from the job
- A parent can fall ill
- Your bike or laptop can break
- A hospital bill can arrive that insurance does not fully cover

These things happen to everyone. The question is not *if*. The question is **where the money will come from**.

With a fund, it comes from your own account. Without one, it comes from a credit card or a loan — and that is very expensive.

### The same problem, two people

Two friends. Both get a hospital bill of ₹60,000.

| | Ramu (has a fund) | Kiran (no fund) |
|---|---|---|
| Where the money came from | His savings account | Credit card |
| Interest paid | ₹0 | About ₹16,000 |
| Time to clear it | Same day | 14 months |
| His SIP | Kept running | Stopped for a year |
| **Total cost** | **₹60,000** | **About ₹76,000** |

*Table 2.1 — the cost of not having the money ready. Assumes a card rate of about 40% a year, cleared in equal instalments over 14 months.*

Kiran paid ₹16,000 extra, and stopped investing for a full year. Same problem, very different result.

And that assumes he paid it down steadily. Paying only the **minimum due** each month — which is what most people actually do — would take him over ten years and cost more than the original bill in interest.

![Ramu pays sixty thousand from savings; Kiran pays about seventy-six thousand on a credit card over fourteen months](/diagrams/fund-vs-card.svg)

*Fig 2.1 — the gap is pure interest, paid for not having ₹60,000 ready.*

### This is why it comes before investing

Many people start a SIP first, because everyone says "start early". Starting early is good advice. But there is a problem.

When an emergency comes and you have no fund, the first thing you stop is the SIP. Or you sell investments at a bad time. Or you reach for a credit card.

A credit card costs roughly 36% to 45% a year. A mutual fund may give around 10% to 12%. You cannot win that fight.

> **Do this first.** Build the emergency fund, then invest. But you do not need the full amount before you start — build about ₹25,000, clear any credit card balance, then begin your SIP and keep filling the fund alongside it.

## 2.3 – How much you actually need

Many people say "keep six months of salary". That is wrong, and it frightens beginners away.

You need six months of **essential expenses**, not salary. That is a much smaller number.

Here is Ramu's list, taken from the month he wrote down in [Chapter 1](/learn/budgeting):

| Things he cannot skip | Per month |
|---|---|
| Rent | ₹16,000 |
| Groceries | ₹6,400 |
| Bike EMI | ₹4,200 |
| Transport and fuel | ₹2,100 |
| Health insurance premium | ₹1,850 |
| Electricity and water | ₹1,340 |
| Phone and internet | ₹899 |
| **Total** | **₹32,789** |

*Table 2.2 — essential expenses, not salary*

His take-home is ₹58,000. The other ₹18,407 went on eating out, shopping, subscriptions and a weekend trip — all of which can stop in a real problem.

### How many months?

| Your situation | Months to keep |
|---|---|
| Government job, nobody depends on you | 3 |
| Private job, single | 4–6 |
| You are the only earning member | 6–9 |
| Freelancer or your own business | 9–12 |
| Your industry is cutting jobs right now | 12 |

*Table 2.3 — months by how replaceable your income is*

> **Careful — two things people leave out.** EMIs, because the bank will not pause your loan just because you lost your job. And insurance premiums, because if a health policy lapses you lose the waiting periods you have already served.

## 2.4 – Where to keep it

The money must follow two rules: you can get it within a day, and the amount cannot fall.

Returns are not the point here. Safety is.

| Where | How fast you get it | Roughly earns |
|---|---|---|
| Savings account, different bank | Instant | 2.5–7% |
| Sweep-in FD | Instant | FD rate |
| Fixed deposit | Same day to two days | 6–7.5% |
| Liquid mutual fund | Next working day | Varies |

*Table 2.4 — where the money can sit*

**The simplest answer for a beginner:** open a separate savings account at a different bank. Do not link UPI to it. Once it crosses ₹50,000, move some into fixed deposits.

> **Do this.** Break large FDs into small ones. Three FDs of ₹25,000 beat one of ₹75,000 — a ₹15,000 problem should not force you to break the whole thing.

### Never keep it here

- **Stocks or equity mutual funds** — the value can be down 20% on the exact day you need it
- **ELSS funds** — locked for three years
- **PPF, EPF, NPS** — locked, you cannot withdraw this week
- **Land or property** — takes months to sell
- **Gold jewellery** — you lose making charges, and families resist selling it
- **Your salary account** — it will quietly get spent

### Your credit card limit is not an emergency fund

Many people think: "I have a ₹2 lakh limit, so I am covered."

You are not. That is the bank's money, not yours. It costs 36% to 45% a year. And the bank can reduce or block that limit at any time — including right after you lose your job, which is exactly when you would need it.

## 2.5 – What it gives you

- You do not have to borrow when a problem comes
- You do not have to sell investments at a bad time
- Your SIP keeps running, so the long-term plan is not damaged
- You can leave a bad job without panic
- You sleep better
- Your family does not have to ask relatives for money

### And what it costs

Being honest about this matters.

- **The money grows slowly.** A savings account earns less than inflation. On about ₹1,96,700 you may lose around ₹5,900 a year in real value
- **It takes time.** For Ramu it took two years. Many people give up before that
- **It is easy to spend by mistake** if you keep it in the wrong account

But treat that as a price, not a loss. Kiran paid ₹16,000 extra on a single emergency — more than five years of Ramu's "loss", in one event.

You are not trying to earn money here. You are buying safety.

## 2.6 – A full worked example

**Meera, 29, Pune. Take-home ₹55,000.**

**Step 1 — her essential expenses:**

| Item | Per month |
|---|---|
| Rent | ₹15,000 |
| Groceries | ₹6,000 |
| Bills and phone | ₹2,500 |
| Travel | ₹2,000 |
| Home loan EMI | ₹8,000 |
| Insurance premium (monthly share) | ₹1,500 |
| **Total** | **₹35,000** |

*Table 2.5 — Meera's essentials*

**Step 2 — how many months.** She is married and her husband also earns, but they have a home loan. She chooses six.

**Step 3 — the target.** ₹35,000 × 6 = **₹2,10,000**

**Step 4 — the account.** A savings account at a different bank, with no UPI linked.

**Step 5 — the transfer.** ₹9,000 automatically on the 2nd of every month.

| Month | Saved | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| 3 | ₹24,000 | A phone, a small repair, a doctor visit |
| 8 | ₹64,000 | Two months of expenses |
| 12 | ₹96,000 | Nearly three months of expenses |
| 25 | ₹2,00,000 | Target reached |

*Table 2.6 — the fund does its job long before it is finished*

She also put her Diwali bonus straight in, so she actually finished in 20 months. After that she stopped adding and moved the ₹9,000 into a SIP instead.

## 2.7 – How to build it, step by step

1. **Find your essential monthly expenses.** Only what you must pay if the salary stops. Not shopping, outside food or subscriptions
2. **Decide the months** — three to six for most people, more if your income is uneven
3. **Multiply.** Essentials × months = your target
4. **Open a separate account** at a different bank. Do not link UPI
5. **Automate the transfer** for the day after your salary arrives. Even ₹2,000 a month is a real start
6. **Stop at the target.** Do not keep adding forever — move that money into investments instead
7. **Use it when a real emergency comes, then refill it.** That is what it is for

## 2.8 – Who needs one

**Everyone.** It is most urgent if you are in a private job, the only earner in your family, a freelancer or running a business, paying EMIs, supporting parents, or working in an industry that is cutting jobs.

**Students** should keep a small amount too — ₹10,000 to ₹20,000 covers a phone repair or a train ticket home without having to ask anyone.

## 2.9 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **1. Starting a SIP with no emergency fund** | Your first problem forces you to stop the SIP or borrow |
| **2. Thinking a credit card limit is enough** | It is the costliest money you can borrow, and it can be withdrawn |
| **3. Keeping the fund in stocks or equity funds** | It can be down 20% on the exact day you need it |
| **4. Sizing it against salary instead of expenses** | The target looks impossible, so people never start |
| **5. Keeping it in the salary account** | It quietly gets spent without you noticing |
| **6. Forgetting EMIs and insurance premiums** | Neither bill stops when your salary does |
| **7. Putting everything in one large FD** | A ₹15,000 problem forces you to break the whole ₹1,96,700 |
| **8. Not refilling it after use** | It saved you once; the next problem is still coming |
| **9. Using it for sales, trips or a new phone** | The fund is empty on the day you actually need it |
| **10. Thinking health insurance replaces it** | Insurance pays the hospital, not your rent |
| **11. Never revisiting the target** | The number that fits at 24 does not fit at 32 |
| **12. Waiting for the right time to start** | There is no right time |

## 2.10 – Questions people actually ask

#### I already started a SIP and have no emergency fund. What now?

Do not stop everything. Reduce the SIP for a few months and send that money to the fund until you reach about ₹25,000. Then raise the SIP again and keep building the fund slowly alongside it.

#### I earn only ₹18,000 a month. Can I still do this?

Yes. Your expenses are smaller too, so your target is smaller. Start with ₹500 or ₹1,000 a month. In the beginning the habit matters far more than the amount.

#### Should I keep it in an FD or a liquid fund?

Both are fine. An FD is simpler. A liquid fund is easier to withdraw in parts. Both are taxed at your income slab rate, so pick whichever is more convenient for you.

#### I have health insurance. Do I still need this?

Yes. Health insurance pays the hospital. It does not pay your rent and food during the weeks you cannot work, and it usually leaves some part of the bill with you.

#### I used the whole fund. Did I do something wrong?

No — that is exactly what it was for. Refilling it now becomes your first priority, ahead of any new spending.

#### I lost my job. Should I use the fund or take a loan?

Use the fund. Taking on an EMI with no income is the worst possible timing, and banks are least willing to lend to you at that moment anyway.

#### My parents will help me if something happens.

That is good to have, but it is not a plan. It depends on their situation at that time — and very often the emergency is theirs.

#### I reached my target. Should I keep adding?

No. Stop, and move that money into investments. Check the target once a year and raise it only if your expenses have genuinely gone up.

## Key takeaways

- An emergency fund is cash for the month things go wrong, not an investment.
- Size it against essential monthly expenses, never against salary. That one change makes the target achievable.
- Three to twelve months, depending on how quickly you could replace your income. Include EMIs and insurance premiums.
- Keep it somewhere you can reach the same day and where the value cannot fall — a separate bank account, not your salary account.
- Your credit card limit is not an emergency fund. It is the bank's money, at 36–45% a year, and it can be withdrawn.
- Build about ₹25,000 before you start investing, then do both together.
- Use it without guilt when a real emergency comes, then refill it.
