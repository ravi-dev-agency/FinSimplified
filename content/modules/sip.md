## 9.1 – Start with why, not with how

Most people who start a SIP stop within a few years. Not because they ran out of money — because the market fell and they panicked.

Everything in this chapter comes down to one chain:

**A strong why → you don't stop when the market falls → you don't sell at the bottom → you keep buying cheap → your average cost stays low → your long-term goal is reached.**

Read it backwards and it works too. People who reach their goal are simply the ones who did not stop. People who did not stop are the ones who had a reason worth not stopping for.

That is the real subject of this chapter. The mechanics take one evening. The reason has to last twenty years.

### The fall is the opportunity, not the emergency

This is the part that separates people who succeed at SIPs from people who quit.

When the market falls, your ₹5,000 buys more units than usual. Same money, more of the fund. A falling market is the only time your SIP gets a discount.

| What most people see | What is actually happening |
|---|---|
| "My investment is down 30%" | Your monthly amount now buys 30% more |
| "I should stop before it gets worse" | This is the cheapest month in years |
| "I have lost money" | You have lost nothing unless you sell |

*Table 9.1 — the same event, two readings*

**A fall is not a loss. It only becomes a loss when you sell.** Until then it is just a lower price on something you are still buying.

This is why the two halves fit together. A long-term goal is what makes you able to see a crash as a sale rather than a disaster — because you are not spending that money for fifteen years, so this year's price is not your problem.

### The question that decides everything

Before the amount, before the fund, before the date, answer this:

**What am I actually doing this for?**

"To invest" is not an answer. "To get good returns" is not an answer. These are not goals, they are moods, and a mood cannot survive a market crash.

An answer looks like this:

| Not a reason | A reason |
|---|---|
| "I should start investing" | "₹20 lakh for a flat deposit, in 7 years" |
| "Everyone is doing SIPs" | "₹30 lakh for my daughter's education, in 15 years" |
| "To build wealth" | "₹3 crore so I can stop working at 55" |

*Table 9.2 — the difference between a wish and a goal*

A goal has a **number** and a **year**. Both matter. The number tells you how much to invest; the year tells you where to invest it and how long to stay.

### What your goal costs each month

Once the goal is real, the monthly amount stops being a guess. At an assumed 12% a year:

| Your goal | Amount | By when | Needed each month |
|---|---|---|---|
| A car | ₹8,00,000 | 4 years | ₹12,938 |
| Flat down payment | ₹20,00,000 | 7 years | ₹15,154 |
| Child's education | ₹30,00,000 | 15 years | ₹5,946 |
| Retirement | ₹3,00,00,000 | 25 years | ₹15,809 |

*Table 9.3 — illustration at an assumed 12%. Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

Look carefully at the last two rows. **₹30 lakh in 15 years needs ₹5,946 a month. ₹3 crore in 25 years needs ₹15,809.** A goal ten times larger costs under three times as much each month — because it has ten more years to compound.

That is the single most encouraging fact in personal finance, and it only works for people who do not stop.

> **Do this now, before reading further.** Write one sentence: "I am investing ₹____ a month for ____, which I need in ____." Put it somewhere you will find it again. In year three, when your balance is below what you put in and the news is telling you it will get worse, that sentence is the only thing standing between you and a decision you will regret.

### What stopping actually costs

Say you start a 20-year SIP of ₹5,000 a month and stop after three years, because it did not seem to be working.

| | After 20 years |
|---|---|
| Kept going | ₹49,95,740 |
| Stopped at year 3, left the money invested | ₹16,56,135 |
| **Given up** | **₹33,39,604** |

*Table 9.4 — illustration at an assumed 12%*

Those three years of instalments were not wasted — they kept growing. What was lost is the seventeen years of instalments that never happened.

**The person who stopped did not lose money in the market. They lost it by leaving.**

## 9.2 – What a SIP actually is

Now the mechanics, which are genuinely simple.

**SIP stands for Systematic Investment Plan. It is a method — investing a fixed amount, on a fixed date, every month, automatically.**

A SIP is not an investment. You do not "buy a SIP". You buy a mutual fund, and choose to pay for it monthly instead of in one lump.

| This is the investment | This is the method |
|---|---|
| A mutual fund | SIP — ₹5,000 on the 5th, every month |
| An index fund | SIP — ₹2,000 on the 1st, every month |
| An ETF | Usually bought manually, not by SIP |

*Table 9.5 — the fund is what you own. The SIP is how you pay for it.*

Once set up, your bank sends the money on the chosen date and units of the fund arrive in your account. You do nothing else.

> **Do this.** When someone asks "which SIP should I take?", translate it to "which fund should I buy?" — that is the real question, and it is answered in [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds).

### Why it exists

Most people receive money monthly, from a salary. They do not have ₹5 lakh sitting ready, but they can spare ₹5,000 a month.

A SIP matches investing to how income actually arrives. That is why it is the default for almost every salaried investor in India.

## 9.3 – Why a fixed monthly amount works

There are two real advantages, and one of them is far more important than the other.

### It removes the timing decision

The hardest question in investing is "is now a good time?" Nobody knows, including the people who sound most certain.

A SIP answers the question by refusing to ask it. You buy every month regardless — some months near a high, some near a low. You stop needing to predict anything.

### Rupee cost averaging

Because you invest a fixed *amount* rather than buying a fixed number of units, you automatically buy **more units when the price is low and fewer when it is high**.

Watch what happens through a falling and recovering market, investing ₹5,000 a month:

| Month | NAV | Units bought | Total units |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹50 | 100.0 | 100.0 |
| 2 | ₹44 | 113.6 | 213.6 |
| 3 | ₹38 | 131.6 | 345.2 |
| 4 | ₹34 | 147.1 | 492.3 |
| 5 | ₹40 | 125.0 | 617.3 |
| 6 | ₹48 | 104.2 | 721.4 |
| 7 | ₹55 | 90.9 | 812.3 |

*Table 9.6 — ₹35,000 invested across seven months. Illustration only.*

Look at month 4. The price has fallen 32% and the news is grim — and that is the month your ₹5,000 buys the most units of all.

![As the price falls the same 5,000 rupees buys more units, and as it rises it buys fewer](/diagrams/rupee-cost-averaging.svg)

*Fig 9.1 — the fixed amount does the buying for you: more units when cheap, fewer when expensive.*

The result:

| | |
|---|---|
| Total invested | ₹35,000 |
| Units owned | 812.3 |
| **Your average cost per unit** | **₹43.08** |
| Simple average of the seven prices | ₹44.14 |
| Value at the final NAV of ₹55 | **₹44,679** |

*Table 9.7 — you paid less per unit than the average price over the period*

Compare that with putting the whole ₹35,000 in at the start, when the NAV was ₹50. That buys 700 units, worth **₹38,500** at the end — about ₹6,000 less, from the same money.

> **Careful.** This works because the market fell and then recovered. In a market that only rises, a lump sum invested at the start would have done better. A SIP is not a way to earn more — it is a way to stop needing to be right about timing.

## 9.4 – What a SIP does not do

This section matters because the marketing around SIPs oversells them.

| A SIP does | A SIP does not |
|---|---|
| Remove the timing decision | Guarantee a profit |
| Average your purchase price | Protect you from a falling market |
| Build a monthly habit | Turn a bad fund into a good one |
| Make investing automatic | Beat a well-timed lump sum in a rising market |

*Table 9.8 — an honest scorecard*

**If the market is lower on the day you need the money, you will have less than you put in.** A SIP does not change that. It only improves your odds by spreading your purchases over time and letting you stay invested for longer.

The fund still matters. A SIP into an unsuitable fund is a disciplined way to end up somewhere you did not want to be.

## 9.5 – Check these three before you start

A SIP only works if you can leave it alone for years. Markets are volatile — that is normal and expected — and the whole plan depends on you not being forced to sell during a bad stretch.

Three things make sure you are never forced.

| Check this first | Why it matters for your SIP |
|---|---|
| **Emergency fund** — three to six months of expenses | Without it, a job loss or a car repair means selling units. Often in a falling market, which is the worst possible time |
| **Health insurance** | One hospital bill can be several lakh. Without cover it comes out of your investments, or a loan |
| **Term insurance** — only if someone depends on your income | Protects your family from your absence. It is not for you |

*Table 9.9 — the base that lets a SIP survive a volatile market*

### Why this matters more for a SIP than anything else

A SIP is a long promise. You are committing to keep buying for ten, fifteen, twenty years — through crashes, through bad news, through years when nothing seems to work.

**What breaks that promise is almost never the market. It is life.** A medical bill, a lost job, a family emergency. Without a base, those events reach straight into your investments and force a sale at whatever price the market happens to be offering that week.

That is how a temporary fall becomes a permanent loss. Not because markets fell, but because you had to sell while they were down.

The emergency fund and health cover exist so that a bad month never touches your SIP.

> **Careful.** Starting a SIP before the emergency fund exists feels productive, and it is the most common way beginners get hurt. The market does not need to do anything unusual — an ordinary emergency in an ordinary bad year is enough. Build the base first, even if it delays your SIP by six months.

Also clear any credit card balance first. A card charges roughly 36–45% a year, and no investment reliably beats that — see [Chapter 12](/learn/credit-cards).

Each of these has a chapter of its own: [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund), [health insurance](/learn/health-insurance), [term insurance](/learn/term-insurance).

## 9.6 – How to start one, step by step

The whole process takes an evening. Your KYC, once done, works across every platform.

1. **Decide the amount.** Something you can sustain every month without strain — see 9.7
2. **Choose one fund.** One broad, diversified fund is a complete starting portfolio. Not five
3. **Pick the platform** — the fund house's own site, your bank, or an investment app. Prefer **direct plans**, which cost less each year than regular plans
4. **Set the date.** Two or three days after your salary arrives, so the money leaves before you can spend it
5. **Set up the auto-debit** so it happens without you
6. **Leave it alone.** Check once a quarter at most

### Choosing the date

It matters far less than people think. Any date works, and the difference between the 1st and the 25th over twenty years is noise.

What does matter is that it falls **just after payday**. That single choice is the difference between investing what is left over and investing first.

> **Do this.** Pick the date and amount before you close this page. "I will start a SIP" fails. "₹3,000 on the 3rd, into one index fund" happens.

## 9.7 – How much should you invest?

Start with what you can sustain, not what a calculator says you need.

A SIP you stop after eight months is worse than a smaller one you keep for twenty years. The amount can always rise; a broken habit is much harder to restart.

| Your situation | A reasonable start |
|---|---|
| First job, tight budget | ₹500–1,000 a month |
| Salaried, base in place | 10–20% of take-home |
| Comfortable, no debt | As much as you will not miss |

*Table 9.10 — a starting point, not a rule*

### Why the amount matters less than you think

Over twenty years at an assumed 12%, here is what different monthly amounts become:

| Monthly | After 20 years |
|---|---|
| ₹1,000 | ₹9,99,148 |
| ₹2,000 | ₹19,98,296 |
| ₹3,000 | ₹29,97,444 |
| ₹5,000 | ₹49,95,740 |
| ₹10,000 | ₹99,91,479 |

*Table 9.11 — illustration at an assumed 12%. Not a promise; actual returns vary and may be lower.*

Notice it scales exactly — doubling the amount doubles the result. What does **not** scale in a straight line is time, which is the subject of 9.9.

### Step it up

Increase the amount every time your salary rises. Many platforms have a "step-up SIP" that raises it automatically each year.

This is the single easiest way to grow your investing without ever feeling poorer — the increase comes out of a raise you have not yet got used to spending.

## 9.8 – Where to put it

The short answer, if you are starting out: **one broad large cap or index fund is enough.** That is a complete, sensible SIP on its own, and you do not need a mix on day one.

Once you have been investing for a few months and want to split your amount across large cap, mid and small cap, gold and debt — that is a proper subject of its own, and it is [Chapter 10](/learn/sip-strategy).

> **Do this.** Start with one fund this month. Come back to the split later. Beginning matters far more than optimising, and an allocation you never start is worth nothing.

## 9.9 – Time, and why starting now matters most

Here is the same ₹5,000 a month, at an assumed 12%, held for different lengths of time:

| Years | You put in | It becomes | Growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | ₹3,00,000 | ₹4,12,432 | ₹1,12,432 |
| 10 | ₹6,00,000 | ₹11,61,695 | ₹5,61,695 |
| 15 | ₹9,00,000 | ₹25,22,880 | ₹16,22,880 |
| 20 | ₹12,00,000 | ₹49,95,740 | ₹37,95,740 |
| 25 | ₹15,00,000 | ₹94,88,175 | ₹79,88,175 |
| 30 | ₹18,00,000 | ₹1,76,49,569 | ₹1,58,49,569 |

*Table 9.12 — illustration at an assumed 12%, from the same formula as the [SIP calculator](/calculators/sip). Actual returns vary.*

![A five thousand rupee monthly SIP: growth is a small slice at five years and most of the total by thirty years](/diagrams/sip-growth.svg)

*Fig 9.2 — the blue is your money. The green is what time added.*

Look at the growth column rather than the value column. Over 5 years growth is a third of what you put in. Over 30 years it is nearly **nine times** what you put in.

That is compounding, and it does almost all of its work at the end — which is exactly why starting matters more than the amount.

### The cost of waiting

Same ₹5,000 a month, everyone stopping at 60:

| Start at | You put in | You end with |
|---|---|---|
| 25 | ₹21,00,000 | ₹3,24,76,345 |
| 30 | ₹18,00,000 | ₹1,76,49,569 |
| 35 | ₹15,00,000 | ₹94,88,175 |
| 40 | ₹12,00,000 | ₹49,95,740 |

*Table 9.13 — illustration at an assumed 12%*

Starting at 25 rather than 35 means investing ₹6,00,000 more — and ending with roughly **₹2.3 crore more**.

The early starter was not cleverer. Their money simply had ten more years to compound.

> **Do this.** If you are deciding between starting small now and starting properly later, start small now. The years matter more than the rupees, and you cannot buy the years back.

## 9.10 – The hard part: the first few years look disappointing

This is where most SIPs die, so it is worth seeing the numbers honestly.

Here are the first five years of a ₹5,000 SIP at an assumed 12%:

| After year | Invested | Value | Growth |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹60,000 | ₹64,047 | ₹4,047 |
| 2 | ₹1,20,000 | ₹1,36,216 | ₹16,216 |
| 3 | ₹1,80,000 | ₹2,17,538 | ₹37,538 |
| 4 | ₹2,40,000 | ₹3,09,174 | ₹69,174 |
| 5 | ₹3,00,000 | ₹4,12,432 | ₹1,12,432 |

*Table 9.14 — illustration at an assumed 12%. And note: real markets do not rise smoothly like this.*

After a full year of discipline, the growth is ₹4,047. That is the honest picture, and it is why people quit.

Worse, real markets do not behave like that table. In a bad year your total value can sit **below** what you have put in — you invested ₹1,20,000 and the account shows ₹1,05,000. That feels like failure. It is not.

**A SIP that is below cost is buying cheaply.** Those are the months that produce the later numbers in Table 9.12.

> **Careful.** Stopping a SIP because markets fell is the single most expensive habit in investing. You are switching off the mechanism precisely when it works hardest. If falls make you want to stop, you likely have more in equity than your horizon supports — fix the allocation, not the SIP.

### Decide now what you will do when it falls

Decide while nothing is happening, because during a crash you will not be thinking clearly.

A 30% fall will happen at some point in a twenty-year SIP. That is near certain, and it will always arrive with convincing reasons why this time is different.

| When this happens | I will |
|---|---|
| The market falls 10% | Nothing |
| The market falls 30% | Nothing. Keep the SIP running |
| It stays down for two years | Nothing. Keep the SIP running |
| Everyone says to sell | Re-read my goal sentence |
| I genuinely cannot afford it | Reduce the amount. Never cancel |

*Table 9.15 — write this down before you need it*

Having already decided means there is nothing left to decide when it matters.

**Four sentences worth keeping somewhere you will see them:**

- *My why is strong, so I will not stop.*
- *A market fall is an opportunity, not a reason to panic.*
- *A fall is not a loss unless I sell.*
- *My goal is long term, so I will get a good average.*

## 9.11 – Things that go wrong, and what to do

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| **The market has crashed** | Nothing. Keep it running. This is when it buys most |
| **You cannot afford it this month** | Reduce the amount rather than stopping. Most platforms allow it |
| **A payment bounced** | Usually just a bank penalty and a missed month. Fix the balance; the SIP continues |
| **You need the money** | If it is an emergency, that is what the [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) is for |
| **Your salary rose** | Increase the amount now, before the raise becomes normal |
| **The fund has done badly for years** | Compare against its benchmark and similar funds, not against your hopes. Switching is reasonable; switching often is not |

*Table 9.16 — the common situations*

### Pausing and stopping

Most platforms let you **pause** a SIP for a few months, which is far better than cancelling. A pause keeps the arrangement alive; cancelling means restarting from scratch, and most people never do.

Your existing units stay invested either way. Stopping a SIP does not sell anything — it only stops new purchases.

## 9.12 – SIP or lump sum?

Both are valid. It depends on where the money is.

| | Use a SIP | Use a lump sum |
|---|---|---|
| Money arrives monthly from salary | ✓ | |
| You already have a large amount | | ✓ (or stagger it) |
| You are nervous about timing | ✓ | |
| Market is at an all-time high | ✓ | |

*Table 9.17 — which suits which situation*

If you have a large amount and are uneasy about investing it all at once, a common approach is to park it in a liquid fund and move it into equity in instalments. Ask your platform about an **STP** (Systematic Transfer Plan), which automates exactly that.

There is no rule that a lump sum is riskier. Historically, investing early has often beaten waiting — but a SIP is easier to live with, and an approach you can actually stick to beats a better one you abandon.

## 9.13 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **Stopping when the market falls** | Switching it off exactly when it works hardest |
| **Starting five SIPs in five funds** | Overlapping holdings, more to track, no extra diversification |
| **Thinking a SIP guarantees profit** | It averages your cost. It does not remove risk |
| **Choosing the fund by last year's return** | Last year's winner is not next year's |
| **Setting the date before payday** | You invest what is left, which some months is nothing |
| **Never increasing the amount** | Your salary grew and your investing did not |
| **Using a SIP for money needed in two years** | Equity needs seven years or more. The date does not move |
| **Checking the value weekly** | Many chances to panic, no useful information |
| **Cancelling instead of pausing** | A pause can be restarted. Most cancellations never are |
| **Starting before the emergency fund exists** | The first emergency forces a sale at the worst time |

## 9.14 – Questions people actually ask

#### How much do I need to start a SIP?

Many funds allow ₹500 a month, some ₹100.

Start with an amount you are confident you can continue for years. You can raise it whenever you like.

#### Which is the best SIP?

There is no such thing — a SIP is a payment method, not a product. The real question is which fund to buy, and that is [Chapter 7](/learn/mutual-funds).

#### Can I stop a SIP any time?

Yes. There is no lock-in on the arrangement itself, and no penalty for stopping. Your existing units simply stay invested.

The exception is a tax-saving fund, where each instalment has its own lock-in period.

#### What happens if I miss a payment?

Usually nothing serious — your bank may charge a failed-mandate fee, and that month's instalment does not happen. The SIP continues next month.

If several bounce, some platforms cancel the mandate and you will need to set it up again.

#### Is a SIP better than a fixed deposit?

They are different tools. An FD gives a known return with no movement, and after tax and inflation it often barely holds its value — see [Chapter 5](/learn/how-to-start-investing).

A SIP into an equity fund can fall in value and has historically beaten inflation by a wide margin over long periods. Use an FD for money you need within three years, a SIP for money you do not.

#### My SIP is showing a loss. Should I stop?

If the money is not needed for years, no. A fund below your average cost means your instalments are buying more units than usual.

The question to ask is not "is it down?" but "has anything changed about why I chose this fund?" Usually nothing has.

#### How many SIPs should I have?

One is enough to start. Two or three is reasonable once you have a larger amount and a reason for each.

Five SIPs in five similar funds is not diversification — the funds will mostly hold the same companies.

#### Should I pick a date when markets are low?

You cannot know that in advance, which is the whole point. Pick the date that follows your salary and stop thinking about it.

#### What is a step-up SIP?

One that increases automatically each year, often by a percentage you choose. It is a good way to keep your investing in line with your income without having to remember.

#### Do I pay tax on a SIP?

Not on the SIP itself. Tax applies when you sell units, and **each instalment has its own holding period** — so one withdrawal can include both short-term and long-term units.

## Key takeaways

- A SIP is not an investment. It is a method — a fixed amount, on a fixed date, every month, automatically. The fund is what you actually own.
- Its main benefit is removing the timing decision, which nobody can get right consistently.
- A fixed amount buys more units when prices fall and fewer when they rise, so your average cost can be below the average price.
- A SIP does not guarantee profit and does not protect you from a falling market. If the market is down when you need the money, you have less.
- Set the date two or three days after payday, so you invest first and spend what remains.
- ₹5,000 a month at an assumed 12% grows to about ₹4.1 lakh in 5 years and ₹1.76 crore in 30. Nearly all of that difference is time, not money.
- Starting at 25 instead of 35 can mean roughly ₹2.3 crore more, for ₹6 lakh more invested.
- The first few years look disappointing — after year one, growth is about ₹4,000. That is normal, and it is when most people quit.
- Check your emergency fund, health cover and term cover before starting. Markets are volatile, and the base is what stops an ordinary emergency forcing you to sell at a bad price.
- Never stop a SIP because markets fell. Pause it if you must, and increase it every time your salary rises.
