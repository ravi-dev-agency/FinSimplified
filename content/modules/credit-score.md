## 11.1 – The number you did not know you were building

Ramu applies for a car loan. Same job he has had for four years, same salary, no debts. He expects a formality.

The bank comes back with a rate four percentage points higher than the one advertised. Nobody explains why. On an ₹8,00,000 loan over seven years, that is what it costs him:

| | Rate offered | Monthly EMI | Total repaid |
|---|---|---|---|
| What he expected | 9.5% | ₹13,075 | ₹10,98,316 |
| What he got | 13.5% | ₹14,772 | ₹12,40,841 |
| **Difference** | | **₹1,697 a month** | **₹1,42,525** |

*Table 11.1 — illustrative. The same car, the same person, ₹1.4 lakh apart.*

The reason turned out to be a credit card he stopped using three years earlier. A ₹1,200 annual fee had been charged, he never noticed, and it sat unpaid for eleven months.

**Nobody told him. That is the point of this chapter.** Your credit score is being built right now, from things you may not be watching, and you usually discover it at the exact moment it costs you money.

### What the number is

A credit score is a three-digit number that tells a lender how reliably you repay money.

That is the whole idea. You borrow, you repay, and a record is kept. The number summarises that record.

In India the range runs from **300 to 900**. Higher is better. Most lenders treat anything above 750 as good, and below 650 as a problem.

### Where the number comes from

Four credit bureaus operate in India, licensed by the RBI: **CIBIL (TransUnion), Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark.** CIBIL is the most widely quoted, which is why people say "CIBIL score" when they mean credit score.

You have a score with each of them, and the numbers will not match exactly, because each bureau holds a slightly different set of records. A lender may check any one of them.

Every time you take a loan or use a credit card, the lender reports your behaviour to these bureaus each month — whether you paid, how much you owe, how long the account has been open. The bureaus keep that history and calculate a score from it.

> **Careful.** Nobody "sets" your score, and no bank decides it. It is calculated from your own repayment record. This matters, because it means the only way to change it is to change the record.

### What the number actually means to a lender

| Score | What a lender sees |
|---|---|
| 750–900 | Reliable. Best interest rates, fastest approval |
| 700–749 | Acceptable. Approved, but not the best rate |
| 650–699 | Risky. Approved with a higher rate, or a smaller amount |
| 600–649 | Difficult. Often refused, or asked for a guarantor |
| Below 600 | Very difficult. Mainstream lenders will usually decline |

*Table 11.2 — how lenders read the number*

**No credit history at all** is a separate case. If you have never borrowed, you may have no score, or a placeholder like −1 or 0. That is not a bad score — it is an absent one, and it is fixable in about six months.

## 11.2 – Why it matters more than people think

The obvious reason is that it decides whether a loan is approved. The bigger reason is what it costs you when it is approved anyway.

Two people take the same ₹30 lakh home loan for 20 years. One has a score of 780, the other 670. The bank offers the second person a rate one percentage point higher.

| | Score 780 | Score 670 |
|---|---|---|
| Rate offered | 8.5% | 9.5% |
| Monthly EMI | ₹26,035 | ₹27,964 |
| Total repaid over 20 years | ₹62,48,400 | ₹67,11,360 |
| **Extra paid** | — | **₹4,62,960** |

*Table 11.3 — illustrative. One percentage point on a ₹30 lakh, 20-year loan.*

![A 780 score pays about 26,000 a month; a 670 score pays about 28,000, which is over four and a half lakh more across twenty years](/diagrams/score-cost.svg)

*Fig 11.1 — the same house, the same loan, ₹4.6 lakh apart. The difference is a number in a database.*

Nothing about the house changed. Nothing about the person's income changed. A number in a database cost them ₹4.6 lakh.

### Where else it shows up

- **Credit card approval**, and the limit you are given
- **Loan against property, car loans, personal loans** — approval and rate
- **Some employers** check it for roles that handle money
- **Some landlords** ask for it in metros
- **Insurance premiums**, in some cases, though this is less common in India

## 11.3 – What actually moves the number

This is where most advice goes wrong. People are told to "maintain a good score" without being told which actions matter and which are noise.

Five things drive it, and they do not carry equal weight.

| What | Roughly how much it matters | What it measures |
|---|---|---|
| **Payment history** | Most important | Whether you paid on time, every time |
| **Credit utilisation** | Very important | How much of your limit you are using |
| **Age of your accounts** | Moderate | How long you have been borrowing |
| **Credit mix** | Small | Whether you have both loans and cards |
| **New enquiries** | Small | How often you have applied recently |

*Table 11.4 — the five factors, in order of weight*

![Payment history and utilisation dominate; credit mix and enquiries are small](/diagrams/score-factors.svg)

*Fig 11.2 — the first two bars do most of the work.*

The first two do most of the work. If you only get two things right, get those two.

### 1. Payment history — pay on time, every time

One missed payment is recorded. It stays on your report for years, and it damages a high score far more than a low one.

**What counts as a miss:** paying after the due date, paying less than the minimum due, or bouncing an EMI. Paying the minimum due on a credit card is *not* a miss for scoring purposes — but it is expensive for other reasons, which the [credit cards chapter](/learn/credit-cards) covers.

> **Do this.** Set auto-debit for every EMI and at least the minimum due on every card. Then set a calendar reminder two days before. Auto-debit fails sometimes — an expired card, a changed account, an insufficient balance on the wrong day — and the reminder is what catches it.

### 2. Credit utilisation — the one nobody explains

Utilisation is how much of your total credit limit you are using.

If your cards have a combined limit of ₹2,00,000 and your statements show ₹80,000 outstanding, your utilisation is 40%.

**Keep it under 30%.** Above that, the score falls even if you pay the full bill every month. The bureaus read high utilisation as dependence on borrowed money.

![A two lakh limit with sixty thousand used is 30 percent and healthy; the same limit with a lakh sixty used is 80 percent and damaging](/diagrams/utilisation.svg)

*Fig 11.3 — the same person, the same income. Only the timing of the spending changed.*

Here is the part that catches people out. **Utilisation is measured on your statement date, not on the day you pay.** You can pay your bill in full every single month and still show 80% utilisation, if you happen to spend heavily just before the statement is generated.

> **Do this if your utilisation is high.** Three fixes, in order of ease. Pay part of the bill *before* the statement date rather than after. Ask for a limit increase, which lowers the percentage without changing your spending. Or spread spending across two cards instead of loading one.

> **Do not** close an old card to "tidy up". Closing it removes that limit from the calculation, so your utilisation percentage jumps overnight — and you lose the account's age at the same time.

### 3. Age of your accounts

A ten-year-old card with a clean record is worth more than a new one. The bureaus look at the average age of your accounts and the age of your oldest.

This is why the advice above matters: closing your oldest card can quietly lower your score even though you did nothing wrong.

### 4. Credit mix

Having both a secured loan (home, car) and unsecured credit (cards, personal loan) is read as slightly more favourable than having only one type.

This is a small factor. **Never take a loan you do not need in order to improve your mix.** The interest will cost more than the points are worth.

### 5. Enquiries

Every time you formally apply for credit, the lender pulls your report. That is a **hard enquiry**, and several in a short window suggest you are desperate for money.

Checking your own score is a **soft enquiry** and does not affect it at all. Check it as often as you like.

> **Careful.** Applying to five banks at once to "see who gives the best rate" creates five hard enquiries. Use the bank's eligibility checker or a marketplace that does a soft pull first, and only formally apply where you intend to proceed.

## 11.4 – How to build a score from nothing

If you have never borrowed, you have no score. This is common at 22 or 23, and it is a genuine obstacle — you cannot get a loan without a score, and you cannot build a score without borrowing.

Three ways out, from easiest to hardest.

| Route | How it works | How long |
|---|---|---|
| **Secured credit card** | You place a fixed deposit, the bank gives a card against it. Almost no rejection, because the bank holds your money | 6 months to a usable score |
| **Become an add-on cardholder** | On a parent's or spouse's card. Reporting varies by issuer, so check whether it builds *your* file | 6–12 months |
| **A small consumer loan** | The EMI plans offered on phones and appliances are reported like any loan | 6–12 months |

*Table 11.5 — building a file from zero*

The secured card is the cleanest route. Use it for something small and regular — a phone bill, a subscription — and pay it in full every month. In six months you will have a score.

> **Do not** take a personal loan purely to build history. It is expensive, and a consumer loan or secured card does the same job for almost nothing.

## 11.5 – How to repair a damaged score

A low score is not permanent. But nobody can fix it quickly, and anybody who says otherwise is selling something.

**What actually works, in order:**

1. **Clear any overdue amount immediately.** An account currently overdue does more damage than one settled years ago. This is the single highest-impact action
2. **Bring utilisation below 30%** and keep it there. This shows up within one or two statement cycles — faster than anything else on this list
3. **Pay every due on time from now on.** Recent behaviour is weighted more heavily than old behaviour
4. **Do not apply for anything new** for six months. Let the hard enquiries age
5. **Leave your old accounts open**, even unused ones, for their age and their limit
6. **Check your report for errors** — see below

### How long each thing takes to fade

The most common question is "how long until it recovers?" The honest answer depends on what happened.

| What you did | How long it affects you |
|---|---|
| High utilisation this month | Clears in 1–2 statement cycles. The fastest fix there is |
| A hard enquiry | Weight fades over about a year |
| One late payment, quickly cleared | Limited lasting damage if the rest is clean |
| A pattern of late payments | Years, and it keeps hurting while it continues |
| An account currently overdue | Hurts most of all, and every month it stays open |
| A "settled" account | Years. See below — this is the one to avoid |

*Table 11.6 — roughly how long different marks last*

**Recent behaviour counts more than old behaviour.** That is the encouraging part. A clean twelve months does real work even if the years before it were not clean, because the scoring weights recency.

### What recovery actually looks like

Someone starting at 620 after clearing an overdue amount, doing everything right and applying for nothing new:

| | Roughly |
|---|---|
| Month 1–2 | Utilisation drops below 30%. First visible movement |
| Month 3–6 | Steady climb as on-time payments accumulate |
| Month 6–12 | Enquiries age off. Score approaching usable territory |
| Month 12–24 | A genuinely good score, if nothing new goes wrong |

*Table 11.7 — an illustration of the shape, not a promise. Individual results vary with what is on the report.*

![A score climbing from 620 towards 750 over twenty-four months](/diagrams/score-recovery.svg)

*Fig 11.4 — steady, not sudden. The first movement comes from utilisation.*

There is no faster path. Anybody offering one is selling something.

> **Do not** pay anyone who offers to "fix your CIBIL score" or "remove defaults". Nobody outside the bureau can alter your record, and a genuine default cannot be deleted. What they usually do is file the same dispute you can file yourself, free.

### The word that costs people years: "settled"

If you cannot repay a loan, a lender may offer to close it for less than you owe. That sounds like relief. It is recorded as **"settled"**, not "closed", and it is one of the worst marks on a credit report.

A settled account tells every future lender that you did not repay in full. It stays on the report for years, and many lenders refuse applicants with one.

> **Careful.** If you are in difficulty, ask the lender about restructuring the loan or extending the tenure instead. Both are far less damaging than a settlement. And if you have already settled something, paying the remaining amount later gets it updated to "closed" — ask the lender for a no-dues certificate and check that the bureau record changes.

## 11.6 – Check your own report, twice a year

Errors are more common than people expect — a loan that is not yours, a paid account still showing an outstanding balance, a duplicate record, a wrong address or PAN.

Each bureau is required to give you **one free full credit report a year**. Four bureaus means four free reports, so you can check one every three months at no cost.

**How to actually do it.** Go to the bureau's own website — CIBIL, Experian, Equifax or CRIF High Mark — and use their free annual report option. You will need your PAN, some personal details, and you will answer a few questions about your existing loans or cards to prove identity.

| | |
|---|---|
| **What you need** | PAN, date of birth, registered mobile number |
| **What it costs** | Free, once a year per bureau |
| **Does it hurt your score?** | No. Checking your own is a soft enquiry |
| **How often to check** | Every 3 months, rotating between the four bureaus |

*Table 11.8 — checking your own report*

> **Careful.** Many apps and websites offer a "free credit score" instantly. Most are genuine and useful for a quick number, but they show a score rather than the **full report** — and the report is where errors hide. Once a year, get the actual report from the bureau itself.

**What to look for:**

- Accounts you do not recognise, which can mean identity fraud
- A loan you closed still showing as active or overdue
- The wrong amount outstanding
- "Settled" on an account you actually repaid in full
- Personal details that are wrong — name, PAN, address

**If something is wrong,** raise a dispute on the bureau's website. It is free. They are required to investigate, usually within about 30 days, and will contact the lender who reported it. Keep a copy of what you filed.

## 11.7 – Common mistakes people make

| | |
|---|---|
| **Closing old credit cards** | You lose the account's age and its limit, so utilisation jumps and history shortens |
| **Paying only the minimum due** | Protects the score but the balance compounds at 36–45% a year |
| **Maxing out a card just before the statement date** | Utilisation is measured then, not when you pay |
| **Applying to several lenders at once** | Multiple hard enquiries in a short window |
| **Settling a loan instead of restructuring** | "Settled" is close to the worst mark a report can carry |
| **Ignoring a small overdue amount** | ₹800 unpaid damages a score as surely as ₹80,000 |
| **Never checking the report** | Errors and fraud go unnoticed for years |
| **Being a guarantor casually** | Their default becomes your record |
| **Taking a loan to improve credit mix** | The interest costs more than the points are worth |

## 11.8 – Questions people actually ask

#### Does checking my own score reduce it?

No. Checking your own is a soft enquiry and has no effect, however often you do it. Only a formal application by a lender creates a hard enquiry.

#### Why are my scores different at different bureaus?

Because lenders do not all report to all four bureaus, and they report on different dates. A card that appears in one bureau's file may be missing from another's.

Check the one your lender uses if you know it. Otherwise treat them as broadly consistent — if three say 760 and one says 735, you have a good score.

#### I have no loans and no cards. Why is my score bad?

It probably is not bad — it is absent. No borrowing history means nothing to calculate from, which shows as no score or a placeholder.

A secured credit card against a fixed deposit is the simplest fix. Six months of regular use and full payment gives you a real score.

#### How long does a missed payment stay on my report?

Several years. It does not vanish when you pay, though the record updates to show it was cleared, and its weight fades as it ages.

The practical answer: one missed payment on an otherwise clean record recovers within six to twelve months of consistent payment. A pattern of misses takes much longer.

#### I paid off a loan. Should I close the account?

For a loan, it closes automatically when the last EMI is paid. Get the **no-dues certificate** and check your report a month later to confirm it shows as closed.

For a credit card, keep it open. An old card with no balance helps your score in two ways — its age, and its limit lowering your utilisation.

#### My score dropped and I did not do anything wrong.

Usually one of four things. Your utilisation rose because of a large purchase. A card issuer reduced your limit. An old account was closed, by you or by them. Or someone reported something incorrectly.

Pull your full report and compare it to last time. The cause is almost always visible in the account list.

#### Does my salary affect my score?

No. Income is not part of the calculation at all, and bureaus do not hold it.

Lenders look at your income *separately* when deciding how much to lend. A high score with a low income can still mean a small loan.

#### Does using UPI or a debit card build credit?

No. Neither involves borrowing, so nothing is reported to the bureaus. Only credit — cards, loans, EMIs — builds a credit file.

#### My spouse has a bad score. Does it affect mine?

Not unless you are jointly liable. Credit files are individual.

But a joint loan, or standing as a guarantor, puts their behaviour on your record. Think carefully before signing as a guarantor for anyone, including family.

#### Is a credit card necessary to have a good score?

Not strictly — a loan repaid on time builds history too. But a card is the cheapest way to do it, because if you pay in full every month you borrow for free.

The chapter on [credit cards](/learn/credit-cards) covers how to use one without it costing you anything.

#### How fast can I get from 650 to 750?

With no overdue amounts, utilisation brought under 30%, and every payment on time, expect meaningful movement in six months and a substantial change in twelve.

If there is an active default or a settled account on the file, it takes longer. There is no shortcut, and anyone promising one is not being honest with you.

## Key takeaways

- A credit score is a 300–900 number summarising how reliably you have repaid. Nobody sets it; it is calculated from your own record.
- India has four bureaus — CIBIL, Experian, Equifax and CRIF High Mark — and your score differs slightly across them.
- Recovery takes months, not days. Utilisation moves within two statement cycles; everything else takes six months to two years.
- One percentage point of interest on a ₹30 lakh home loan is worth about ₹4.6 lakh over 20 years. That is what the number is really worth.
- Two things do most of the work: paying on time, every time, and keeping credit utilisation under 30%.
- Utilisation is measured on your statement date, not when you pay. Paying in full each month is not enough on its own.
- Never close your oldest credit card. You lose its age and its limit at the same time.
- "Settled" is close to the worst mark a report can carry. Ask about restructuring instead.
- Checking your own score is free and harmless. Check one of the four bureaus every three months.
- No score is not a bad score. A secured credit card builds one in about six months.
