![A single coin on the left growing into a tall stack on the right, over time](/diagrams/cover-compounding.svg)

Compounding is the most quoted idea in finance and the least believed.

People repeat the lines. They nod along. Then they do the opposite of what the lines imply — they start late, stop early, and chase the year rather than the decade.

This week I collected what the people who understand it best have actually said about it, and checked whether the arithmetic backs them up.

## First, what it actually is

Compounding means **your returns start earning returns.**

That is the whole mechanism. Everything else is a consequence of it.

Put ₹1,00,000 somewhere earning 10% a year:

| Year | You earn | Because the base is now |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | ₹10,000 | ₹1,00,000 |
| 2 | ₹11,000 | ₹1,10,000 |
| 3 | ₹12,100 | ₹1,21,000 |
| 10 | ₹23,579 | ₹2,35,795 |

*Table 1 — the same 10%, on a base that keeps growing*

Notice what changes. **The rate never moves. The base does.** Each year's growth becomes next year's starting point, so the amount you earn rises even though nothing about your effort or the return changes.

Contrast that with simple interest, where you only ever earn on the original amount:

| After | Simple interest | Compounding |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | ₹1,10,000 | ₹1,10,000 |
| 10 years | ₹2,00,000 | ₹2,59,374 |
| 30 years | ₹4,00,000 | ₹17,44,940 |

*Table 2 — ₹1,00,000 at 10%. Illustration only.*

After one year, **no difference at all**. After thirty, compounding produces more than four times as much.

That first row is why the idea is so easy to dismiss. In the period when you are deciding whether it is worth bothering with, it genuinely is not doing anything visible.

## "The eighth wonder of the world"

The most famous line of all:

> *"Compound interest is the eighth wonder of the world. He who understands it, earns it. He who doesn't, pays it."*

Attributed to **Albert Einstein**, endlessly.

**He almost certainly never said it.** Nobody has found it in anything he wrote or was recorded saying, and the quote only starts appearing decades after his death. It has also been attributed, with equally little evidence, to Rothschild and to Franklin.

So why keep it? Because the *second* half is the part people skip, and it is the most useful sentence in this article.

**He who doesn't, pays it.** Compounding is not a thing you can opt out of. If you are not earning it on savings, you are paying it on a credit card at 42% a year, or losing it to inflation at 6%. It runs in both directions with the same patience.

![A person choosing between three million rupees today and a single paisa that doubles every day for a month](/diagrams/penny-choice.svg)

*The classic illustration of the idea — and almost everybody gets it wrong.*

Someone offers you a choice. **₹30,00,000 in cash right now**, or **one paisa that doubles every day for thirty-one days**.

| Day | The paisa is worth |
|---|---|
| 5 | ₹0.16 |
| 10 | ₹5.12 |
| 20 | ₹5,242 |
| 29 | ₹26,84,354 |
| 30 | ₹53,68,709 |
| 31 | **₹1,07,37,418** |

*Table 3 — one paisa, doubled daily*

The paisa wins by more than three times. But look at **day 29** — twenty-nine days in, and it is still behind the cash. Everything decisive happens in the last forty-eight hours.

That is the whole problem with compounding in one line: **for most of the time it is running, it looks like it is losing.**

## Warren Buffett, and the number that explains him

Buffett is the most cited example of compounding on earth, usually for the wrong reason. People point at his returns. The more interesting fact is his *runway*.

He bought his first stock at **eleven years old**, and the overwhelming majority of his net worth was built after he turned fifty. Not because he got better after fifty — because compounding does almost all of its work at the end.

Here is why, with pure arithmetic. Take any steady rate — 20% for this illustration — and run it for sixty years:

| | Multiple of what you started with |
|---|---|
| After 30 years | 237× |
| After 60 years | 56,348× |

*Table 4 — illustration of the shape, not a claim about anyone's actual returns*

At the halfway mark you have 237×, which sounds enormous. It is **0.4%** of where you finish.

**More than 99% of the final value appears in the second half.**

![A curve barely off the floor at year thirty, rising almost vertically by year sixty](/diagrams/buffett-curve.svg)

*Fig 1 — the same rate throughout. Only the number of years changes.*

His own explanation is characteristically plain:

> *"Someone's sitting in the shade today because someone planted a tree a long time ago."*

And on the mechanism itself, Charlie Munger was blunter about what it demands:

> *"The first rule of compounding: never interrupt it unnecessarily."*

That sentence is worth more than most investing books. Not *earn more* — **do not interrupt**. Selling in a panic, stopping a SIP, dipping into the corpus: each one resets the clock on part of your money.

## What it looks like on an ordinary Indian salary

You do not need Buffett's runway for this to matter. Here is ₹5,000 a month at an assumed 12%.

![A line almost flat for a decade, then curving steeply upward](/diagrams/compounding-curve.svg)

*Fig 2 — the flat part is where almost everyone quits.*

| By the end of | It is worth | That decade alone added |
|---|---|---|
| Year 10 | ₹11,61,695 | ₹11,61,695 |
| Year 20 | ₹49,95,740 | ₹38,34,044 |
| Year 30 | ₹1,76,49,569 | ₹1,26,53,829 |
| Year 40 | ₹5,94,12,101 | ₹4,17,62,532 |

*Table 5 — illustration at an assumed 12%, from the same formula as the [SIP calculator](/calculators/sip). Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

The fourth decade adds **more than the first three combined, several times over** — for the identical ₹5,000 a month.

This is the Buffett curve on a normal salary. Same shape, shorter runway.

## The ten years you cannot buy back

Two people, same fund, same assumed 12%.

**Ramu** starts at 25, invests ₹5,000 a month for ten years, then **stops completely at 35** and never adds another rupee.

**Kiran** starts at 35 and invests ₹5,000 a month every month until 60.

![Ramu pays in six lakh and stops at 35; Kiran pays in fifteen lakh over 25 years and still finishes far behind](/diagrams/start-early.svg)

*Fig 3 — the ten years Kiran cannot buy back.*

| | Ramu | Kiran |
|---|---|---|
| Years invested | 10 | 25 |
| **Total put in** | **₹6,00,000** | **₹15,00,000** |
| Value at 60 | **₹2,29,88,170** | **₹94,88,175** |

*Table 6 — illustration at an assumed 12%. Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

Kiran invested two and a half times more money over two and a half times more years, and finishes roughly **₹1.35 crore behind**.

Ramu did not choose better funds or work harder. He gave his money twenty-five extra years of being left alone — the one input that cannot be bought later at any price.

## Why 1% is not a small number

The most counter-intuitive part of compounding is how much a tiny edge is worth, because our instinct treats 1% as a rounding error.

Take a choice that makes you **1% better every day** for a year, against one that makes you 1% worse.

![Two lines starting together; one rises to 38 times, the other falls almost to zero](/diagrams/one-percent.svg)

*Fig 4 — identical daily choices, opposite directions.*

| After 365 days | You end at |
|---|---|
| 1% better each day | **37.8×** where you started |
| 1% worse each day | **0.03×** where you started |

*Table 7 — the arithmetic of 1.01³⁶⁵ and 0.99³⁶⁵*

Roughly **1,480 times apart** — from a daily difference so small you could not feel it on any given day.

Look at the left of that chart again. For the first two months the two lines are practically touching. Anyone measuring progress in February would conclude neither choice mattered.

### The same thing, with actual money

This is not just a metaphor. One percentage point of **return** does this over thirty years, on ₹5,000 a month:

| At this return | After 30 years |
|---|---|
| 11% | ₹1,41,51,139 |
| 12% | ₹1,76,49,569 |
| **Difference** | **₹34,98,430** |

*Table 8 — illustration only. Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

**One percentage point is worth ₹35 lakh.** Same monthly amount, same thirty years.

This is exactly why a fund's expense ratio matters, and why the gap between a direct plan and a regular plan is not a technicality. A percent here or there is not noise — over decades it is the largest number on the page.

## It runs backwards too

The Einstein line's second half again: *he who doesn't, pays it.*

The **Rule of 72** shows how fast, in either direction. Divide 72 by the annual rate to get the doubling time.

| At this rate | Doubles every |
|---|---|
| 6% (inflation) | 12 years |
| 12% (a long-run equity assumption) | 6 years |
| **42% (credit card)** | **under 2 years** |

*Table 9 — a close approximation*

A card balance you ignore doubles in under two years. Inflation halves your idle cash in twelve. **The same force, pointed at you.**

This is why clearing a credit card outranks investing — covered in [chapter 12](/learn/credit-cards).

## What all of them are actually saying

Strip away the names and the quotes agree on three things.

**1. Time matters more than rate.** Buffett's edge is partly his returns and hugely his eighty years. You cannot control the market's rate. You can control when you start.

**2. Not interrupting beats optimising.** Munger's rule. Most damage is self-inflicted — selling in a fall, pausing a SIP, raiding the corpus for something that was not an emergency. This is what the [emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) is really protecting.

**3. It will look like nothing is happening.** For years. The paisa is behind on day 29; the curve is flat for a decade. That is not a malfunction — it is the shape of the thing, and knowing it in advance is what lets you survive it.

## The line worth keeping

Not Einstein's, since he probably never said it. Buffett's:

> *"Someone's sitting in the shade today because someone planted a tree a long time ago."*

You cannot grow the tree faster by watching it. You plant it, you do not dig it up to check the roots, and you wait — and the waiting is the entire skill.
