*Rich Dad Poor Dad* has sold over 40 million copies. It is the book almost every Indian beginner reads first — and the one most of them cannot act on afterwards.

That is not the reader's fault. The book is 30 years old, written by an American, about American tax law and American property. Some of its advice is genuinely dangerous to follow literally in India.

But a few of its ideas are among the most useful things a beginner can learn, and they survive the translation intact.

The book is built as **six lessons**. Below is each one as Kiyosaki named it, what it actually means, what to do about it on an Indian salary — and, at the end, the parts to leave on the page.

## The one idea the whole book rests on

Robert Kiyosaki grew up around two men. His own father — educated, respected, a government employee, permanently short of money. And his friend's father, who had little formal education and became wealthy.

He calls them poor dad and rich dad. Their difference, as he tells it, was not income. It was what they did with it.

That is the book's real argument, and it is correct. Everything useful in it follows from that one observation.

## Lesson 1 — The rich don't work for money

The provocative title hides an ordinary point: a salary is rented income. It stops the day you stop. The rich, his argument goes, spend their working life building things that pay them whether they turn up or not.

He is not telling you to quit your job. He is telling you that the job is the *input*, never the destination.

> **A high salary does not make you rich. What you do with the salary does.**

Which leads directly to the question the whole book turns on.

## Lesson 2 — Financial literacy: know what an asset actually is

This is the lesson. If you take nothing else from the book, take this.

Kiyosaki's definition is deliberately blunt:

- An **asset** puts money *into* your pocket.
- A **liability** takes money *out* of your pocket.

That is it. Not what it is called, not what it is worth, not what it says on a balance sheet. Which direction does the money move.

![Money flowing out through EMIs and bills, versus money flowing in from investments and rent](/diagrams/asset-or-liability.svg)

*Fig 1 — the same question, asked of everything you own.*

Most people's "assets" fail this test. Apply it honestly:

| What people call it | Which way the money actually moves | So it is |
|---|---|---|
| The car in the parking spot | EMI, petrol, insurance, service — out | A liability |
| The phone bought on EMI | Out, every month, while it loses value | A liability |
| A flat you live in, on a home loan | EMI, maintenance, property tax — out | A liability while the loan runs |
| A flat you rent out, rent above EMI | In, every month | An asset |
| Index funds, SIP, shares | In, as they grow | An asset |
| EPF and NPS | In, quietly, every month | An asset |

*Table 1 — Kiyosaki's test, applied to ordinary Indian purchases*

> The house you live in is the argument everyone has with this book. Kiyosaki calls it a liability, which sounds absurd to Indian ears. He is not saying don't buy a home. He is saying don't count it as the asset that will fund your life — because it does not pay you anything while you live in it. Buy it because you want to live in it. That is a good enough reason.

**What to implement:** list everything you own that you think of as an asset. Write next to each one whether it put money in or took money out last month. The list that survives is your real asset column — and for most people it is much shorter than they expected.

## Lesson 3 — Mind your own business

A line people misread as "start a company". It means something narrower:

**Your profession is how you earn. Your business is the asset column you build with what you earn.** Most people spend a career minding someone else's business and never start their own.

For a salaried person in India, this is the whole lesson: the job is fine. The job is not the plan. The plan is what the job funds — and the ordering matters more than the amount.

**Income → buy assets → let the assets pay for the lifestyle.**

Most people run it the other way: income → buy lifestyle → invest whatever survives. Nothing survives. That is not a discipline problem, it is an ordering problem.

Here is the ordering, in rupees. ₹15,000 a month for seven years — roughly a mid-range car EMI:

| ₹15,000 a month for 7 years | Result |
|---|---|
| As a car EMI | ₹12,60,000 paid out, car worth a fraction of it |
| Invested instead | ₹19,79,685 |

*Table 2 — illustration at an assumed 12%, from the same formula as the [SIP calculator](/calculators/sip). Actual returns vary and may be lower; past performance does not guarantee future results.*

![The same monthly amount: one path ends with a depreciated car, the other with a larger sum](/diagrams/same-emi.svg)

*Fig 2 — the same ₹15,000, pointed in two directions.*

The point is not that cars are forbidden. It is that the car is a choice to convert ₹19.8 lakh into transport — and almost nobody makes that choice knowingly, because the EMI is presented as ₹15,000, never as the other number.

**What to implement:** before any EMI, work out what that instalment becomes if invested for the same number of years. Then decide. You may still buy the car. You will at least know the price.

### How to actually do it: pay yourself first

This idea is older than the book — it comes from *The Richest Man in Babylon* — but Kiyosaki put it in front of millions, and it is how Lesson 3 gets done.

The rule: the moment income arrives, a fixed amount goes to your asset column, **before** rent, bills and spending. Not what is left at month end. There is never anything left at month end.

In practice, in India, this is one afternoon of setup:

- A SIP auto-debit dated **2–3 days after payday**, not the 28th
- EPF already does this for you — that is why it works
- NPS, if you want the extra section 80CCD(1B) deduction
- A separate account for the money, not the one your salary lands in

What a fixed monthly amount becomes over 20 years:

| Every month | You put in | It becomes |
|---|---|---|
| ₹2,000 | ₹4,80,000 | ₹19,98,296 |
| ₹5,000 | ₹12,00,000 | ₹49,95,740 |
| ₹10,000 | ₹24,00,000 | ₹99,91,479 |

*Table 3 — illustration at an assumed 12% over 20 years, from the [SIP calculator](/calculators/sip). Actual returns vary and may be lower.*

₹5,000 a month is one food delivery order a week. Over 20 years the gap between doing it and not doing it is roughly **₹50 lakh**.

**What to implement:** set the auto-debit for 2 days after your salary date. Start at an amount that feels slightly too small — the habit matters more than the number, and a SIP you never cancel beats a larger one you stop in March.

## Lesson 4 — Taxes and the power of corporations

This is the lesson that does **not** transfer, and it needs saying clearly rather than skipping.

Kiyosaki's argument is that the rich own things through companies, so expenses come out *before* tax, while employees earn, are taxed, and spend what is left.

The observation is fair. The tactic is not available to you, and attempting it is the most expensive mistake in the book — the section below on what not to take explains why.

What *does* transfer is the underlying habit: **know the tax treatment of a thing before you buy it, not after.** In India that means knowing the four things that actually move your tax — which regime you are on, what 80C and 80D cover if you are on the old one, how capital gains are taxed on what you hold, and what EPF and NPS do.

**What to implement:** read your own payslip and Form 16 once, properly, until you can say where every deduction went. Most people have never done this, and it is the cheapest financial education available.

## Lesson 5 — The rich invent money

The most misread lesson in the book, and the one that has cost people the most.

Kiyosaki does not mean printing money. He means that opportunities are *created* by people who are prepared to recognise them — and that confidence plus financial knowledge beats waiting for a safe, obvious deal that everyone else can also see.

The useful half is real: the people who spot an opportunity are the ones who already understood the subject before it appeared. That is Lesson 2 doing its work.

The dangerous half is also real. In the book this becomes an argument for aggressive leverage and quick property deals — written by a man whose company later went bankrupt, in a country whose property maths does not match India's. Taking "the rich invent money" as permission to borrow heavily and move fast is how beginners lose money while believing they are being bold.

> **Careful.** Every scheme that has ever taken money from an Indian beginner — options tipping groups, guaranteed-return apps, "invest in my project" — sells itself as this lesson. Opportunity recognised by knowledge is one thing. Risk taken on confidence alone is the other.

**What to implement:** get fluent in one thing before putting money in it. The lesson is the preparation, not the daring.

## Lesson 6 — Work to learn, not to earn

His argument: take the job that teaches you a skill you lack, over the one that pays slightly more right now — especially when young.

The skills he names are sales, communication, negotiation and leadership — because they compound and move with you between jobs.

This translates cleanly to India, with one caveat: it is advice for people with a cushion. If you are supporting a family on one income, the higher salary *is* the correct answer, and no book should make you feel otherwise.

**What to implement:** when comparing two offers, ask which one leaves you more employable in three years. If the answer is clear and you can afford the difference, take the one that teaches you something.

## What not to take from this book

Now the part most summaries leave out — and the reason this article exists.

### The company-owns-everything trick does not work for Indian salaried people

The book's most-repeated tactic: own things through a corporation, so expenses come out *before* tax instead of after.

For a salaried employee in India, this is simply not available. Your tax is deducted at source on gross salary. You get a **standard deduction of ₹75,000** under the new regime and the specific deductions the Act allows — you cannot route your car, phone or travel through a company you invented to make them pre-tax.

Attempting it without a genuine business is not clever structuring. It is tax evasion, and it is prosecutable.

> **Careful.** If a video tells you to open a company so your lifestyle becomes a business expense, and you have no business, you are being advised into a crime by someone who will not be there when it is assessed.

### "Your house is a liability" is not "don't buy a house"

Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the single most misread line in the book. A home you live in is a reasonable purchase for reasons that have nothing to do with investing.

### The real estate advice is American

Leverage, flipping, cash-flowing rentals from day one — this assumes US mortgage rates, US rental yields and US tax treatment. Indian residential rental yields are typically in the low single digits, often below the home loan rate. The arithmetic that makes his examples work does not hold here.

### Kiyosaki is not a model to copy

This matters for calibration, so here it is plainly:

- **Rich Global LLC, one of his companies, filed for bankruptcy in 2012**, after a US court ordered it to pay around **$24 million** to The Learning Annex, the promoter that built his speaking career, over an unpaid share of profits.
- **"Rich dad" was unidentified for nearly 20 years.** Kiyosaki gave varying accounts; in 2016 he named him as Hawaiian hotelier Richard Kimi, with a confidentiality agreement offered as the reason for the silence. Whether he was one man or a composite is still disputed.
- **His crash predictions have a poor record.** He has forecast the "biggest crash in history" repeatedly across two decades, including for February 2025. Acting on those warnings would have cost you money.

None of that makes the asset/liability idea wrong. It does mean: take the framework, ignore the man's current opinions, and never buy a course off the back of the book.

## All six lessons, as things to actually do

| Lesson | What to do this month |
|---|---|
| 1 — The rich don't work for money | Treat the salary as the input, not the destination |
| 2 — Financial literacy | Audit your asset column: did each item put money in or take it out last month? |
| 3 — Mind your own business | Set one auto-debit, 2 days after payday — and price every EMI as its investment alternative first |
| 4 — Taxes and corporations | Read your payslip and Form 16 until you can explain every deduction |
| 5 — The rich invent money | Get fluent in one thing before putting money into it |
| 6 — Work to learn | Pick the job that leaves you more employable in three years — when you can afford to |

*Table 4 — the book's six lessons, as actions available to a salaried person in India*

Not one of these requires a company, a property, or anything the book sells.

## The honest summary

*Rich Dad Poor Dad* is a mindset book wearing the costume of a how-to book.

Read it for the asset column, the ordering of income, and the habit of paying yourself first — those three are genuinely worth the 30 years it has been in print, and most Indian readers would be materially better off for applying only the first one.

Ignore the tax structuring, the property mechanics, and every prediction the author has made since. Those parts were written for a different country, and in places by someone whose own record does not support them.

**The book's best idea is also its simplest: money that arrives is not yours until you have decided what it is for. Decide before it arrives.**
