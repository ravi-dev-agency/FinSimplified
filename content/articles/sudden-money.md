In 2011, a computer operator from Motihari in Bihar became the first person to win ₹5 crore on *Kaun Banega Crorepati*.

His name is Sushil Kumar. If you search for him today, you will find hundreds of articles saying the same thing: he lost everything, became an alcoholic, and ended up selling milk from two cows to survive.

I went looking for the details, because the story is useful and I wanted the numbers.

**Most of it does not hold up.**

## The story everyone repeats

The milk-selling detail appears in dozens of publications. It is the hook in almost every headline.

It came from Kumar himself — but not as a confession.

In a Facebook post in 2020, he described a journalist calling him while he was out walking. The conversation irritated him, so he said his money was finished and he was surviving by selling milk from two cows.

He made it up to end the call.

It has been reprinted as fact ever since.

> **Why this matters for a money article.** The most-shared financial cautionary tale in India is built on a line a man invented to get off the phone. If we are going to learn something from his story, it should at least be the part that is true.

## What the record actually shows

Strip out the recycled headlines and what remains is more ordinary, and more useful.

**What he has said himself.** In that same 2020 post, Kumar wrote that after the win he became, in his words, a philanthropist "addicted to secret donations" — giving constantly, attending events almost daily. He said people cheated him, and that he only realised it after the money had gone. His wife told him he could not tell the difference between people who deserved help and people who were using him, and that he was not thinking about their future.

He has also spoken about drinking and smoking heavily in that period.

**What happened next.** He cleared the Bihar Public Service Commission teacher recruitment exam — ranked 119th in Psychology for classes 11–12 — and enrolled as a PhD scholar in Psychology. He runs a sparrow conservation campaign in Champaran that has distributed around 5,500 wooden nests since 2019.

That is not a man ruined. That is a man who handled a windfall badly, and then rebuilt.

**What nobody documents.** Here is the part I found most surprising. Not one source I could find specifies a failed business, a bad property deal, or a wrong investment.

The only spending anyone can point to is giving it away.

## So what actually went wrong

If you were expecting a story about foolish investments, there isn't one. What the record describes is simpler, and far more common:

| What happened | Why it drains money so fast |
|---|---|
| Money arrived as one lump, all at once | No time to learn, no time to think, and a decision required immediately |
| Everyone found out | A public win means a queue at the door that does not form for a salaried person |
| Giving became constant | Each individual amount felt small and justified; nobody was adding them up |
| No structure between the money and the requests | Nothing to say *no* on his behalf |

*Table 1 — the mechanism, not the mistake*

None of this requires stupidity. It requires a person with no system suddenly holding more money than they have ever handled, while being asked for it daily by people who know they have it.

**That is the real lesson, and it applies far below ₹5 crore.** A bonus, an inheritance, a settlement, the sale of family land, provident fund on retirement — all of it arrives the same way. One lump, no instructions, everyone aware.

## What ₹5 crore actually is

Start with what nobody mentions in the headlines: the tax.

Game show winnings are taxed under Section 194B at a **flat 30%, plus 4% cess — 31.2%**. There is no slab, no basic exemption limit, and no deductions of any kind. It is deducted before the money reaches you.

| | |
|---|---|
| Prize announced | ₹5,00,00,000 |
| Tax at 31.2% | ₹1,56,00,000 |
| **What actually arrives** | **₹3,44,00,000** |

*Table 2 — the number on the cheque is not the number you get*

*[VERIFY: current Section 194B rate and cess before publishing. This was 30% plus 4% cess at the time of writing, but rates change.]*

So the real question is what ₹3.44 crore can do.

Left in something conservative earning 7% a year, touching none of the capital:

| | |
|---|---|
| Withdraw 4% a year | ₹13,76,000 |
| Which is per month | **₹1,14,667** |
| What happens to the ₹3.44 crore | It keeps growing |

*Table 3 — illustration at an assumed 7%. Actual returns vary and past performance does not guarantee future results.*

**₹1,14,667 a month. Every month. Without ever touching the ₹3.44 crore.**

That is more than most people in India will ever earn in a month, arriving forever, from money that is still there at the end of it.

The amount was never the problem.

## What the order should have been

Everything on this site is built around one idea: the sequence matters more than the size. A windfall does not change the sequence — it just compresses it into a single afternoon.

Here is what ₹3.44 crore would look like run properly.

**1. Move it somewhere boring, and do nothing for six months.**

Not invested. Not allocated. A separate account, a liquid fund, a fixed deposit — somewhere it cannot be spent by accident and cannot fall in value.

Six months of doing nothing costs you a little interest. It buys you time to think while the noise dies down, and it is the single highest-value thing you can do in the first week.

**2. Decide what you will say, once, to everybody.**

This is the step that would have mattered most here. Not a number — a sentence.

*"I've committed what I can give, and it's already decided."*

You need it ready before the requests start, because they arrive faster than you can think, each one is individually reasonable, and saying yes is easier than explaining. A rule you set in advance is doing the refusing, not you.

If you want to give — and there is nothing wrong with wanting to — **fix the amount first, then give it away.** A decided number is generosity. An open tap is not.

**3. Build the base before anything else.**

The same order as [Chapter 5](/learn/how-to-start-investing), just with more zeroes:

- Emergency fund — three to six months of expenses, reachable the same day
- Health insurance for the family
- Term insurance if anyone depends on your income
- Clear every expensive debt, credit cards first

On this amount that is a rounding error. It is also the thing that stops a bad year forcing you to sell.

**4. Then, and only then, invest — and never in one go.**

Split by when you need the money, exactly as [Chapter 5](/learn/how-to-start-investing) sets out. Money needed inside three years does not go near equity, however good things look.

**5. Do not change your life for a year.**

Same house, same job, same expenses. You can upgrade later from a position of knowing what you are doing. Upgrading first is how a windfall becomes a higher cost of living funded by a shrinking pot.

> **Do this if it ever happens to you.** Tell almost nobody. The queue at the door is proportional to how many people know, and it never gets shorter once it has formed.

## What I actually take from this

Three things.

**Sudden money is harder than earned money.** Income arrives slowly enough that your judgement grows alongside it. A windfall skips that entirely — you get the money without the years of small decisions that teach you to handle it.

**The danger was not a bad investment. It was having no rule.** He was not reckless in the way the headlines suggest. He was generous, constantly, with nothing in place to bound it — and the people around him noticed.

**And the story people tell about money is usually wrong.** The most-shared version of this one is that a poor man got rich and destroyed himself. The verifiable version is that a man handled a windfall badly, stopped, and went back to studying — and is now a teacher and a PhD scholar.

The second version is less dramatic. It is also the one worth learning from, because it is the one that is true.
