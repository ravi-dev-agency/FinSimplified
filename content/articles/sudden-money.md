In 2011, a computer operator in Motihari, Bihar earned ₹6,000 a month. His family could not afford a television, so he watched *Kaun Banega Crorepati* at a neighbour's house.

Then he went on the show and won ₹5 crore.

At ₹6,000 a month, that is **8,333 months of salary. Six hundred and ninety-four years of earnings**, arriving in a single evening.

Within five years it was mostly gone. He was drinking daily, his marriage was strained, and he later described himself in that period as *"a fugitive running away from the truth."*

His name is Sushil Kumar. This is what happened, in order — and what he says about it now, which is the most valuable part.

## 1. He had a good plan

Start here, because it is the detail that makes the rest worth studying.

In an interview the week he won, he was asked what he would do with the money:

> *"I'm going to repair my house, fulfil a few basic needs and then move to Delhi to study for the civil service exams."*

> *"Our house has almost broken down and we have a lot of loans that have to be repaid. If it hadn't been for this money, I would have got old before I sorted out my life."*

**Repair the house. Clear the loans. Fund an education.**

Ask any financial planner to design a first plan for a man in his position and they would write down roughly that. He was not fantasising about cars. He had a modest, correct idea of what the money was for.

He lost it anyway.

That is why this story matters. **The thing that destroys a windfall is almost never the plan. It is everything that arrives alongside the money.**

## 2. Three things arrived at once

### The people

He was on national television. Everyone knew his name, the number, and where he lived. Overnight he stopped being a computer operator with ₹5 crore and became *the man who had ₹5 crore* — which is a different job.

He was attending programmes somewhere in Bihar ten, sometimes fifteen days a month. The plan to study quietly disappeared underneath the schedule.

### The giving

He became, in his own words, a philanthropist "addicted to secret donations."

> *"People cheated on me, which I got to know only later after the donations were made."*

There is no villain in that sentence and no single catastrophic decision. There is a man being asked for money daily by people he had no way of evaluating, saying yes each time, and finding out much later which yeses were lies.

**Every individual request was small enough to grant. Nobody was adding them up.**

### The businesses

He also tried to put the money to work. He started a car rental business in Delhi, and other ventures besides.

> *"Those businesses eventually used to fail after a few days."*

Not *failed after two hard years*. **After a few days.**

A man whose working life had been operating a computer for a government scheme was now starting companies in a city he did not know, in industries he had never worked in, with no experience of hiring, pricing or cash flow — and with enough capital to start another one each time a venture collapsed.

**Having the money to try is not the same as having the knowledge to succeed.** The capital hid the gap until the gap had eaten the capital.

| Where ₹5 crore went | Why it could not be stopped |
|---|---|
| Donations, asked for daily and in person | Refusing someone in front of you is far harder than refusing an idea |
| Businesses he had no experience running | Enough money to start again meant nothing ever forced a rethink |
| Ten to fifteen days a month at events | Being the KBC winner quietly replaced the plan to study |
| No rule, no adviser, nobody to point at | Every request became a fresh decision, made alone, in the moment |

*Table 1 — a fortune leaving without one dramatic mistake*

## 3. What it cost at home

This is the part the headlines skip, and the part that matters most.

His wife could see it happening. She told him — repeatedly — that he **could not tell the difference between people who genuinely needed help and people who were using him**, and that he was not thinking about their future.

They fought about it. By his account the relationship worsened steadily through this period.

Look at what that argument actually is. It is not about money. It is a woman watching her husband give away the one thing that could secure their children's lives, to strangers, while being praised for it — and being unable to make him see.

**A windfall does not only drain an account. It puts a person under a pressure that the people closest to them absorb.** He was generous in public and unreachable at home.

## 4. How the drinking started

This is not the story people assume, and it is worth reading slowly.

He was in Delhi for the car rental business. He met media students at Jamia Millia. Through them, students at IIMC. Then their seniors. Then researchers at JNU, then some theatre artists.

Every circle was interesting. Every circle drank.

> *"At one time, if I stayed for seven days, then it would have been different sittings with seven such groups for seven days."*

> *"I used to finish a cigarette packet in a day."*

Nobody sets out to become an alcoholic. He became a man with money and free time in a city where seven different groups were pleased to see him, and there was drinking at all seven.

He has called 2015–16 the most difficult period of his life, and described what he was doing in it in five words:

> *"A fugitive running away from the truth."*

The truth being that the money was going, the plan was abandoned, the marriage was strained — and that the easiest way to avoid all of it was another evening with another group.

## 5. The rumour that proved everything

Then something happened that accidentally tested every relationship he had.

A journalist called while he was out walking. The conversation irritated him, so — to end it — he said his money was finished and he was surviving by selling milk from two cows.

**He made it up.**

It ran as news. It has been reprinted as fact ever since, in hundreds of articles, and he has since called it fake news from a misunderstood post.

But the consequence was real:

> *"All those who I was surrounded with, cornered themselves. I wasn't invited to events."*

Seven drinking groups. Constant invitations. **All of it gone on the rumour of an empty bank account.**

He never had to actually lose everything to learn what those relationships were worth. He only had to be believed to have lost it.

## 6. He stopped

**He last drank alcohol in March 2016.** He gave up cigarettes the year after. Then he went home to Bihar and started preparing to become a teacher.

He did a B.Ed. He cleared CTET, then the Bihar STET. In 2023 he cleared the Bihar Public Service Commission's teacher recruitment exam, ranking **119th in Psychology** for classes 11–12. The appointment letter came in January.

He now teaches psychology in a Bihar government school and is enrolled in a PhD at BRA Bihar University.

And the prize money did leave something behind:

- Plantation drives for peepal and banyan trees
- A sparrow conservation campaign — around **5,500 wooden nests** distributed since 2019
- A coaching centre he opened in 2015 in Machhargawan for children of the Musahar community, one of Bihar's most marginalised, with free books, pens, and teachers' salaries paid

He is 40, lives in Motihari with his father, brother, wife and two young daughters, and rides a scooter.

**His salary is ₹45,440 a month.** He took that job after winning ₹5 crore — not because he had to, but because he wanted it.

> *"In KBC, I had said that I like teaching children and now I have got the job of my choice."*

## 7. What he tells people now

This is his own conclusion, and it is sharper than anything I could write:

> *"Those who show off by buying branded clothes, cars etc., to get praise and appreciation from people, believe in outer happiness. But when you derive happiness from the results of your own deeds, it makes you happy from within."*

> *"I live a simple life and am not dependent upon others' validation."*

He is not saying money does not matter. He is saying something more precise, learned the expensive way.

**The praise he was buying with those donations was the thing that cost him everything — and it was never worth anything.** Seven groups of friends proved that the week they heard he was broke.

He also put it more simply, in 2020:

> *"Happiness is hidden in small-small things."*

## What ₹5 crore could have been

Before the lessons, one piece of arithmetic — because the number itself makes the point.

Game show winnings are taxed under Section 194B at a flat **30% plus cess**. No slab, no exemption limit, no deductions. It is taken before the money reaches you. Reports put what he actually received at around **₹3.6 crore**.

*[VERIFY: current Section 194B rate and cess before publishing. It was 30% plus 4% cess at the time of writing, and the cess was 3% in 2011. Rates change.]*

Now suppose that ₹3.6 crore had simply been left in something conservative earning 7%, withdrawing 4% a year and never touching the capital:

| | |
|---|---|
| Withdraw 4% a year | ₹14,40,000 |
| Which is per month | **₹1,20,000** |
| What happens to the ₹3.6 crore | It keeps growing |

*Table 2 — illustration at an assumed 7%. Actual returns vary and past performance does not guarantee future results.*

**₹1,20,000 every month. Indefinitely. From money that is still there at the end.**

Against a salary of ₹6,000 a month, that is a life permanently transformed — for him, his wife, and both daughters — requiring exactly one good decision and then no further decisions at all.

He did not need a business. He did not need to be clever. **The amount was never the problem.**

## Five things to actually take from this

None of these require ₹5 crore.

**1. A plan is not protection. A structure is.**

He had the right plan. He lost the money anyway, because a plan lives in your head while the requests arrived in the real world, daily, in person.

What he needed was not better intentions. It was a boundary that existed outside of him — a fixed amount for giving, the rest moved somewhere that took effort to reach.

**2. Decide your sentence before you are asked.**

Not a number, a sentence, ready in advance:

*"I've committed what I can give, and it's already decided."*

The requests come faster than you can think, and each is individually reasonable. A rule made in advance does the refusing so you do not have to.

**3. If you want to give — and you should — fix the amount first.**

He was not wrong to want to help. Look at what the coaching centre and the nests actually did.

The failure was that it was **unbounded**. A decided number is generosity. An open tap is something else, and the people at the other end can tell the difference.

**4. Money to try is not knowledge to succeed.**

The businesses failing "after a few days" is the clearest warning in the story. A windfall lets you skip the stage where you learn an industry — and skipping it is exactly why the venture fails.

If you would not have started that business without the windfall, the windfall is not a reason to start it.

**5. The people who arrive with the money leave with it.**

Seven groups, constant invitations, all of it gone the week people believed he was broke.

If money brought them, money is what holds them. You do not have to lose it to find out.

## The lesson underneath all five

**Sudden money is harder than earned money.**

A salary arrives slowly enough that your judgement grows alongside it. You learn to handle ₹50,000 a month before you are asked to handle ₹1 lakh. Each decision is small, and the mistakes are survivable.

A windfall skips every bit of that. You receive the amount without the years of small decisions that teach you to carry it — which is why the person handling it is almost always underqualified, however intelligent they are.

And it applies far below ₹5 crore. **A bonus. An inheritance. Family land sold. A provident fund at retirement. An insurance settlement.** Same shape every time: one lump, no instructions, everyone aware.

## He did not lose everything

The popular version of this story ends with a man selling milk to survive. That version is built on a line he invented to get off the phone.

The true ending is quieter and much better.

He gave away too much, too fast, to too many people, tried businesses he had no business trying, and it cost him his money, his health and nearly his marriage. Then he stopped. He went back to studying, and became the teacher he had told the whole country he wanted to be — for ₹45,440 a month, by choice, raising two daughters in the town he grew up in.

He lost the money. He kept the thing he actually wanted.

Most of us will never win ₹5 crore. Almost all of us will one day receive more money at once than we are used to handling. When that day comes, the question will not be whether we are clever.

**It will be whether we decided anything in advance.**
