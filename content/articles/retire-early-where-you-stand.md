*Episode 1 of [Planning to Retire Early](/series/retire-early).*

![One lakh rupees today buys only what fifty-six thousand three hundred buys, twenty years later](/diagrams/cover-retire-early.svg)

Ask Priya how much money she has and she says, without much thought, "maybe five, six lakh."

The real number is ₹4,01,800. And the part she could actually use tomorrow, if her job ended this week, is ₹40,000 — less than one month of her spending.

She is not careless. She earns well, spends sensibly, and has never once added it all up. Almost nobody has.

That is why this series about retiring early starts somewhere unexpected. **Nobody invests a single rupee in this episode.** Most people who want to retire early begin by asking which fund to buy. That is the wrong first question, and asking it first is how plans fall apart.

Instead, five steps, about an hour in all:

1. **Find out what you actually have** — not what you think you have.
2. **See why earning more will not fix it** on its own.
3. **Test the FD** most families keep their savings in.
4. **See where else money can go**, and what each choice costs you.
5. **Put four safety nets in place**, so nothing forces you to sell an investment at the worst possible moment.

Skip these and every later step stands on sand. Do them and the rest of the series — your first SIP, your first portfolio, your retire-early number — is just arithmetic.

If those words are new, they are simpler than they sound. A **SIP** (Systematic Investment Plan) is a standing instruction to invest a fixed amount every month, automatically, usually into a mutual fund. A **portfolio** is just the full list of everything you have invested in. Both come in later episodes.

## What "retire early" actually means

The idea has a name: **FIRE**, short for *Financial Independence, Retire Early*.

Financial independence means one specific thing: **your investments pay for your life, so your salary becomes optional.** You might keep working. The point is that you no longer *have* to.

In practice that means building up one large pot of investments — big enough that you can take a little out every year, for the rest of your life, without it running dry. A later episode in this series works out how big yours needs to be.

It is not a lottery win and it is not a trick. It is a long, boring habit: spend less than you earn, invest the difference, and leave it alone for years. This series takes that habit apart into steps you can do.

## Meet the salary we will use

To keep this concrete, the whole series follows the same person.

Priya is 29, a QA engineer in Hyderabad. If you read the [Rich Dad Poor Dad article](/articles/rich-dad-poor-dad), you have met her already. Take-home **₹58,400 a month**, spending **₹52,180**, living in a 1BHK in Madhapur.

She has read the books and watched the videos. What she has never done is the first step.

## Step 1 — The money audit

**Time: 20 minutes.**

Write down every place your money is sitting today. Not what you earn — what you *have*. Every account, every deposit, every gram of gold.

Here is Priya's, filled in honestly for the first time:

| Where her money sits | Amount | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Savings account | ₹40,000 | 10% |
| EPF (from four years of salary) | ₹2,46,800 | 61% |
| Gold jewellery from family | ₹1,15,000 | 29% |
| Fixed deposits | ₹0 | 0% |
| Mutual funds, stocks, PPF | ₹0 | 0% |
| **Total** | **₹4,01,800** | **100%** |

*Table 1 — Priya's money audit*

Two words in that table, if they are new: **EPF** (Employees' Provident Fund) is the money cut from your salary every month, with a matching amount from your employer, saved for your retirement. **PPF** (Public Provident Fund) is a government savings scheme you can open yourself at a bank or post office.

Two things stand out.

First, the total is **₹4 lakh, not "five, six"**. Almost everyone guesses high, because we remember the big deposits and forget the withdrawals.

![Priya guessed five to six lakh; her real total is four lakh; the part she can use tomorrow is forty thousand](/diagrams/money-audit-gap.svg)

*Fig 1 — the guess, the real total, and the part she can actually use.*

Second, look at what she can actually use. The EPF is meant for retirement — she can take some out only for specific reasons, like buying a home or a medical emergency. The gold is family jewellery she will not sell. The money she could reach tomorrow is **₹40,000** — less than one month of her spending.

That is not a failure. It is where most people start. But you cannot plan from a number you have never seen.

## Step 2 — Why a bigger salary will not do it

The natural response to a small number is: *I'll earn more.* Work harder, get the promotion, switch jobs.

That helps. But a salary is money you *earn*. Wealth is money you *keep* — and money that sits still is quietly shrinking.

The reason is **inflation** — the steady rise in prices over time. A plate of idli that cost ₹30 some years ago costs ₹45 now. Your ₹100 note did not change. What it can buy did.

In India, prices have risen by around 6% a year over the long run. So ₹1 lakh today will not buy ₹1 lakh of things later.

The table below shows what ₹1 lakh will be able to buy in the future, written in **today's money** — that is, how much it would be worth if you spent it today. It assumes 6% inflation:

| After | Kept as cash | Kept in a savings account at 3% |
|---|---|---|
| 5 years | ₹74,700 | — |
| 10 years | ₹55,800 | ₹75,000 |
| 20 years | ₹31,200 | ₹56,300 |

*Table 2 — what ₹1 lakh can still buy, in today's money*

Read the last row slowly. Leave ₹1 lakh in a savings account for 20 years and the bank statement will say **₹1,80,600**. It will feel like growth. But it will buy only what **₹56,300** buys today.

![Bars showing one lakh shrinking to fifty-six thousand three hundred in buying power over twenty years](/diagrams/inflation-shrink.svg)

*Fig 2 — the money never left the account. It just buys less every year.*

You did nothing wrong, and you still lost almost half.

**So the first goal of investing is not to double your money. It is to stop losing it.** Everything above inflation is the actual return.

## Step 3 — The FD test

"Fine," most people say, "I'll keep it in an FD instead. FDs pay 7%."

They do. But the interest is not all yours.

Income tax in India works in bands called **slabs**: the more you earn, the higher the rate on the top part of your income — 5%, 10%, 20%, up to 30%. FD interest is added to your salary and taxed at whatever your top rate is. On top of that comes a **cess** — an extra 4% charged on the tax itself.

And the tax is due every year as the interest builds up, not just when the FD finishes. So the 7% you are quoted is not the 7% you keep.

On ₹1 lakh at 7% for one year:

| Your income tax slab | Interest | Tax | You keep | After 6% inflation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| No tax | ₹7,000 | ₹0 | 7.0% | +0.9% |
| 10% | ₹7,000 | ₹728 | 6.3% | +0.3% |
| 20% | ₹7,000 | ₹1,456 | 5.5% | −0.4% |
| 30% | ₹7,000 | ₹2,184 | 4.8% | −1.1% |

*Table 3 — the same FD, four different people*

The last column is the one that matters. It is what you gain **after** tax and **after** prices have risen — your real gain in what the money can buy. A plus sign means you are slowly getting richer. A minus sign means you are slowly getting poorer, even though the FD statement shows more money every year.

![A seven percent FD after tax and inflation: small gains at low tax slabs, losses at twenty and thirty percent](/diagrams/fd-real-return.svg)

*Fig 3 — the same 7% FD. Whether it grows your money depends on your tax slab.*

At lower slabs, an FD roughly **keeps pace** with inflation. In the 20% and 30% slabs it **falls behind** — your money grows on paper and shrinks in what it can buy.

That does not make FDs bad. They are safe, predictable, and exactly right for some jobs — your emergency fund, for one. It means an FD alone will not get you to financial independence, because standing still is not the same as moving forward.

**Toolkit:** the **FDs Real Return** tab lets you put in your own FD rate and tax slab, and shows whether it beats inflation for you.

## Step 4 — Where else money can go

If cash shrinks and FDs barely hold their ground, what is left? Broadly, five places, from steady to swinging:

| Option | What you are actually doing | The trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Savings account, FD | Lending to a bank | Safe and steady; often loses to inflation after tax |
| Government bonds | Lending to the government | Very unlikely not to be paid back; fixed, modest returns |
| Gold | Holding a metal | Long flat stretches, then sudden jumps; pays no income |
| Real estate | Owning property | Can pay rent and rise in value; large, slow to sell, costly to hold |
| Shares (equity) | Owning a slice of a business | Historically the highest long-run growth; also the biggest falls along the way |

*Table 4 — the main choices, in rough order of how much they swing*

Equity has historically grown faster than the rest over long periods — but past performance does not guarantee future results, and the same table explains why: it is also the one that can fall by half in a bad year, as Indian shares did in 2008. Nobody can tell you in advance which businesses will grow and which will shrink.

## Step 5 — Four safety nets before your first rupee

Investing before these are in place is how people end up selling at a loss. An emergency arrives, the market happens to be down, and the investment has to go — at exactly the wrong price.

So, in this order:

![Four steps in order: emergency fund, clear costly debt, health and term cover, open an account, then invest](/diagrams/safety-nets-order.svg)

*Fig 4 — the order matters. Each one protects the next.*

### 1. An emergency fund

Six months of spending, kept somewhere safe and easy to reach. Not invested for growth. Its only job is to be there.

Three common places:

- A **savings account** — simplest, but earns little.
- A **sweep-in FD** — a savings account where the bank moves extra money into an FD automatically, and back again when you need it.
- A **liquid fund** — a very low-risk mutual fund that lends money for a few weeks at a time. You can usually have your money back in a day.

For Priya: ₹52,180 × 6 = **₹3,13,080**. She has ₹40,000. This is her first target, and it comes before any SIP.

A new phone, a holiday and a wedding gift are not emergencies. A job loss, a hospital bill and an urgent trip home are.

The [Emergency fund](/learn/emergency-fund) chapter covers exactly how much and where to keep it.

### 2. Clear expensive debt

Credit card interest commonly runs above 40% a year. Personal loans are often in the teens. No investment reliably earns that — so paying off a 40% debt *is* a 40% return, with no risk at all.

Clear credit card balances and personal loans first. A low-rate home loan is a different matter and does not need to be cleared before you invest.

The [Credit cards](/learn/credit-cards) chapter explains where the free credit ends and the 40% begins.

### 3. Your own insurance

- **Health insurance in your own name.** Company cover ends the day you leave the job — and that is often exactly when you need it most. See [Health insurance](/learn/health-insurance).
- **Term life insurance**, if anyone depends on your income — parents, a spouse, children. It pays your family a large amount if you die during the policy, and nothing otherwise, which is why it is cheap. See [Term insurance](/learn/term-insurance).

Insurance is not an investment. It is what stops one bad event from undoing years of saving.

### 4. An investment account

To buy shares or ETFs you need two accounts, which are always opened together:

- A **demat account** holds your shares electronically — like a bank account, but for investments instead of cash.
- A **trading account** is what you use to buy and sell them.

You open both with a **broker** — a company licensed to do this for you. Check that it is registered with **SEBI**, the government body that regulates India's markets. Opening one takes your PAN, your Aadhaar and your bank account details, and is usually done online in a day or two.

If you only plan to invest in mutual funds through a SIP, you can also do that directly on the fund company's website or app, without a demat account.

This site does not recommend a broker. Compare charges, read the reviews, pick one and move on — it matters far less than whether you actually start.

## What Priya does next

Priya's audit took twenty minutes. What it changed:

- She now knows her real number: **₹4,01,800**, of which ₹40,000 she can actually use.
- She knows her first target: **₹3,13,080** in an emergency fund — before anything else.
- She has no credit card balance, so the expensive-debt step is already done.
- She has company health cover only, so a personal policy goes on the list.

No fund was chosen. No SIP was started. That is the point. Every step in the rest of this series stands on this one.
